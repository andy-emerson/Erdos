# Adding Rationals with Shared Greatest Common Divisors

*Draft v0.2, sections 1–6. This draft states the problem and the core idea only. Credit to earlier work, related work, costs and limits, and evidence will come in later drafts. Changes from v0.1: the idea is presented as an extension of the gcd method rather than as factoring, and costs are given in three forms throughout.*

## 1. The problem

A rational number is a quotient $a/b$ of integers with $b > 0$. Two rationals are added exactly by the identity

$$
\frac{a}{b} + \frac{c}{d} = \frac{ad + cb}{bd}.
$$

On a machine, integers are stored in words of fixed width. Larger integers are stored as sequences of words, and arithmetic on them costs more as they grow. We count the cost of arithmetic on integers of $n$ bits:
- **addition and subtraction:** $O(n)$;
- **multiplication:** $M(n)$, and $M(n, u)$ for an $n$-bit number times a $u$-bit number;
- **division with remainder:** $D(n)$, and $D(n, s)$ for an $n$-bit number by an $s$-bit one;
- **greatest common divisor:** $G(n)$.

These costs depend on the algorithm used. Each cost below is therefore given in three forms:
- **symbolically,** in terms of $M$, $D$ and $G$;
- **with schoolbook algorithms,** the simple methods;
- **with the fastest known algorithms.**

The last two are each the worst-case bound of their algorithms. Together they mark the range in which practical implementations lie.

| Operation | Schoolbook | Fastest |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $M(n)$ | $O(n^2)$ | $O(n \log n)$ |
| $M(n, u)$, $u \le n$ | $O(nu)$ | $O(n \log u)$ |
| $D(n)$ | $O(n^2)$ | $O(n \log n)$ |
| $D(n, s)$, $s \le n$ | $O(ns)$ | $O(n \log s)$ |
| $G(n)$ | $O(n^2)$ (Euclid) | $O(n \log^2 n)$ |

$O$-notation hides constant factors, and they matter here: even when $G$ and $M$ have the same order, a gcd costs several multiplications in practice.

The identity needs three multiplications and one addition:

| | Symbolic | Schoolbook | Fastest |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Identity | $3M(n) + O(n)$ | $O(n^2)$ | $O(n \log n)$ |

That is cheap per operation, but the result is larger than its operands. $bd$ has about $2n$ bits, and a sequence of $k$ additions produces denominators of about $kn$ bits, even when the true value is small. For example, $\tfrac{1}{6} + \tfrac{1}{10} = \tfrac{16}{60}$, which is $\tfrac{4}{15}$. Left alone, the integers grow without bound, every later operation pays for their size, and the computation eventually exceeds the machine.

## 2. Lowest terms and the greatest common divisor

The standard remedy is to keep every rational in **lowest terms**: $\gcd(a, b) = 1$ and $b > 0$. Every rational then has exactly one representation, and its integers are as small as its value allows.

### 2.1 The direct method

Compute the identity's result, then divide both parts by their greatest common divisor:

$$
g = \gcd(ad + cb,\ bd), \qquad \frac{a}{b} + \frac{c}{d} = \frac{(ad + cb)/g}{bd/g}.
$$

| | Symbolic | Schoolbook | Fastest |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Direct method | $3M(n) + G(2n) + 2D(2n)$ | $O(n^2)$ | $O(n \log^2 n)$ |

This is *more* work per operation than the identity alone. What the gcd buys is not speed but boundedness: it keeps the integers small, and so keeps long computations feasible. The gcd becomes the dominant cost of exact rational arithmetic, and the target of every further optimisation.

### 2.2 The refined method: splitting the gcd

The gcd in the direct method takes a sum, $ad + cb$, and a product, $bd$. A sum cannot be broken apart, but a product can: whatever the sum shares with $bd$ must come from $b$ or from $d$. That suggests splitting the work.

First find what the two denominators share, $g = \gcd(b, d)$, and write $b = g\,b'$ and $d = g\,d'$. The residuals $b'$ and $d'$ are coprime. Then

$$
\frac{a}{b} + \frac{c}{d} = \frac{t}{g\, b'\, d'}, \qquad t = a d' + c b'.
$$

The numerator $t$ shares no factor with $b'$. Indeed $t \equiv a d' \pmod{b'}$; $a$ is coprime to $b'$, since $a/b$ is in lowest terms and $b'$ divides $b$; and $d'$ is coprime to $b'$. Likewise $t$ shares no factor with $d'$. So **any cancellation must come from $g$ alone,** and a second gcd, against $g$ (usually a much smaller number), completes the reduction:

$$
h = \gcd(t, g), \qquad \frac{a}{b} + \frac{c}{d} = \frac{t/h}{b'\, d'\, (g/h)}.
$$

When $g = 1$ the denominators share nothing, the identity's result is already in lowest terms, and the second gcd is skipped. In short, **one gcd on $n$ bits and one usually small gcd replace one gcd on $2n$ bits,** and the multiplications act on the smaller residuals.

| | Symbolic | Schoolbook | Fastest |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Refined method | $G(n) + 3M(n) + G(\lvert g \rvert) + $ divisions | $O(n^2)$ | $O(n \log^2 n)$ |

The order is unchanged, but the constants fall, because the operands are smaller. The first gcd, $\gcd(b, d)$ on the full denominators, is now the largest single cost of an addition.

## 3. A shared denominator

When both operands are written over the same denominator $L$, the refined method's first gcd is $L$ itself, and the numerators simply add:

$$
\frac{m_x}{L} + \frac{m_y}{L} = \frac{m_x + m_y}{L}.
$$

| | Symbolic | Schoolbook | Fastest |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Shared denominator, reduction deferred | $O(n)$ | $O(n)$ | $O(n)$ |

That is one integer addition, with no multiplication and no gcd. What remains is the refined method's second gcd, $\gcd(m_x + m_y, L)$, now on the full $L$. Rather than pay it at every addition, a whole collection of values over one $L$ is kept in lowest terms *as a whole*, and reduced only occasionally, for example at the end of a computation, by $\gcd(L, m_1, \ldots, m_N)$. Postponing reduction in this way is called **lazy reduction**. Its cost is spread over every operation performed in between:

| | Symbolic | Schoolbook | Fastest |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| One lazy reduction of $N$ values | $N\,G(n) + (N + 1)\,D(n)$ | $O(N n^2)$ | $O(N n \log^2 n)$ |

This calls for a different storage. The collection is held in a **common-denominator representation**: $L$ once, and an array of integer numerators. Rationals $a_i / b_i$ are brought into this form by taking $L = \text{lcm}(b_1, \ldots, b_N)$ and $m_i = a_i \cdot L / b_i$.

The method works when the data share a scale, such as amounts in cents over $100$. It fails when denominators are unrelated, because their lcm grows quickly: the lcm of $1, 2, \ldots, N$ grows like $e^N$. Every numerator is then scaled up to that size, and arithmetic on small numbers becomes arithmetic on enormous ones. The method is all or nothing: either the values share one denominator, or we are back to Section 2.

## 4. Sharing the gcds ahead of time

Sections 2 and 3 are two ends of one idea. The refined method computes what two denominators share, $\gcd(b, d)$, at the moment they meet. The shared-denominator method knows it in advance, because it is everything. Most real data lies in between: denominators share some factors without being equal, as $12$, $18$ and $30$ do.

The idea of this work is to **do the refined method's first gcd ahead of time, for the whole collection at once.** Instead of computing $\gcd(b, d)$ whenever two values meet, we keep every denominator broken into the parts that denominators share. Then $\gcd(b, d)$ for any pair is simply the product of the parts the two have in common, already in hand.
- On the arithmetic side, this is the refined gcd method.
- On the storage side, it is a generalisation of the common-denominator representation: each value shares with every other value exactly the part of its denominator they have in common.

### 4.1 A coprime base from gcds and residuals

Start from the denominators. Take two of them, $b$ and $d$, compute $g = \gcd(b, d)$, and replace $b$ and $d$ by $g$, $b/g$ and $d/g$, dropping any 1s and merging duplicates. Repeat on any two numbers in the set that still share a factor, until no two do. The result is a set of pairwise coprime integers greater than 1, $p = (p_1, \ldots, p_k)$, called a **coprime base**, and every denominator is a product of powers of its elements. Only gcds and exact divisions are used, never factoring into primes. Replacing numbers by finer pairwise coprime parts in this way is called **factor refinement**.

**Example.** For $12$ and $18$: $\gcd(12, 18) = 6$, with residuals $2$ and $3$, so the set is $\{6, 2, 3\}$. Then $6$ and $2$ share $2$; replacing them by $2$, $3$ and $1$ (dropped) and merging duplicates leaves $\{2, 3\}$. The base is $(2, 3)$, with $12 = 2^2 \cdot 3$ and $18 = 2 \cdot 3^2$.

### 4.2 Denominators as exponent vectors

Over a coprime base, each denominator is described by an **exponent vector** $\alpha \in \mathbb{N}^k$. In multi-index notation,

$$
p^{\alpha} = p_1^{\alpha_1} p_2^{\alpha_2} \cdots p_k^{\alpha_k}.
$$

Because the base is pairwise coprime, this description is unique, and the gcd and lcm of denominators become operations on vectors. Write $\alpha \vee \beta$ and $\alpha \wedge \beta$ for the componentwise maximum and minimum:

$$
\gcd(p^{\alpha}, p^{\beta}) = p^{\alpha \wedge \beta}, \qquad \text{lcm}(p^{\alpha}, p^{\beta}) = p^{\alpha \vee \beta}, \qquad p^{\alpha}\, p^{\beta} = p^{\alpha + \beta}.
$$

So if two denominators have exponent vectors $\alpha$ and $\beta$, the refined method's $g$ is $p^{\alpha \wedge \beta}$, and its residuals are $p^{\alpha - (\alpha \wedge \beta)}$ and $p^{\beta - (\alpha \wedge \beta)}$. All of these are read off the vectors, not computed.

### 4.3 Addition

Let $x = m_x / p^{\alpha}$ and $y = m_y / p^{\beta}$, each in lowest terms. Then

$$
x + y = \frac{m_x\, p^{(\alpha \vee \beta) - \alpha} + m_y\, p^{(\alpha \vee \beta) - \beta}}{p^{\alpha \vee \beta}}.
$$

The arithmetic:
- **The first gcd is gone.** $\gcd(p^{\alpha}, p^{\beta})$ and the residuals are read off the vectors, at a cost proportional to the number of base elements the two values use, typically a handful.
- **Two multiplications by cofactors** $p^{(\alpha \vee \beta) - \alpha}$ and $p^{(\alpha \vee \beta) - \beta}$. These are the residuals: the parts of each denominator the other lacks. Write $u$ for the larger of their sizes in bits; the more the denominators share, the smaller $u$. The cofactors are products of base elements, and can be kept from one operation to the next.
- **One integer addition.**
- **The denominator is never multiplied out:** it is the vector $\alpha \vee \beta$.
- **The second gcd shrinks.** In the refined method, cancellation can only come from $g$. Here it can only come from base elements shared at *equal* power, those with $\alpha_i = \beta_i$.

  To see why, suppose $\alpha_i > \beta_i$. Then $p_i$ divides the second term of the numerator, since its cofactor contains $p_i^{\alpha_i - \beta_i}$. And $p_i$ is coprime to the first term: to $m_x$, because $x$ is in lowest terms and $p_i$ divides $p^{\alpha}$; and to its cofactor, because the base is coprime. So $\gcd(\text{sum}, p_i) = \gcd(\text{first term}, p_i) = 1$: neither $p_i$ nor any divisor of it can cancel.

  The reduction therefore works only against $\sigma = \prod_{\alpha_i = \beta_i} p_i^{\alpha_i}$, the part shared at equal power. Write $s$ for its size in bits. One remainder of the numerator modulo $\sigma$, then one gcd on $s$ bits, finds what cancels. If what cancels divides a base element only partly, that element is refined (Section 5.3).

| | Symbolic | Schoolbook | Fastest |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Shared gcds | $2M(n, u) + O(n) + D(n, s) + G(s)$ | $O(nu + ns)$ | $O(n \log n + s \log^2 s)$ |

Compared with the refined method, the gcd on full denominators disappears. What remains is the reduction against the equal-power shared part, and it shrinks with $s$.
- **Fastest model:** the order falls from $O(n \log^2 n)$ to $O(n \log n)$ whenever $s$ is small. With lazy reduction, the remaining term is deferred altogether.
- **Schoolbook model:** the cost is $O(nu + ns)$. It falls from $O(n^2)$ towards $O(n)$ when the denominators share most of their factors at *different* powers (small $u$ and small $s$), or when the equal-power reduction is made lazy.

**Example.** Over the base $(2, 3)$, $12$ has vector $(2, 1)$ and $18$ has $(1, 2)$. So $\alpha \vee \beta = (2, 2)$, the common denominator is $36$, and the cofactors are $3$ and $2$:

$$
\frac{1}{12} + \frac{5}{18} = \frac{1 \cdot 3 + 5 \cdot 2}{36} = \frac{13}{36}.
$$

No component is equal in the two vectors, so $\sigma = 1$, nothing can cancel, and no reduction is done: the result is already in lowest terms.

### 4.4 The spectrum

The cost now depends on how much the operands share:

| Sharing | What happens | Symbolic | Schoolbook | Fastest |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Everything ($\alpha = \beta$) | cofactors are 1; the sum is $(m_x + m_y)/p^{\alpha}$, the shared-denominator case | $O(n)$ with lazy reduction | $O(n)$ | $O(n)$ |
| Nothing ($\alpha \wedge \beta = 0$) | cofactors are the whole other denominator; the result is already in lowest terms, with no gcd and no reduction | $2M(n) + O(n)$ | $O(n^2)$ | $O(n \log n)$ |
| In between | cost falls as the shared part grows | $2M(n, u) + O(n) + D(n, s) + G(s)$ | $O(nu + ns)$ | $O(n \log n + s \log^2 s)$ |

At the "nothing shared" end, the cofactors are the whole other denominator as an integer. If those integers are kept with the values, the cost is $2M(n)$ and below the identity's $3M(n)$, because the denominator itself is never multiplied out. If they have to be built from the base elements, add about $M(n)$ more. Either way no gcd is needed to know that nothing cancels.

The all-or-nothing choice of Section 3 becomes a matter of degree.

### 4.5 Multiplication

In lowest-terms arithmetic, a product is reduced by two gcds: each numerator against the *other* operand's denominator, $\gcd(a, d)$ and $\gcd(c, b)$, since nothing else can cancel. With shared gcds the denominators are exponent vectors, and those cancellations are found by reducing each numerator against the base elements, at their powers, of the other operand's denominator:

$$
x \cdot y = \frac{m_x\, m_y}{p^{\alpha + \beta}}, \quad \text{after cancelling } m_x \text{ against } p^{\beta} \text{ and } m_y \text{ against } p^{\alpha}.
$$

As with addition, a cancellation that divides a base element only partly refines that element (Section 5.3).

| | Symbolic | Schoolbook | Fastest |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Lowest terms (two cross gcds) | $2G(n) + 2M(n) + $ divisions | $O(n^2)$ | $O(n \log^2 n)$ |
| Shared gcds | $M(n) + 2D(n) + 2G(\cdot) + O(1)$ per base element | $O(n^2)$ | $O(n \log^2 n)$; less when the base elements are small |

The denominator is a vector sum, $\alpha + \beta$, and is never multiplied out. For multiplication the gain is mainly in constant factors: the cancellations are found against known, usually small, base elements rather than by gcds on whole denominators.

## 5. Storing the shared gcds

Section 4 assumes that, when two values meet, their exponent vectors over a common coprime base are at hand. This section describes a storage that keeps them.

### 5.1 Why the base must be coprime

The rules of Section 4.2 hold only if the base elements are pairwise coprime. Over a base that is not coprime, both fail:
- **Uniqueness fails.** Over $(2, 4)$, the denominator $16$ is $2^4$, $4^2$ and $2^2 \cdot 4$, so it has no unique exponent vector.
- **The gcd and lcm rules fail.** Over $(4, 6)$, $4$ and $6$ have vectors $(1, 0)$ and $(0, 1)$, whose maximum $(1, 1)$ means $24$, while $\text{lcm}(4, 6) = 12$.

Factor refinement (Section 4.1) restores coprimality whenever two elements are found to share something.

### 5.2 The structure

The stored values and the base together form a weighted bipartite graph:
- **on one side, the values,** each identified by its integer numerator;
- **on the other, the base elements;**
- **an edge** from each value to each base element in its denominator, weighted by the exponent.

Equivalently, the denominators form a sparse matrix: one row per value, one column per base element, with the exponents as entries. Row $i$ is the exponent vector of value $i$, and alongside the matrix is a vector of numerators. Addition and multiplication of two values read two rows and the base elements in their columns, nothing else.

### 5.3 Building it incrementally

When a new denominator $d$ arrives, it is refined against the base:
- **The part of $d$ that shares nothing with the base** becomes new base elements.
- **When $d$ shares part of an existing element $q$,** that is, when $\gcd(d, q)$ is neither 1 nor $q$, the element $q$ is refined into $\gcd(d, q)$ and its residual, and the process repeats until the base is pairwise coprime again.
- **Every value that used a refined element** receives the corresponding exponents on its parts, so its denominator is unchanged. For example, when $15$ is refined into $3$ and $5$, an exponent $w$ on $15$ becomes $w$ on $3$ and $w$ on $5$.

The cost of building and maintaining the base is paid when denominators first appear, not when values are added. It is a separate question from the arithmetic, and is left to later sections. An arithmetic operation touches only its two operands and the base elements they use, so its cost does not depend on how many values are stored.

## 6. One method across the whole spectrum

The methods compared, for adding two $n$-bit values. Here $u$ is the size of the parts of the denominators that are not shared, and $s$ is the size of the part shared at equal power (Section 4.3).

| Method | Common denominator from | Symbolic | Schoolbook | Fastest |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Identity (§1) | multiplication; the size grows | $3M(n) + O(n)$ | $O(n^2)$ | $O(n \log n)$ |
| Direct gcd (§2.1) | multiplication, then a gcd on $2n$ bits | $3M(n) + G(2n) + 2D(2n)$ | $O(n^2)$ | $O(n \log^2 n)$ |
| Refined gcd (§2.2) | $\gcd(b, d)$ on $n$ bits, then a gcd on $\lvert g \rvert$ bits | $G(n) + 3M(n) + G(\lvert g \rvert) + $ divisions | $O(n^2)$ | $O(n \log^2 n)$ |
| Shared denominator (§3) | known in advance; reduction lazy | $O(n)$ | $O(n)$ | $O(n)$ |
| Shared gcds (§4) | read off exponent vectors; reduction only against the equal-power shared part | $2M(n, u) + O(n) + D(n, s) + G(s)$ | $O(nu + ns)$ | $O(n \log n + s \log^2 s)$ |

The shared-gcd method contains the others as special cases:
- **Values with identical exponent vectors** share one denominator exactly. They can be stored together in the common-denominator representation, as a block of numerators over one row of exponents, and added with plain integer arithmetic and lazy reduction. Nothing is lost on data that share one scale.
- **Values whose vectors share nothing** add by two multiplications and an addition, with the result already in lowest terms. Nothing is lost on unrelated denominators either.
- **Everything in between,** which neither the gcd methods nor the shared denominator serves well, costs in proportion to what the denominators do *not* share, plus a reduction limited to what they share at equal power.

What makes this possible is a change of storage: each denominator is kept not as an integer but as an exponent vector over a coprime base shared by all values, built and refined by gcds as the data require. The refined method's first gcd, which every other approach pays at every addition, is then paid once, when a denominator first appears.
