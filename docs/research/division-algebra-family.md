# A family over the division algebras: research findings

Oct 26, 2026 · Andrew Emerson

This note collects the research done before drafting a new specification that grows out of [Q v0.3](../spec/q-v0.3.md). The proposal under study:

- **One family, four algebras.** Every value is a pair: a one-byte header and one unsigned word. The header holds a signed unit of a division algebra and the slash. The algebra's size sets how many unit bits are needed, and the word's size sets how many slash bits, so that the two fill a byte:

| Algebra | Unit bits | Slash bits | Word | Size |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| reals | 1 (sign) | 7 | U128 | 136 bits |
| complex numbers | 2 (sign, imaginary) | 6 | U64 | 72 bits |
| quaternions | 3 (sign, axis i, j or k) | 5 | U32 | 40 bits |
| octonions | 4 (sign, seven imaginary axes) | 4 | U16 | 24 bits |

  These are exactly the four normed division algebras (Hurwitz, 1898). The pattern cannot usefully go further: the next Cayley–Dickson algebra, the sedenions, has zero divisors.
- **Promotion.** A value that does not fit moves to a word twice as wide, keeping its one-byte header. The missing slash bits are borrowed from the top of the word, so that, read as one bit string, a value is: unit, header slash bits, borrowed slash bits, numerator, denominator minus one.
- **Arrays.** Arrays use the vector form: integers over one shared denominator per vector, one vector per axis. With fixed widths of any power of two ("uN"), the whole vector widens to the narrowest width its values provably need.

Three questions were open: whether fixed-width tiers beat GMP and FLINT where the v0.3 prototype lost; whether quaternion arrays keep the fast path; and what prior art covers. The design of the header was tested alongside.

The code is in [`bench/q-research`](../../bench/q-research/) (`family.py`, `cbench/un.c`, `cbench/quat.c`, `family_bench.py`); results are in `bench/q-research/results/family.md`. Every result was checked against Python's exact fractions. An independent review of the code, its soundness and the fairness of the comparisons came before the measured run, and its findings were fixed. One of those findings was a real overflow: the quaternion vector form could wrap with components near $2^{63}$. It now refuses such inputs instead.

## Prior art

- **Exact complex rationals** are old. Common Lisp has had complex numbers with rational parts as a built-in exact type since the 1980s.
- **Exact quaternions** exist:
  - SageMath stores a rational quaternion as four integers over one shared denominator, $(x + yi + zj + wk)/d$.
  - Boost, Eigen and Julia quaternion types are exact when built over a rational scalar type.
  - A Python package handles Hurwitz integer quaternions.
- **Octonions** appear in Cayley–Dickson libraries (Julia, JavaScript, Perl, Python, Rust), mostly in floating point.
- **Fixed-width integers of any size** are a standard language feature: C23's `_BitInt(N)`, which clang supports up to 8,388,608 bits. 256-bit integers are routine in blockchain software, and FLINT 3.4's `nfixed` provides fixed-width multi-limb vectors for matrix code.
- **Nothing found** combines a per-value tag naming one signed unit of a division algebra with the slash of a floating-slash rational in one byte, or sizes a family across the four division algebras that way, or pairs it with a shared-denominator vector form. Exact quaternions with a shared denominator per value (Sage) and fixed-width integers (C23) are the closest pieces.

## The unit tables and the header

The signed units of each algebra were generated by the Cayley–Dickson construction, $(a, b)(c, d) = (ac - d^* b,\ da + b c^*)$, applied to basis elements. Tests confirm:
- every table is closed;
- each table is the restriction of the next, so a complex unit is the same unit in the quaternions and octonions;
- the construction reproduces Hamilton's quaternion table exactly ($ij = k$, $ji = -k$);
- the tables are associative up to the quaternions and not for the octonions;
- octonions are alternative, $x(xy) = (xx)y$, on general elements, while the sedenions are not.

So one rule, a sign bit and an axis index with products from one recursive table, covers all four algebras.

The header layout with borrowed slash bits was checked in two ways:
- exhaustively, on small words with a 4-bit header, where every pattern decodes and canonical encodings are unique and round-trip;
- by random round trips at full width for every member and two promotions of each.

## Fixed-width tiers in arrays (uN)

Vector arithmetic was compiled with `_BitInt(W)` for $W$ = 64, 128, 256, 512 and 1024. The whole vector widens to the narrowest width proven by one scan of bit lengths, and that scan is inside the timed region. It is compared with GMP and FLINT integers over the same shared denominator, which do the same work without reducing, and with GMP rationals, which reduce every step. All are compiled C, on $10^5$ 3-place decimals.

| Workload | uN width | GMP `mpz_t` / uN | FLINT `fmpz` / uN | GMP `mpq_t` / uN |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $(ab)^2$, no wide values | 128 | 7.5× | 10× | 86× |
| sum of $(ab)^2$, no wide values | 128 | 8.3× | 12× | 170× |
| $(ab)^2$, 1% of values near $10^{16}$ | 256 | 2.2× | 3.1× | 25× |
| $(ab)^2$, all values near $10^{16}$ | 256 | 4.0× | 4.9× | 22× |
| growth chain $x \leftarrow xb + c$, 3 steps | 128 | 9.6× | 6.2× | 95× |
| 10 steps | 256 | 5.6× | 7.4× | 53× |
| 25 steps | 512 | 2.4× | 3.9× | 21× |
| 40 steps | 1024 | 1.5× | 2.3× | 12× |
| 70 steps | 1024 | 1.0× | 1.3× | 9× |
| 90 steps | over 1024 | — | — | — |

Times are relative to uN; above 1 means uN is faster. At 90 steps the uN method detected that 1,024 bits would not suffice, before overflowing, and stopped; in a full implementation it would continue in arbitrary precision.

**Findings.**
- **Fixed widths remove the v0.3 losses.** In v0.3, past 128 bits the prototype fell to Python integers and lost to GMP rationals and FLINT integers. Up to 512 bits, compiled fixed widths are 2× to 12× faster than GMP and FLINT integers over the same denominator. At 1,024 bits they are 1.0× to 2.3× faster. Against GMP rationals they are 9× to 170× faster.
- **The advantage narrows as the width grows.** Fixed-width arithmetic on 1024-bit words costs the same for every value, while GMP pays only for the bits each value uses. At about 1,000 bits, after 70 steps, the two tie.
- **So a cap around 1,024 bits is a measured choice, not an arbitrary one.** Beyond it, fixed widths stop paying, and arbitrary precision, GMP's territory, is no slower.
- **Widening the whole vector for a few wide values costs about 3.5×** (128 µs to 448 µs when 1% of values are wide), and uN still beats both libraries. Per-element promotion, as FLINT does, would avoid that cost but give up the dense layout.

## Quaternion arrays

The quaternion vector form is four integer vectors over one shared denominator, with the Hamilton product written out with constant signs. Every baseline also has its Hamilton product written out by hand. They are GMP and FLINT rational 4-tuples, FLINT integers over one denominator per value (SageMath's layout), and checked int64 4-tuples. All compiled; $2 \times 10^5$ quaternions with 3-place components below 1000, and $10^5$ with 6-place components below $10^6$, whose products need 128 bits.

| Operation | FLINT `fmpz`, one denominator per value / vector form | GMP `mpq_t` 4-tuples / vector form | checked int64 / vector form |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $p + q$, 3 places | 1.4× | 83× | 0.60× |
| Hamilton product $pq$, 3 places | 20× | 440× | 1.1× |
| sum of squared norms, 3 places | 4.9× | 210× | 1.6× |
| rotation $q v q^*$, 3 places | 8.2× | 250× | 0.71× |
| $p + q$, 6 places | 1.4× | 87× | 0.62× |
| Hamilton product, 6 places | 26× | 420× | overflows |
| sum of squared norms, 6 places | 10× | 190× | overflows |
| rotation, 6 places | 23× | 330× | overflows |

**Findings.**
- **Quaternion arrays keep the fast path.** The vector form is 1.4× (addition) to 26× (Hamilton products) faster than the closest exact layout, FLINT integers over a shared denominator, as SageMath stores quaternions. It is 80× to 440× faster than rational 4-tuples.
- **It runs at checked machine-integer speed.** Checked int64 is between 1.6× slower and 1.7× faster where it does not overflow. Where it does, the vector form widens to 128 bits and continues.
- **What the format contributes**, as the review pointed out, is the shared denominator (no gcd) and one bound check per operation in place of a check per arithmetic step. Writing the product out by hand is not an advantage of the format, which is why every baseline here has it too.

## Precision of each member

Rounding is not part of the formats; it is measured only to describe how finely each member covers the reals.

| Member | Size | Data bits | Median relative error | Worst (beside 1) | Float significand to match: median / worst |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| reals, U128 | 136 | 128 | $3.4 \times 10^{-39}$ | $2.7 \times 10^{-20}$ | 127 / 65 |
| complex, U64 | 72 | 64 | $7.3 \times 10^{-20}$ | $1.2 \times 10^{-10}$ | 62 / 33 |
| quaternions, U32 | 40 | 32 | $2.6 \times 10^{-10}$ | $7.6 \times 10^{-6}$ | 31 / 17 |
| octonions, U16 | 24 | 16 | $1.8 \times 10^{-5}$ | $2.0 \times 10^{-3}$ | 15 / 9 |
| quaternions promoted once, U64 | 72 | 63 | $1.3 \times 10^{-19}$ | $2.3 \times 10^{-10}$ | 62 / 32 |
| quaternions promoted twice, U128 | 136 | 126 | $1.6 \times 10^{-38}$ | $5.4 \times 10^{-20}$ | 125 / 64 |

Each borrowed slash bit costs one data bit, so a promoted quaternion is one bit short of the complex member of the same size. The canonical quaternion member is about as precise as a 32-bit float typically, and much less precise in the worst case. The octonion member holds small exact constants rather than computed values.

## Design questions for discussion

1. **Bit order of borrowed slash bits.** Placing them at the top of the word, as tested, keeps the whole value one contiguous bit string in the same field order as the canonical members. Borrowing from the bottom is equally cheap but breaks that.
2. **One header form for every algebra (the octonion layout)?** It would mean one decoder and one unit table, with values moving between algebras without re-encoding. The cost is unused unit bits: 3 for reals, 2 for complex, 1 for quaternions, which is 3, 2 and 1 bits of precision.
3. **The cap.** uN stops paying near 1,024 bits, so a cap there is supported by measurement. Should the spec fix 1,024, or define uN parametrically and recommend 1,024?
4. **Widening rule in arrays.** Whole-vector widening keeps arrays dense but costs about 3.5× when a few values are wide. Is per-element promotion worth specifying as an option?
5. **Octonions.** Specify the U16 member, or record it as the end of the pattern? At 16 data bits it holds small constants, not computed values.
6. **The unit type on its own.** The unit bits alone are each algebra's signed units, a useful type for direction without magnitude. It needs a name: mathematicians call the quaternion units "Q8", which clashes with the family's naming.
7. **Naming the family**, now that it is no longer only the Q family of v0.3.
