# A family over the division algebras: research findings

Oct 26, 2026 · Andrew Emerson

This note collects the research done before drafting a new specification that grows out of [Q v0.3](../../03-q/v0.3.md). The proposal under study:

- **One family, four algebras.** Every value is a pair: a one-byte header and one unsigned word. The header holds a signed unit of a division algebra and the slash. The algebra's size sets how many unit bits are needed, and the word's size sets how many slash bits, so that the two fill a byte:

| Algebra | Unit bits | Slash bits | Word | Size |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| reals | 1 (sign) | 7 | U128 | 136 bits |
| complex numbers | 2 (sign, imaginary) | 6 | U64 | 72 bits |
| quaternions | 3 (sign, axis i, j or k) | 5 | U32 | 40 bits |
| octonions | 4 (sign, seven imaginary axes) | 4 | U16 | 24 bits |

  These are exactly the four normed division algebras (Hurwitz, 1898). The pattern cannot usefully go further: the next Cayley–Dickson algebra, the sedenions, has zero divisors.
- **Promotion.** A value that does not fit moves to a word twice as wide, keeping its one-byte header. The missing slash bits are borrowed from the top of the word, so that, read as one bit string, a value is: unit, header slash bits, borrowed slash bits, numerator, denominator minus one.
- **Arrays.** Arrays use the vector form: integers over one shared denominator per vector, one vector per axis. With fixed widths of any power of two ("uN"), the whole vector widens to the narrowest width its values provably need.

The research covered:
- **Fixed-width tiers:** whether they beat GMP and FLINT where the v0.3 prototype lost, now up to 2,048 bits.
- **Arrays:** whether quaternion and octonion arrays keep the fast path.
- **A uniform header:** the octonion layout used for every algebra.
- **Exact matrix products:** whether wide entries can be sped up with floating-point BLAS.
- **Prior art.**

The code is in [`test`](../test/) (`family.py`, `uniform.py`, `cbench/un.c`, `cbench/quat.c`, `cbench/oct.c`, `cbench/gemm.c`, `family_bench.py`); results are in [`test/results/family.md`](../test/results/family.md). Prior art is in [`prior-art-family.md`](prior-art-family.md). Every result was checked against Python's exact fractions or, for large matrix products, against FLINT and a random-vector (Freivalds) check, and the checks themselves were tested by corrupting a result on purpose. Two independent reviews of the code, its soundness and the fairness of the comparisons came before the measured runs, and their findings were fixed. Two of those findings are worth recording:
- **A real overflow:** the quaternion vector form could wrap with components near $2^{63}$. It now refuses such inputs instead.
- **An unfair baseline:** the system's FLINT was built without BLAS. FLINT was rebuilt with BLAS for the matrix comparison.

## Prior art

- **Exact complex rationals** are old. Common Lisp has had complex numbers with rational parts as a built-in exact type since the 1980s.
- **Exact quaternions** exist:
  - SageMath stores a rational quaternion as four integers over one shared denominator, $(x + yi + zj + wk)/d$.
  - Boost, Eigen and Julia quaternion types are exact when built over a rational scalar type.
  - A Python package handles Hurwitz integer quaternions.
- **Octonions** appear in Cayley–Dickson libraries (Julia, JavaScript, Perl, Python, Rust), mostly in floating point.
- **Fixed-width integers of any size** are a standard language feature: C23's `_BitInt(N)`, which clang supports up to 8,388,608 bits. 256-bit integers are routine in blockchain software, and FLINT 3.4's `nfixed` provides fixed-width multi-limb vectors for matrix code.
- **Exact matrix products through floating point** are established. FLINT's `fmpz_mat_mul_blas` and the Ozaki scheme II compute products modulo many primes with floating-point matrix multiplication and rebuild the result with the Chinese remainder theorem.
- **Nothing found** combines:
  - a per-value tag naming one signed unit of a division algebra with the slash of a floating-slash rational in one byte;
  - a family sized across the four division algebras that way;
  - pairing it with a shared-denominator vector form.

  The closest pieces are exact quaternions with a shared denominator per value (Sage) and fixed-width integers (C23).

## The unit tables and the header

The signed units of each algebra were generated by the Cayley–Dickson construction, $(a, b)(c, d) = (ac - d^* b,\ da + b c^*)$, applied to basis elements. Tests confirm:
- every table is closed;
- each table is the restriction of the next, so a complex unit is the same unit in the quaternions and octonions;
- the construction reproduces Hamilton's quaternion table exactly ($ij = k$, $ji = -k$);
- the tables are associative up to the quaternions and not for the octonions;
- octonions are alternative, $x(xy) = (xx)y$, on general elements, while the sedenions are not;
- the octonion product of general elements satisfies $|pq| = |p||q|$.

So one rule, a sign bit and an axis index with products from one recursive table, covers all four algebras.

The header layout with borrowed slash bits was checked in two ways:
- exhaustively, on small words with a 4-bit header, where every pattern decodes and canonical encodings are unique and round-trip;
- by random round trips at full width for every member and two promotions of each.

## Fixed-width tiers in arrays (uN), up to 2,048 bits

The vector arithmetic was compiled two ways, for widths of 64 to 2,048 bits. The whole vector widens to the narrowest width proven by one scan of bit lengths, and that scan is inside the timed region.
- **`_BitInt(W)`:** C23's fixed-width integers, the simplest implementation.
- **Fixed limb arrays:** each value is a sign and $W/64$ 64-bit words in one contiguous array, with arithmetic by GMP's low-level `mpn` routines. A one-word operand uses a one-word multiply.

The contenders are GMP and FLINT integers over the same shared denominator, which do the same work without reducing, and GMP rationals, which reduce every step. All are compiled C, on $10^5$ 3-place decimals. Times are relative: above 1 means the uN vector is faster.

| Workload | Width | GMP `mpz_t` / `_BitInt` | GMP `mpz_t` / limb arrays | FLINT `fmpz` / faster uN | GMP `mpq_t` / faster uN |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $(ab)^2$, no wide values | 128 | 5.1× | 1.2× | 7.3× | 61× |
| sum of $(ab)^2$, no wide values | 128 | 8.8× | 1.9× | 12× | 170× |
| $(ab)^2$, 1% of values near $10^{16}$ | 256 | 2.8× | 1.3× | 2.7× | 22× |
| $(ab)^2$, all values near $10^{16}$ | 256 | 2.9× | 1.4× | 4.8× | 17× |
| growth chain $x \leftarrow xb + c$, 3 steps | 128 | 10× | 1.3× | 6.6× | 102× |
| 10 steps | 256 | 5.8× | 1.3× | 7.6× | 55× |
| 25 steps | 512 | 2.3× | 1.2× | 3.6× | 21× |
| 40 steps | 1024 | 1.4× | 1.2× | 2.1× | 13× |
| 70 steps | 1024 | 0.96× | 1.2× | 1.8× | 12× |
| 100 steps | 2048 | 0.63× | 0.96× | 1.4× | 7.6× |
| 140 steps | 2048 | 0.60× | 1.1× | 1.3× | 7.3× |
| 200 steps | over 2048 | — | — | — | — |

At 200 steps every uN method detected that 2,048 bits would not suffice, before overflowing, and stopped. A full implementation would continue in arbitrary precision.

A second question was whether building the wide tiers on demand costs time. Nothing is built at run time; every width is compiled ahead. What happens on demand is widening: copying the vector to the next width as values grow. So each chain was also run on a ready-made 2,048-bit vector that never widens, in three forms: `_BitInt(2048)`, limb arrays, and limbs with the length fixed at compile time and hand-written loops.

| Chain | Fastest uN that widens as needed | Fastest ready-made 2,048-bit vector |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 3 steps | 0.55 ms | 79 ms |
| 25 steps | 26 ms | 598 ms |
| 70 steps | 259 ms | 1,418 ms |
| 140 steps | 1,094 ms | 2,273 ms |

**Findings.**
- **Up to 1,024 bits, fixed widths win.** `_BitInt` is 1.4× to 10× faster than GMP integers over the same denominator, and limb arrays are 1.2× to 1.9× faster at every width. Against GMP rationals the uN vector is 7× to 170× faster throughout.
- **At 2,048 bits it levels off rather than continuing to fall,** given a careful implementation. Limb arrays stay at parity with GMP (0.96× to 1.1×). `_BitInt` falls to 0.6×, because it multiplies the full width even when one operand is a single word.
- **Widening on demand is not the cost.** A ready-made 2,048-bit vector is 2× to 140× slower than one that widens as needed, because it pays for all 2,048 bits from the first step. The decline at large widths comes from fixed widths paying for every bit, used or not, while GMP pays only for the bits each value has.
- **So a cap near 1,024 bits is supported by measurement.** Beyond it, element-by-element work is GMP's territory.
- **Widening the whole vector for a few wide values costs about 2.7×.** Even then uN beats both libraries. Per-element promotion, as FLINT does, would avoid that cost but give up the dense layout.

## Quaternion arrays

The quaternion vector form is four integer vectors over one shared denominator, with the Hamilton product written out with constant signs. Every baseline also has its Hamilton product written out by hand. They are GMP rational 4-tuples, FLINT integers over one denominator per value (SageMath's layout), and checked int64 4-tuples. All are compiled; the inputs are $2 \times 10^5$ quaternions with 3-place components below 1000, and $10^5$ with 6-place components below $10^6$, whose products need 128 bits.

| Operation | FLINT `fmpz`, one denominator per value / vector form | GMP `mpq_t` 4-tuples / vector form | checked int64 / vector form |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $p + q$, 3 places | 1.4× | 76× | 0.53× |
| Hamilton product $pq$, 3 places | 14× | 448× | 1.1× |
| sum of squared norms, 3 places | 4.2× | 175× | 1.35× |
| rotation $q v q^*$, 3 places | 9.5× | 272× | 1.1× |
| $p + q$, 6 places | 2.0× | 89× | 0.60× |
| Hamilton product, 6 places | 26× | 408× | overflows |
| sum of squared norms, 6 places | 8.9× | 166× | overflows |
| rotation, 6 places | 23× | 330× | overflows |

## Octonion arrays

The octonion vector form is eight integer vectors over one shared denominator. Its product has 64 terms, written out from the Cayley–Dickson table by a generator (`gen_oct_table.py`) that every implementation uses: the vector form, GMP rational 8-tuples, FLINT integers over one denominator per value, and checked int64. $10^5$ octonions with 3-place components below 1000, and $5 \times 10^4$ with 6-place components below $10^6$.

| Operation | FLINT `fmpz`, one denominator per value / vector form | GMP `mpq_t` 8-tuples / vector form | checked int64 / vector form |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $p + q$, 3 places | 2.6× | 80× | 0.61× |
| octonion product $pq$, 3 places | 13× | 521× | 1.4× |
| sum of squared norms, 3 places | 4.9× | 200× | 1.1× |
| $p + q$, 6 places | 1.4× | 89× | 0.64× |
| octonion product, 6 places | 33× | 511× | overflows |
| sum of squared norms, 6 places | 11× | 196× | overflows |

**Findings, quaternions and octonions.**
- **Both keep the fast path.** The vector form is:
  - 1.4× (addition) to 33× (octonion products) faster than the closest exact layout, FLINT integers over a shared denominator;
  - 76× to 520× faster than rational tuples.

  The advantage grows with the algebra, because a larger product has more terms that each avoid a library call.
- **They run at checked machine-integer speed.** Checked int64 is up to 1.9× faster on addition, where the vector form spends most of its time on its two bit-length scans. On products and norms it is 1.1× to 1.4× slower. Where it overflows, the vector form widens to 128 bits and continues.
- **What the format contributes**, as the review pointed out, is the shared denominator (no gcd) and one bound check per operation in place of a check per arithmetic step. Writing the product out is not an advantage of the format, which is why every baseline here has it too.

## Precision of each member

Rounding is not part of the formats; it is measured only to describe how finely each member covers the reals.

| Member | Size | Data bits | Median relative error | Worst (beside 1) | Float significand to match: median / worst |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| reals, U128 | 136 | 128 | $3.4 \times 10^{-39}$ | $2.7 \times 10^{-20}$ | 127 / 65 |
| complex, U64 | 72 | 64 | $7.3 \times 10^{-20}$ | $1.2 \times 10^{-10}$ | 62 / 33 |
| quaternions, U32 | 40 | 32 | $2.6 \times 10^{-10}$ | $7.6 \times 10^{-6}$ | 31 / 17 |
| octonions, U16 | 24 | 16 | $1.8 \times 10^{-5}$ | $2.0 \times 10^{-3}$ | 15 / 9 |
| quaternions promoted once, U64 | 72 | 63 | $1.3 \times 10^{-19}$ | $2.3 \times 10^{-10}$ | 62 / 32 |
| quaternions promoted twice, U128 | 136 | 126 | $1.6 \times 10^{-38}$ | $5.4 \times 10^{-20}$ | 125 / 64 |

Each borrowed slash bit costs one data bit, so a promoted quaternion is one bit short of the complex member of the same size.

## The uniform header

In the uniform design every algebra uses the octonion header: 4 unit bits (a sign and a 3-bit axis) and 4 slash bits. The base word is U16, and each doubling borrows one more slash bit. A value of a smaller algebra uses only axes below its dimension.

| Algebra | Sizes available (uniform) | Data bits, uniform vs fitted, at the same size | Unit codes unused |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| reals | 24, 40, 72, 136, … | 3 fewer from U128 on; U16–U64 are new sizes | 14 of 16 |
| complex | 24, 40, 72, 136, … | 2 fewer from U64 on; U16 and U32 are new | 12 of 16 |
| quaternions | 24, 40, 72, 136, … | 1 fewer from U32 on; U16 is new | 8 of 16 |
| octonions | 24, 40, 72, 136, … | the same | 0 of 16 |

Decoding cost, in C on random U64 patterns:

| Data | Fitted complex layout | Uniform layout | One decoder, layout given at run time |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 4,096 values, in cache | 1.2 ns | 1.4 ns | 1.8 ns |
| $10^7$ values, from memory | 3.4 ns | 3.8 ns | 4.0 ns |

A first version of this measurement made the fitted layout look 2.5× slower. The cause was a branch the compiler emitted for the sign in one loop only. With the sign computed without a branch, all three decoders are within about half a nanosecond per value, which is small next to the arithmetic. The C decoder is tested against the reference decoder in `family.py`.

**Findings.**
- **One decoder and one unit table serve every algebra,** and tests confirm that a value has the same bits in every algebra that contains it: complex $i$ is quaternion $i$ is octonion $i$.
- **This protects exactness, not just convenience.** In the fitted design, a complex value at 72 bits that uses all 64 data bits cannot move into the quaternions at the same size without losing a bit: a quaternion at 72 bits has 63. In the uniform design, moving between algebras never changes a value's bits.
- **The costs are precision and code space:** 3, 2 and 1 data bits for reals, complex numbers and quaternions at every size, and unused unit codes.
- **It gives every algebra small sizes,** including 24- and 40-bit reals and complex numbers, which the fitted design lacks.

## Exact matrix products with wide entries

Element-by-element work cannot use BLAS: each value is used once, so there is no matrix product for BLAS to run. A matrix product can. It does $n^3$ multiplications on $n^2$ values, which is what BLAS is built for. So in the vector form, a product of matrices over shared denominators is an integer matrix product, with the denominators simply multiplied.

The method:
1. Reduce every entry modulo many primes below about $2^{24}$. They are small enough that one double-precision `dgemm` per prime is exact.
2. Compute one `dgemm` per prime.
3. Rebuild each entry with the Chinese remainder theorem.

Two versions were built:
- **`mm`:** uses FLINT's routines for the reduction and the reconstruction.
- **`mmg`:** keeps entries in the uN fixed-limb layout and turns both conversions into `dgemm`s as well. Reduction multiplies the entries' 16-bit digits by a table of $2^{16j} \bmod p$; reconstruction multiplies the residues by the 16-bit digits of $M/p$.

They are compared with:
- FLINT's `fmpz_mat_mul` and its BLAS method `fmpz_mat_mul_blas`, with FLINT 3.0.1 rebuilt with BLAS against the same OpenBLAS (`build_flint_blas.sh`);
- the system's FLINT without BLAS;
- FLINT's rational matrices.

One thread throughout, dense matrices with random signed entries.

| Size | Entry bits | Fastest FLINT | `mmg` | FLINT / `mmg` | `mmg` with everything timed | Memory, FLINT BLAS / `mmg` |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 128 | 1024 | 81 ms | 64 ms | 1.25× | 1.22× | +30 / +86 MB |
| 128 | 2048 | 203 ms | 152 ms | 1.34× | 1.31× | +56 / +184 MB |
| 256 | 64 | 17 ms | 15 ms | 1.18× | 0.98× | +12 / +27 MB |
| 256 | 256 | 63 ms | 67 ms | 0.94× | 0.91× | +30 / +89 MB |
| 256 | 1024 | 332 ms | 283 ms | 1.17× | 0.99× | +97 / +327 MB |
| 256 | 2048 | 850 ms | 648 ms | 1.31× | 1.01× | +187 / +660 MB |
| 512 | 256 | 296 ms | 305 ms | 0.97× | 0.84× | +114 / +351 MB |
| 512 | 1024 | 1,548 ms | 1,281 ms | 1.21× | 0.44× | +384 / +1,320 MB |
| 512 | 2048 | 3,807 ms | 2,904 ms | 1.31× | 0.77× | +746 / +2,622 MB |

"With everything timed" adds the work `mmg` otherwise does once, outside the timed region: choosing primes, building tables, allocating buffers, converting FLINT's matrices to limbs and back, and freeing. All rows are in [`test/results/family.md`](../test/results/family.md).

**Findings.**
- **The float matrix product is the cheap part.** It is 5–30% of the time; reducing and reconstructing are the rest. That is why `mm`, which uses FLINT's own conversions, loses to FLINT.
- **Doing the conversions as matrix products too is what pays,** and the fixed-limb layout makes that natural. With data already in that layout, `mmg` is 1.2× to 1.3× faster than FLINT's best at 1,024 and 2,048 bits, and about even at 64 and 256 bits.
- **Converting from FLINT's format and allocating each time erase the lead at 512.** The worst case is 0.44× at 512 × 512 with 1,024-bit entries, which is mostly the cost of touching 1.3 GB of fresh memory.
- **`mmg` uses 3–4× FLINT's memory,** because it processes all primes and entries at once. Processing them in chunks, as FLINT does, should remove most of that.
- **Against the system FLINT without BLAS,** `mmg` is 2.0× to 2.2× faster at 512 × 512. An earlier draft of these findings quoted that figure as the result against FLINT; it is not.

## Design questions for discussion

1. **The packed power-of-two form (not yet tested).** Put the header inside the value, so that sizes are 16, 32, 64, 128 … bits instead of a byte plus a word (24, 40, 72, 136 …).
   - **Units:** they would occupy the top 4 bits at every size, so complex $i$ remains quaternion $i$.
   - **Size changes:** they already re-encode in the two-tuple design, because the slash field is split between the header and borrowed word bits.
   - **Precision:** precision per bit is about the same.
   - **Gain:** values align with registers, cache lines and SIMD lanes.
   - **Proposed:** the next build uses this form, with the two-tuple as the comparison.
2. **The uniform header.** The measurements favour one header layout for every algebra, at a cost of 1–3 bits, because moving between algebras then never changes a value or loses exactness.
3. **The cap.** Element-by-element uN work reaches parity with GMP near 2,048 bits and loses with naive fixed-width code, so a cap near 1,024 bits is supported. Should the spec fix it, or define uN parametrically and recommend 1,024?
4. **Wide element-by-element arithmetic in residues.** The residue representation that made the matrix product fast can be used without BLAS: a residue number system turns one wide multiply into many independent small ones, so the cost grows linearly with width. Comparisons, sign checks and division need a conversion back. It is a candidate for the tiers past 1,024 bits.
5. **The comparison suite.** Rows by operation, whatever their shape: add, multiply, exact division, compare and sort, sum and dot product, growth chains, matrix–vector, dense and sparse matrix products, linear solve, and conversion to and from decimal text. GMP, FLINT and the format on every row, one table per width at 128, 256, 512, 1,024 and 2,048 bits. That includes a GMP matrix-product row, which this round lacked.
6. **Sparse and very large problems.** Exact sparse linear algebra uses iterative methods built from sparse matrix–vector products modulo primes, as in LinBox, not BLAS. Very large problems turn to Strassen on top of BLAS for large matrices, and to FFT multiplication for very wide entries. Integer `GEMM` on int8 units (the Ozaki II line of work) is the fastest current route.
   BLIS is a further option for writing our own exact kernels inside its blocking and packing framework, rather than reusing `dgemm` as it stands:
   - a modular or 52-bit integer multiply-add kernel (AVX-512 IFMA), with the reduction modulo $p$ inside the kernel instead of a separate pass;
   - splitting entries into digits while the data is packed;
   - kernels suited to the thin matrix shapes of the two conversions, which dominate the time.
7. **Widening rule in arrays.** Whole-vector widening keeps arrays dense but costs about 2.7× when a few values are wide. Is per-element promotion worth specifying as an option?
8. **Octonions.** Specify the U16 member, or record it as the end of the pattern? At 16 data bits it holds small constants, not computed values. In arrays, octonions are the strongest case for the vector form.
9. **The unit type on its own.** The unit bits alone are each algebra's signed units, a useful type for direction without magnitude. It needs a name: mathematicians call the quaternion units "Q8", which clashes with the family's naming.
10. **Naming the family**, now that it is no longer only the Q family of v0.3.
