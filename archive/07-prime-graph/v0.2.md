# Prime Graph: Exact Rationals with Shared, Factored Denominators, Specification v0.2

Oct 28, 2026 · Andrew Emerson

This version rewrites the prose of [v0.1](v0.1.md) for a reader with a mathematics degree and some computer science. The design is unchanged. Every term is a descriptive name defined before it is used (no internal labels), a [glossary](#glossary) collects them, the main rules come with short justifications, and the research history has moved to [Appendix A](#appendix-a-evidence).

## 1. Overview

### The problem

A computer stores a rational number exactly as a pair of integers: a numerator and a denominator. Libraries that do this, such as GMP and FLINT, keep every value in **lowest terms**, with numerator and denominator sharing no factor. Lowest terms keep the integers small and make each value's representation unique. The price is a greatest common divisor (gcd) computation after nearly every operation. That gcd, not the additions or multiplications themselves, dominates the cost of exact arithmetic.

A known remedy applies when many values share one denominator, for example a column of prices in cents, all over 100. Store the shared denominator once and keep only the numerators. Arithmetic on the column then becomes integer arithmetic on an array, with no gcd per element. Our earlier specs call this the **vector form**. It is very fast when the values share a denominator and very slow when they don't, because the common denominator of unrelated fractions grows enormous.

### The idea

Most real data sits between those extremes. Values neither share one denominator nor have unrelated ones: their denominators share *some* prime factors. For example, 1/12, 5/18 and 7/30 share the primes 2 and 3, though no two have the same denominator.

This design stores **the prime factors of denominators once, and shares them among all the values that use them.** The values and the shared factors form a graph:
- on one side, each value's numerator;
- on the other, the factors of denominators;
- edges labelled with exponents, connecting each numerator to the factors of its denominator.

Operations then work out common denominators and cancellations from the shared factors directly, instead of recomputing gcds. Where values share a whole denominator, the design falls back to the vector form's integer arrays, keeping its speed.

Factoring is done incrementally. A factor is split out of a denominator only when another denominator turns out to share it, and the work never grows with the total size of the graph.

### Relation to other specs

- **[05](../05-exact/v0.1.md)** defines the vector form, which the [array form](#7-arrays) here extends. This spec stores numerators in two's complement, where 05 stores magnitudes with a separate sign column. Two's complement was measured faster for arithmetic on numbers of mixed sign (see [Appendix A](#appendix-a-evidence)).
- **06,** to come, will add complex and other units.

**Scope:** exact arithmetic on the rational numbers. Out of scope: rounding and approximation, programming-language syntax, input and output formats, and complex numbers.

## 2. Terms and notation

Every term is also in the [glossary](#glossary).

- Integers are written $m, n, d, \ldots$; $\gcd$ and $\text{lcm}$ are on positive integers.
- Two integers are **coprime** if their gcd is 1. A set of integers is **pairwise coprime** if every two of them are coprime.
- A **value** is an exact rational number stored by this system.
- A **handle** is how a program refers to a value: an opaque reference, in the way a file handle refers to an open file.

## 3. The representation

All values are stored in one **graph** with two kinds of vertex and labelled edges.

### 3.1 Numerators

Each value has one **numerator**, a signed integer stored in two's complement, which carries the value's sign. The numerator is the value's identity: the program's handle points to it, and the value is stored nowhere else.

### 3.2 Factor nodes, atoms and leftovers

The other kind of vertex is a **factor node** (or just *node*): an integer greater than 1 that occurs as a factor of some denominator.

A node is stored as a short list of **atoms**, each with an exponent. Atoms are integers greater than 1 with two properties:
- **All atoms in the graph are pairwise coprime.**
- **Each atom is either a proved prime, or a *leftover*:** a factor not yet split into primes, which the system treats as if it were prime until it learns otherwise.

The exponents of a node's atoms are **normalised** to have gcd 1. A node with a single atom is then exactly that atom (its exponent is 1), and any power goes on the edges instead.

Why nodes hold several atoms at all: it is wasteful to separate primes that always occur together. If every denominator containing 3 also contains 5, in the same proportions, then 15 can stay one node. Nodes are split only when some denominator uses their atoms differently (§5.3).

### 3.3 Edges and weights

A numerator is connected to each node in its value's denominator. Each **edge** carries a **weight** $w \ge 1$, the power of that node in the denominator. A value with numerator $m$ and edges to nodes $n_1, \ldots, n_k$ with weights $w_1, \ldots, w_k$ is

$$
x = \frac{m}{n_1^{w_1} \cdots n_k^{w_k}}.
$$

An integer has no edges. Zero is the numerator 0 with no edges.

**Edges are stored at both ends.** Each numerator keeps a list of its (node, weight) pairs, and each edge also records its position in the node's list. Each node keeps the list of numerators connected to it, and a **reference count**, the length of that list.

### 3.4 Holders

Numerators are not the only things with edges. An array's shared denominator also has edges (§7). Anything that has edges is called a **holder**: a value's numerator, or an array's shared denominator.

### 3.5 The dictionary

A lookup table, the **dictionary**, maps each atom to the node that contains it. It is a direct-indexed array for atoms below $2^{16}$, and a hash table above. When a node is freed, its atoms are removed from the dictionary.

### 3.6 Invariants

These properties hold between operations:

- **I1. Coprime atoms.** No two atoms in the graph share a factor. Hence no two nodes share a factor.
- **I2. Edge symmetry.** An edge appears in a holder's list exactly when the holder appears in the node's list, with the same weight.
- **I3. Reference counts.** Each node's reference count equals the length of its holder list. When it reaches zero, the node is freed.
- **I4. Lowest terms.**
  - A single value is in lowest terms after every operation that produces it: its numerator shares no factor with any of its nodes, and no edge has weight 0.
  - An array is in lowest terms *as a whole* after every array expression, a chain of operations on arrays evaluated together (§7.3).
- **I5. Values are unique; their internal form is not.** How a denominator is divided into nodes depends on the history of the graph (which values were entered, released and reduced, and in what order), and how an array is divided into dense elements and exceptions depends on the choice of $D$. The rational value, and every result, never depends on either.

*Why I1 matters.* Because nodes are pairwise coprime, each denominator has exactly one way of being written as a product of node powers. Common denominators and cancellations can then be read off the edges, which is the source of the speed.

## 4. Lifetimes

- **Creating** a value enters its denominator (§5), reduces it to lowest terms (§6.1), and returns a handle.
- **Releasing** a value removes its numerator from each of its nodes' lists and lowers their reference counts. Nodes that reach zero are freed (I3). Releasing an array releases its shared denominator and each exception (an element with its own edges, §7.1) in the same way.
- **When a result cancels.** If a result's numerator becomes 0, or an edge's weight falls to 0, those edges are removed and the reference counts lowered in the same way.

Milestone M1 (§10) is single-threaded.

## 5. Entering a denominator

When a new denominator $d$ arrives, it is decomposed as far as cheaply possible and connected to the graph.

### 5.1 Finding the atoms of $d$

1. **Small primes.**
   - Powers of 2 are removed in one step (counting trailing zero bits).
   - The odd primes below $2^{16}$ are then tried in order, stopping as soon as $p^2$ exceeds what remains of $d$: at that point what remains must be 1 or prime.
   - Each divisibility test is a multiplication by a precomputed inverse and a comparison, done eight primes at a time without branches.
   - Each prime that divides $d$ is divided out as often as it goes.
2. **What remains.** Call it $r$. If $r > 1$:
   - look $r$ up in the dictionary as a whole;
   - otherwise, $r$ is prime if trial division stopped early or $r < 2^{32}$;
   - otherwise, try dividing $r$ by the 16 most recently seen large primes. This is a bounded list, a cheap shortcut that correctness never relies on;
   - then test $r$ for primality with a deterministic Miller–Rabin test (a fast probabilistic primality test that is exact for all 64-bit numbers with a fixed set of test bases);
   - and if $r$ is composite, split it with Pollard's rho method (a randomised factoring method that finds a factor of a 64-bit number in microseconds to milliseconds).

   The bounded list is safe because correctness never depends on it: every atom found is still looked up in the dictionary (§5.2).

   **Forbidden:** trying every large prime the system has ever seen. That cost would grow with the graph (§8).

### 5.2 Connecting $d$ to the graph

Each atom of $d$ is looked up in the dictionary.
- **Atoms never seen before** are put together into **one new node**, with their exponents normalised. No existing node is touched.
- **Each existing node $h$ that contains some of $d$'s atoms** must be checked, because $d$ may use $h$'s atoms in different proportions from $h$ itself. The rule for that check is in §5.3.

Finally, $d$'s edges are built by dictionary lookups.

### 5.3 Splitting a node

**When to split.** Let node $h$ have atoms $a_1, \ldots, a_k$ with exponents $e_1, \ldots, e_k$, and let $d$ contain $a_i$ with exponent $f_i$ ($f_i = 0$ if absent). Group $h$'s atoms by the ratio $f_i / e_i$. If all the ratios are equal, to $\rho$ say, then $d$'s share of $h$ is exactly $h^{\rho}$, and no split is needed. Otherwise, $h$ is split into one node per ratio class.

*Why the ratio is a whole number.* If every ratio equals $p/q$ in lowest terms, then $q$ divides every $e_i$; the $e_i$ have gcd 1, so $q = 1$ and $\rho$ is a positive integer, a valid edge weight.

*Why this is right.* An edge can only say "the denominator contains this node to the power $w$". So $d$ can use $h$ through an edge only if $d$'s part on $h$'s atoms is a power of $h$ itself, which is exactly the condition that the ratios agree. Splitting by ratio classes is therefore necessary. It is also the least splitting that works, so the graph stays as coarse as correctness allows.

Examples:
- 30 then 45: $30 = 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5$ and $45 = 3^2 \cdot 5$. The ratios on 30's atoms are 0, 2 and 1, all different, so 30 splits into 2, 3 and 5.
- 30 then 900: $900 = 30^2$. Every ratio is 2, so nothing splits, and 900 is an edge of weight 2 to node 30.
- 100 then 35: $100 = 2^2 5^2$ becomes the single node 10, with weight 2. Then $35 = 5 \cdot 7$ gives ratios 0 and 1 on 10's atoms, so 10 splits into 2 and 5. The result is nodes 2, 5 and 7.

**How to split (copy and loop).** To move a set $Q$ of node $N$'s atoms into a new node $P$:
1. Copy $N$'s holder list as $P$'s holder list. $N$ keeps its other atoms $R$, with exponents renormalised.
2. Loop once over that list, giving each holder an edge to $P$ in the same list position.
3. The holder that triggered the split gets its edges afterwards, when its own edges are built.

If either part would be empty, there is nothing to split. The weights carry over by the following rule. Let $g_Q$ and $g_R$ be the gcds of the original exponents of the atoms in $Q$ and in $R$. Then $N = R^{g_R} P^{g_Q}$, as integers, with $R$ and $P$ normalised. So a holder with weight $w$ on $N$ gets weight $w g_R$ on $R$ and $w g_Q$ on $P$, and its value is unchanged.

*Cost.* One pass over the node's holder list, with no searching, since the list says exactly whom to update. A split happens at most once for each new sharing discovered, and no record of past splits is kept: shared lists, forwarding links and any other history structure are excluded.

### 5.4 Entering a value

A rational $m/d$, with $d > 0$ and the sign carried by $m$, is entered by entering $d$ as above, then reducing $m$ against $d$'s nodes (§6.1).

## 6. Arithmetic

Every operation reads only its operands' holders, their edges and those nodes, never the rest of the graph.

### 6.1 Reduction to lowest terms

To cancel a numerator $m$ against a set of nodes, for each node:
- **A node that is a prime $p$, with weight $w$:** divide $m$ by $p$ while it divides, at most $w$ times, lowering the result's weight each time.
- **A node with several atoms, or a leftover, $N$ with weight $w$:** compute $g = \gcd(m, N)$.
  - **If $g = N$,** divide by $N$ as for a prime.
  - **If $g$ is a proper factor of $N$,** split $N$: if $N$ has several atoms, move them by §5.3; if $N$ is a single leftover $L$, *refine* it, replacing $L$ by the pairwise coprime parts of $g$ and $L/g$ (computed by gcds), with every holder's edges updated by the same copy-and-loop as §5.3. Then continue on the parts.
  - **If $g = 1$,** do nothing.
- Edges whose weight reaches 0 are removed.

Reduction changes the result, never an operand.

*Why testing only these nodes suffices.* A prime dividing both $m$ and the denominator must divide one of the denominator's nodes, since the denominator is their product, and every node is tested.

### 6.2 Addition and subtraction

For $x = m_x / \prod n_i^{a_i}$ and $y = m_y / \prod n_i^{b_i}$:
1. **Common denominator:** each node at the larger of its two weights, $\prod n_i^{\max(a_i, b_i)}$. Because the nodes are pairwise coprime, this is exactly the lcm of the two denominators, read off the edges with no gcd.
2. **Scale and add:** multiply each numerator by the node powers its denominator is missing, and add.
3. **Reduce** the sum against the common denominator's nodes (§6.1).

### 6.3 Multiplication

1. **Cross-cancel:** reduce each numerator against the *other* operand's nodes.
2. **Multiply** the numerators.
3. **Add the weights** on shared nodes.

*Why this gives lowest terms* (for single values; inside a deferred array expression the operands are not in lowest terms, and the final reduction does this work). Each operand is already in lowest terms, so $m_x$ shares nothing with $x$'s own denominator. Any common factor of the product can only pair $m_x$ with $y$'s denominator, or $m_y$ with $x$'s, and step 1 removes exactly those.

### 6.4 Division

$x / y$, with $y = m_y / d_y$, is $x$ times $d_y / m_y$:
1. the sign of $m_y$ moves to the result's numerator;
2. $|m_y|$ is entered as a new denominator (§5);
3. $d_y$'s node powers are multiplied into the result's numerator, with overflow checked;
4. the result is reduced (§6.1).

**Division by zero is an error.**

### 6.5 Comparison, negation and sign

- **Comparison** scales the two numerators to the common denominator, as in addition, and compares them as integers. With 64-bit values each scaled numerator fits in 128 bits (64 + 63), so comparison never overflows.
- **Negation** negates the numerator. It overflows only at the most negative 64-bit integer.

## 7. Arrays

### 7.1 The array form

An array of values, created from a list or produced by an array operation, is stored in the **array form**:
- **a shared denominator $D$:** a holder with its own edges;
- **dense elements:** the values whose denominator divides $D$, stored only as integer numerators scaled to $D$, with no edges of their own;
- **exceptions:** the remaining values, each a holder with its own edges, kept in a sparse separate list.

An element becomes an exception when its denominator does not divide $D$, or when an operation's result for it overflows at the scale of $D$.

Elementwise operations and aggregates such as sums run as plain integer loops over the dense elements, and through the graph only for the exceptions. When every element shares $D$, this is exactly the vector form.

### 7.2 Choosing $D$ and the shared denominator of results

**Choosing $D$ for a new array** is not yet decided (§11). Two choices were measured:
- the lcm of all the elements' denominators, which won when it stays small;
- the most frequent denominator, which won when the lcm made products overflow.

**Provisional default for M1, not yet measured:** the lcm if it is below $2^{63}$, otherwise the most frequent denominator. This hybrid still misses cases where an lcm below $2^{63}$ makes products overflow, which is where the most frequent denominator won.

**The shared denominator of a result.** An elementwise result of two arrays with shared denominators $D_a$ and $D_b$ gets:
- for addition and subtraction, $\text{lcm}(D_a, D_b)$ (each node at the larger weight);
- for multiplication, $D_a D_b$ (the weights added);
- for division, the default rule applied to the results.

An aggregate such as a sum produces a single value.

### 7.3 Deferred reduction

Within an expression over arrays, such as $(a \cdot b + c) \cdot e$, intermediate results are **not** reduced to lowest terms:
- **On overflow** in a step, that step's operands are reduced and the step is retried. Only an overflow that survives the retry is reported as an error.
- **At the end** of the expression, the result is reduced once, as a whole: $D$ and the dense numerators are made to share no common factor, and each exception is put in lowest terms (I4).

Whether single values should also defer reduction within an expression is undecided (§11).

## 8. Scalability requirements

The graph grows with every value stored, so the design must make sure no operation slows down as it grows. These requirements are normative:

- **S1. Local operations.** An arithmetic operation touches only its operands, their edges and those nodes. Its cost must not depend on the size of the graph.
- **S2. Edges at both ends** (§3.3), so that an operation finds its denominators without searching, and a split finds its holders without searching.
- **S3. Splits by copy and loop** (§5.3), with no record of past splits.
- **S4. No scanning at entry.** Sharing is found by dictionary lookups, never by comparing a new denominator with every node, or with every known large prime.
- **S5. Freeing.** Nodes nobody uses are freed by reference count, so the size of the graph is bounded by the values alive.

The only costs allowed to grow are those every system has: the size of the numbers themselves, and array operations that are linear in the length of the array.

## 9. Precision and determinism

- **M1 (64 bits).** Numerators and atoms are 64-bit integers; intermediate results use 128 bits. Overflow is always detected, never silently wrapped. It is reported as an error, after deferred reduction's retry where that applies (§7.3).
- **M2 (arbitrary precision).** Numerators and atoms will grow beyond 64 bits, using the multi-word kernels of [05](../05-exact/v0.1.md). Leftovers beyond 64 bits will need a way of finding shared factors that respects S4 (§11).
- **Determinism.** Every result is exact, and the rational value is unique, so results are the same on every platform and do not depend on when nodes were split (I5).
- **Platform requirements:** 64-bit integer arithmetic, and a 64 × 64 → 128-bit multiply.

## 10. Milestones

1. **M1. A working C library at 64 bits:** this specification's representation, lifetimes, entry, splitting, arithmetic, arrays and scalability requirements, with tests and invariant checks, and the small-graph speed fixes listed in §11.
2. **M2.** Arbitrary precision.
3. **M3.** A full comparison with GMP and FLINT, and a full prior-art review, followed by the next version of this spec.

## 11. Open decisions

- **Choosing the shared denominator $D$** of an array (provisional default in §7.2).
- **Deferred reduction for single values.** It was measured helping single values that share denominators (7–18%), but M1 reduces single values after every operation.
- **Small-graph speed.** With few distinct denominators, the system currently trails a simpler design without the dictionary in two ways:
  - about 10% on elementwise operations on decimal data; entry times were equal, so this appears to be an implementation cost in the operations;
  - slower entry, at about 100 distinct denominators.
- **The cost of Pollard's rho at entry:** about 60 µs per new denominator made of two large primes, paid once per distinct denominator. Postponing it gained nothing in single-threaded tests.
- **Finding shared factors among leftovers larger than 64 bits** (M2) without violating S4. The one batch method tested grew faster than the graph; one alternative is to refine leftovers only against leftovers they meet in an operation.
- **Memory after very large splits:** freed edge storage is reused only for lists of the same size class, so memory rises in steps.
- **The operation set and interface:**
  - printing and parsing;
  - conversion to and from integers, floating-point numbers and 05's `Exact`;
  - error reporting.
- **Thread safety** of the shared graph.
- **Errors inside arrays:** whether division by zero fails a whole array operation or marks the affected elements.

## 12. Future directions

These were discussed but are not part of the design yet:
- **Background decomposition on a spare core.** Entering a value would cost almost nothing: its denominator would join the graph as one unsplit node, usable at once with ordinary per-value arithmetic, while another thread decomposes it and splits it into the graph. This needs a thread-safe graph, and a refinement method that respects S4. A single-threaded version of postponed factoring was tested and failed: its batch pass grew faster than the graph (Appendix A, stress test).
- **GPUs.** The dense integer arrays, exact matrix products by modular arithmetic, and batches of trial division suit a GPU. The graph's bookkeeping does not, and would stay on the CPU.

## Appendix A: Evidence

All measurements were made on one machine (an Intel Xeon). Each was preceded by an independent review, and every result was checked against Python's exact `fractions` module.

**First prototype against GMP and FLINT** ([notes](research/light-results.md)). GMP's `mpq_t` and FLINT's `fmpq` are the standard exact rational types.
- The prototype was faster than GMP on most workloads.
- It was faster than FLINT by 1.35–4.5× on:
  - columns of decimals;
  - data with mixed small denominators;
  - chains of repeated multiply-and-add;
  - 32–62-bit denominators built from shared primes.
- On that last workload it was 1.5× faster than both libraries and 20× faster than a single shared denominator. The workload was constructed so that primes are shared.
- It was slower than FLINT on independent single values (0.73–0.84) and on unrelated denominators (0.56).
- **The design in this spec has not yet been compared with GMP and FLINT;** that is milestone M3.

**Replacing grouping** ([notes](research/ablation-grouping.md)). The first prototype kept a global table of values with identical denominators. It was replaced, after testing five alternatives against a baseline without it. The array form of §7 was 3.3–15× faster than the baseline on columns of decimals and on chains. On mixed data its result depended on the choice of $D$ (§7.2): 1.04–14.8× with the lcm, 0.88–8.1× with the most frequent denominator. It ran at 0.85–0.94 of the baseline on single values and on unrelated denominators.

**Second round** ([notes](research/ablation-round2.md)). Three ideas were dropped:
- a quick check for equal or dividing denominators, which had no effect;
- bucketing by denominator during sums, which the array form covers;
- shared denominator objects, which helped only very small values.

The dictionary of §3.5 was 3.5× faster than comparing each new denominator with every node by gcd, once there were 3,512 nodes.

**Third round** ([notes](research/ablation-round3.md)). The decision rule was fixed before the run ([rule](research/round3-rule.md)): 12 interleaved rounds, 95% bootstrap intervals, and a noise band from a null comparison.
- **Deferred reduction (§7.3) improved the array form:** better on 24 of 60 comparisons, worse on 1. It helped mixed and unrelated denominators by 13–35% (and mixed data under the most-frequent choice of $D$ by 7–28%), and single values that share denominators by 7–18%.
- **The branch-free trial division of §5.1** beat a plain version by 10–43% where entry dominates.
- **The dictionary beat gcd comparison with every node** by 23× at 10,000 distinct denominators and 112× at 30,000.
- **It trailed** at 0.89–0.93 on elementwise work with decimals, 0.89–0.92 on denominators of two large primes, and 0.68 at about 100 distinct denominators (§11).

**Stress test** ([notes](research/scalability-stress.md)). Requirements S1–S5 were implemented together, with the array form (without deferred reduction), the bounded list of large primes, and Pollard's rho at entry.
- Operation and entry times stayed flat as the graph grew to 10 million values.
- Memory and node counts stayed bounded through 10 million deletions and insertions, with no leaks.
- Every invariant audit passed.
- An entry procedure comparing each new denominator with every node grew 51× over the same growth.
- Splitting a node shared by 10 million values took 160–236 ms, linear in the number of values.
- A variant that postponed all large-factor work to a batch pass grew faster than the graph, so no batch method has yet been validated.

**Signs.**
- [Q v0.3](../03-q/v0.3.md#the-layout-of-the-vector-form) found two's complement the fastest of its array layouts.
- 04's library comparison found it 2.4–7.6× faster than sign and magnitude at 128 bits on addition, dot products and chains, though slower on scanning bit lengths.

## Appendix B: Prior art

Reviews are in the research folder: a [first pass](research/prior-art-light.md), a [second, more thorough pass](research/prior-art-2.md), and a [review of techniques for finding shared factors](research/prior-art-3-sharing.md). The closest prior art:
- **ALTRAN's factored rational expressions** (W. S. Brown, and A. D. Hall Jr., 1974): rational *functions* stored with factored numerators and denominators, sharing factor objects. The authors explicitly rejected keeping factors coprime as too costly.
- **FriCAS's `Fraction Factored Integer`:** rationals with factored denominators, but factored eagerly and shared between no values.
- **Factor refinement** (Bach, Driscoll and Shallit, 1993; Bernstein, 2005): algorithms for turning a set of integers into pairwise coprime factors. It is used as an algorithm, never as a way of storing rationals.
- **Shared-denominator arrays** in FLINT and LinBox: the shared denominator is never factored.

The second pass judged the combination in this spec probably new, at about 70% confidence. A full review is part of M3.
