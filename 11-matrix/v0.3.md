## 16. Amendment of Oct 30, 2026: round m3

### 16.0 Why

Round m2 ([`test/results-m2-mul/`](test/results-m2-mul/results.md)) found the following:
- Hash-V-pack wins on memory.
- It loses on multiplication time: **0.83–0.90 of TC's speed** (about 12% slower at $n \ge 32$), and the same in sop (0.85–0.90) and pos (0.85–0.92).
- Against Hash-V-fixed it ties on every multiplication job (0.99–1.005). So the loss comes from reading through handles, not from packing.

The arithmetic is TC's, call for call. In TC's multiplication job at $n = 128$ (job 13), the gcds are a large share of the 247 ms:
- the products' 3.6 million gcds, 46 million bits of gcd work;
- the inner sums' 4.0 million gcds, 115 million bits.

Round m3 tries to win that time back. Every whole number is stored with its factorization. Then a gcd becomes the minimum of exponents, an lcm the maximum, and a product the sum ([notation](../09-coprime-base/research/coprime-base-notation.md) §3). This applies to numerators and denominators alike: multiplication's cross cancellations, and the in-between cases of addition where the denominators share some factors but not all.

**A fact this design rests on** (from 08 and 09, and the paper's §2.6–2.7): **every gcd of the gcd-first arithmetic has a denominator as an argument, or a divisor of one.**
- Addition: $\gcd(b, d)$; $\gcd(t, g)$ with $g \mid b$; $\gcd(a + c, b)$.
- Multiplication: $\gcd(a, d)$, $\gcd(c, b)$.

Two consequences follow:
- **A new numerator never needs factoring for the arithmetic.** A new numerator is a sum $t$, or $a + c$, or $d' \pm b'$. Reducing it needs only the denominator's factors (§16.6).
- **Cross-matrix work can be limited to denominator factors** (§16.7).

### 16.2 Terms

As v0.1 §2 and v0.2, with:
- **Factorization** of a whole number $v \ge 2$: the list of pairs $(q, \nu_q(v))$ with $\nu_q(v) \ge 1$, sorted by the factor's value $q$.
  - In Pack-primes, the $q$ are primes.
  - In Pack-coprime, the $q$ are **pieces** of the matrix's coprime base.
  - The factorization of 1 is empty. 0 has none: its list is empty and is never read (§16.6 item 1).
- **Factor**: a prime (Pack-primes) or a piece (Pack-coprime).
- **Equal valuation**: a factor $q$ with $\nu_q(b) = \nu_q(d) \ge 1$ for two denominators $b$, $d$. Only these can cancel in a sum (09 notation note §6).
- **Merge**: one walk of two sorted factorizations together, comparing factors by value. It is the factored rows' replacement for a gcd. Its cost is counted in entries compared.
- **Divisibility test**: one division with remainder of a whole number by a known factor $q$, to test $q \mid t$.
- **Temporary list**: the factorization of a temporary's *denominator*, held in local variables with the temporary (§16.6 item 4).
- **Denominator factors of a matrix**, $D_X$: the distinct factors that occur in the factorization of some denominator of $X$.

**Scratch, not structures** (approved by the author, Oct 30, 2026, as necessary schoolbook scratch). Each is a local array of one calculation, in the same class as a temporary's two 64-bit integers (v0.1 §2). None outlives its calculation, none is shared between calculations, and none is a cache:
- **(t) the temporary lists**, both factored rows: each temporary's denominator list, at most 15 entries. A 64-bit number has at most 15 distinct primes, and at most 15 pairwise coprime pieces, since the product of the first 16 primes exceeds $2^{64}$.
- **The merge's gcd list**, both factored rows: the list of $g = \gcd(\cdot, \cdot)$ that one merge writes, at most 15 entries, read by the same case to multiply out $g$'s value and to form the result's list. It is the plain way to hold a gcd that has more than one factor.
- **Refine's working set $S$**, Pack-coprime: the pieces of $\{h, q/h\}$ or $\{q, r\}$ while a split computes their coprime base (notation note §4), at most 32 entries: the pieces are pairwise coprime and each at least 2, and their product divides a product of two numbers below $2^{64}$.

**Removed after the review:** pass 2's sort buffer. Pass 2 sorts each number's entries in place in (f), as they are appended (§16.4 item 4). The buffer was not needed.

**Replacements, not new structures** (as v0.1's (n) and (w)):
- **(f′)** is (f)'s replacement. A split after the build (§16.8) rebuilds one matrix's (f) in one pass into a new allocation. Old and new coexist only inside that pass, and (f′) then *is* that matrix's (f).
- **(f) and (q) grown by doubling** while they are written (§16.4), then shrunk with `realloc`, as v0.2 shrinks the cells. Each is the same structure, reallocated.

**Counting (f) as one structure** is approved by the author, Oct 30, 2026. Its three arrays have one owner, one lifetime and one index (the handle), as (a)'s two handle fields do. *Alternative:* count three. Then Pack-primes is 5 per matrix (13 and 37 per job) and Pack-coprime 6 (17 and 45).

**Nothing else exists** (v0.1 §3): no cache, no index from factors to numbers, no record of meetings, no prime table, no sieve.

**Driver arrays:** (in-A) to (in-D) and (out), as v0.2.

### 16.4 Building a stored matrix (both factored rows)

1. **Build and pack exactly as Hash-V-pack** (v0.2 §15.4 items 2–3): the build table (h), the packed list (l), the pack map (p), the cells rewritten in place, and (h) and (p) freed. So (h) and (l) are Hash-V-pack's, slot for slot and entry for entry.
2. **Then factor (factor after the pack, not during the build; approved by the author, Oct 30, 2026).** Each whole number of (l) is factored once, in list order, and its entries are appended to (f).
   - 0 and 1 get empty lists.
   - Entries are sorted by factor value.
   - (f)'s arrays start at $2W$ entries, double when full, and are shrunk with `realloc` at the end.

   *Why after the pack:* each distinct number is factored once, whether during the build (at each new store) or after it. After the pack:
   - (h) keeps Hash-V-pack's slots;
   - the store counters stay equal to Hash-V-pack's;
   - (h) and (p) are already freed, which lowers the peak.
3. **Pack-primes: schoolbook trial division (the candidates are 2, then the odd numbers; approved by the author, Oct 30, 2026).**

   ```text
   function factor(v)                                  v ≥ 2
       let m = v, d = 2
       while d · d ≤ m                                 d < 2^32 since m < 2^63, so d · d fits 64 bits unsigned
           let e = 0
           while m mod d = 0: m ← m / d, e ← e + 1     each "mod" is one division with remainder
           if e > 0: append (d, e)
           d ← 3 if d = 2, else d + 2
       if m > 1: append (m, 1)                         the cofactor left is prime
   end function
   ```

   - No sieve, no prime table, no wheel beyond skipping even candidates, and no early exit other than $d^2 > m$.
   - *Alternative:* every integer as a candidate, which doubles the cost; not chosen, since skipping evens is the schoolbook form.
   - The loop stops at the first candidate $d > \max(P_2, \sqrt{P_1})$, where $P_1$ is the largest prime factor and $P_2$ the second largest, with multiplicity. So **its cost is set by the two largest prime factors**, and the model predicts each count in closed form from the factorization.
4. **Pack-coprime: two passes over (l), gcds and divisions only (approved by the author, Oct 30, 2026).**
   - **Pass 1, the base.** For each whole number $v \ge 2$ in list order, 09's `absorb` scan (09 design note, "Meeting two denominators"):
     - `rest` starts at $v$, and the scan goes through (q) from its first piece.
     - Compute $h = \gcd(\text{rest}, q)$:
       - if $h = 1$, go to the next piece;
       - if $h = q$, divide `rest` by $q$ as many times as it goes, and stay at $q$;
       - otherwise **split** $q$ into the coprime base of $\{h, q/h\}$, by gcds alone (notation note §4). The first new piece takes $q$'s index; the others are appended to (q). Stay at this index.
     - The scan never goes back, and it stops when `rest` is 1. If `rest` is not 1 at the end, it is appended as a new piece.
   - **Pass 2, the factorizations.** For each whole number in list order:
     - for each piece $q$ of the final (q), in order, while the number is above 1: divisibility tests by $q$, dividing as often as it goes;
     - append each entry to (f) and move it down past the larger entries already appended for this number (insertion sort in place, at most 15 entries).

   *Why two passes:* no factorization exists until (q) is final, so **a split during the build rewrites nothing but (q)** (§16.8). *Cost:* pass 2 makes about $W \lvert q \rvert / 2$ divisibility tests, cheaper than the gcds of pass 1.

   *Known risk, flagged:* this is the **eager** refinement that 09 abandoned. Refining every new denominator against the whole base cost 58–161 times the control's gcd work there (09 findings, lesson 1). The author has chosen it here deliberately, as the per-matrix form of the coprime row. On the sweep, pass 1 costs about $W \lvert q \rvert / 2$ gcds per result matrix: about $9.5 \times 10^6$ at $n = 64$ (§16.10). At $n = 128$, which this round no longer runs, it would be about $1.5 \times 10^8$.
5. **Where the time falls** (v0.2 §15.4 item 4, unchanged in form):
   - An input matrix's factoring falls in its **build time** (measure 1), inside its last `enter` call.
   - Every intermediate's and every result's factoring falls in the **operation time** (measures 2–5), at the end of the `madd` or `mmul` call that builds it.
   - Each matrix is factored before the next one's build begins, so only one (h) and one (p) exist at a time. Factoring holds neither.
6. **Widths.** (f)'s factors and (q)'s pieces are stored at the list's width $w$: a factor never exceeds its number. Reads sign-extend to 64 bits, as for (l).

### 16.5 The cases, old and new, in cost-of-result order

**The cost of a result**, as round 3's [`../10-interning/v0.3.md`](../10-interning/v0.3.md) §17.3, with the factored rows' work inserted before the core arithmetic:
1. Euclid gcds (08 module): TC; Pack-coprime's per-piece gcds;
2. divisibility tests;
3. merge entries compared;
4. exact divisions, multiplications, additions.

**The order is TC's, unchanged** (10-interning/v0.3 §17.3):
- add: `zero` · `zero_x` · `zero_y` · `int_both` · `int_x` · `int_y` · `cross` · `den` · `num` · `general`;
- multiply: `zero` · `one_x` · `one_y` · `int_both` · `both` · `den` · `num` · `cross_ad` · `cross_cb` · `int_x` · `int_y` · `general`.

It stays cheapest-first under the factored rows' costs:
- every case before a gcd-bearing one is decided by an equality test and makes no merge;
- where two cases overlap, the earlier one's result needs no more work.

For example, `cross` implies $\gcd(b, d) = 1$, which `general` would find only by a merge.

**The new cases** are sub-cases inside the gcd-bearing cases. They are decided by the merge's outcome, so they never overlap, and their order is the order of the decision.

**The value arithmetic is TC's (approved by the author, Oct 30, 2026).** The exact divisions, multiplications and additions of each case are TC's, step for step. The factored rows change only how each gcd value is obtained. For example, a nested pair $d \mid b$ still computes $a \cdot d'$ with $d' = 1$, as TC does. This keeps the call-for-call check exact (§16.9). Value shortcuts that factors allow, such as forming $b' d$ as the lcm from exponents, are a later, separate change.

Notation as 10-interning/v0.3 §17.3: $x = s_x a/b$, $y = s_y c/d$. $F(v)$ is $v$'s factorization: read from (f) for a stored whole number, or the temporary list for a temporary's denominator.

**Addition.** The last column is the result's temporary list, needed only when the result is a temporary (multiplication's sums).

### 16.6 A sum makes a brand-new numerator

A sum $t = a d' + c b'$ (or $s = a + c$, or $d' \pm b'$) is a whole number no structure has seen. Its factors are unknown.

1. **Reducing it needs only the denominator's factors, which are known.** $h = \gcd(t, g)$ is tested only at the factors of $g$ with equal valuation (§16.5).
   - **Pack-primes:** for each such prime $q$, divisibility tests of $t$ by $q$, at most $\nu_q(g)$ divisions, then one failed test. On the sweep every denominator divides $720^2 = 2^8 3^4 5^2$, so there are at most three such primes.
   - **Pack-coprime:** for each such piece $q$, one Euclid gcd $\gcd(t, q^{\nu})$. A piece may be composite, so a divisibility test cannot decide it.
   - Either way, **$t$ itself is never factored during the arithmetic.**
   - $t = 0$ never reaches a reduction: by lowest terms, a zero sum is $x = -y$, case `zero`. So 0's list is never read. The test build counts reads of an empty list of a zero and requires none.
2. **Storing it factored costs a factoring.** When a cell of a result or an intermediate is stored, its numerator, if new to the matrix, enters (l), and the factoring of §16.4 runs on it at the matrix's pack:
   - Pack-primes: trial division;
   - Pack-coprime: refinement into the base, then pass 2.

   **That cost falls under operation time** (measures 2–5), at the end of the `madd` or `mmul` call that builds the matrix. It is counted per matrix (§16.10).
   - Its denominator, if new, is factored the same way. On the sweep that is trivial: a denominator is $\{2, 3, 5\}$-smooth, so trial division stops after the candidate 5, or sooner.
   - The temporary's own list is **not** copied into the result's (f). The result's factors come only from its own values, as the author's design requires, and for Pack-coprime they must be over the result's own base.
3. **Inputs** are factored the same way, in their build time (measure 1). Their numerators have at most 15 bits: on average about 23 trial-division candidates each.
4. **Temporaries carry their denominator's list, not their numerator's (approved by the author, Oct 30, 2026).**
   - In multiplication, the product $p$ and the running sum $s$ each hold their value and a temporary list (t) of their denominator's factorization. Each case builds it from its operands' lists, by sums, differences and maxima of exponents (the last columns of §16.5).
   - The numerator's list is never needed: no product has a temporary operand (§16.5), and every sum's reductions use denominators only.
   - *Alternative:* temporaries carry nothing, and `add(s, p)` stays on Euclid. That would leave the inner sums, about 70% of TC's gcd work in multiplication, unchanged.

### 16.7 The coprime base across matrices (Pack-coprime)

The base is **per matrix**, as stores are. Two pieces of different matrices may share a factor without being equal: $A$'s 6 and $B$'s 2. A merge compares factors by value, so it needs every pair it compares to be **equal or coprime**. Primes are universal and need nothing.

**Every cross gcd has a denominator side** (§16.0):
- in a sum, $\gcd(b, d)$: $D_L$ against $D_R$;
- in a product, $\gcd(a, d)$ and $\gcd(c, b)$: any factor of one operand against $D$ of the other.

So it is enough that **every piece of each operand be equal or coprime to every denominator factor of the other.**

**Scheme, approved by the author, Oct 30, 2026: reconcile in place, against denominator factors only, at the start of each step.**

```text
function reconcile(L, R)                         first thing in madd or mmul; Pack-coprime only; timed in the operation
    repeat
        make D_L and D_R: the distinct factors in the lists of L's and R's denominators       (r)
            (a scan of each matrix's cells, reading the denominator's entries in (f); duplicates found by a scan of the list so far)
        refine(L, D_R) and refine(R, D_L)
    until a whole pass splits nothing
end function

function refine(X, D)                            every piece q of X becomes equal or coprime to every r in D
    let i = 0
    while i < |D|                                    |D| grows when a split appends pieces of r
        let j = 0
        while j < |q_X|                              |q_X| grows when a split appends pieces of q
            let q = q_X[j], r = D[i], h = gcd(q, r)
            if h = 1 or q = r
                j ← j + 1
            else
                let S be the coprime base of {q, r}, by gcds alone (notation note §4)     scratch
                split q, in every matrix of the step whose base holds q, into its pieces from S
                split r likewise, in every matrix that holds it; D[i] becomes r's first piece,
                    and its other pieces are appended to D                              (§16.8)
                j ← 0                                restart the scan of X from its first piece, against the new D[i]
        i ← i + 1
end function
```

- **Correct:** at the end, every pair that a merge of this step can compare is equal or coprime. A split elsewhere only makes pieces finer, and it is applied wherever the piece occurs by value, so pieces that were equal stay equal. The loop ends because each split strictly refines a finite set.
- **Cost:** at most $\lvert q_L \rvert \lvert D_R \rvert + \lvert q_R \rvert \lvert D_L \rvert$ gcds per pass, plus one rescan of $X$ per split. On the sweep $D$ holds at most three pieces, products of 2, 3 and 5, so this is about $3 \times 10^4$ gcds of small numbers at $n = 64$.
- **The operands are changed:** their bases become finer and their (f) lists are rewritten. Their values, cells and lists (l) are not. Inputs are rebuilt on every run, so nothing carries over between runs.

**The alternative, not chosen:** a temporary full common refinement of both bases:
- a common base, plus each operand's factorizations re-expressed over it: three more temporary structures;
- about $\lvert q_L \rvert \cdot \lvert q_R \rvert$ gcds. For sop's $R = P + Q$ at $n = 64$, that is $1.9 \times 10^7$ gcds in a planning simulation, about 1–2 s per run, more than the rest of that step;
- it buys nothing: the numerators' pieces never meet across matrices except against a denominator.

The in-place scheme above is the one approved.

**Primes** need no reconciliation: Pack-primes has no (q) and no (r).

### 16.8 Refinement when a value splits a base element (Pack-coprime): what is rewritten, and when

| When | Cause | Rewritten | Not touched |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **During a matrix's factoring**, pass 1 (§16.4 item 4) | a whole number $v$ shares part of a piece $q$ | (q) only: $q$'s entry becomes the first new piece, and the others are appended | everything else: no factorization exists yet (pass 2 writes them over the final base); the cells and (l) never change |
| **At the start of a step**, reconciliation (§16.7) | a piece of one operand shares part of a denominator factor of the other | in each matrix of the step that holds the piece: its (q) entry, as above; and its (f), **rebuilt in one pass** into (f′), each entry $(q, \nu)$ replaced by $(r_i, \nu e_i)$ for $q = \prod r_i^{e_i}$, each list re-sorted; then the old (f) is freed | values, (l), cells; matrices not in the step |
| **Inside a step**, a leftover (§16.6 item 1) | $h_q = \gcd(t, q^\nu)$ is not a power of $q$, and the result is a temporary whose list must stay exact (multiplication's sums) | the piece $q$ is split as at reconciliation, in every matrix of the step that holds it, and in the live temporary lists $s$ and $p$ | the same |
| **Never** | | a stored value, a cell, a list (l); any matrix not an operand of the step | |

- **Leftovers in stored results.** A sum whose result is stored, not a temporary, needs no split on a leftover: $h = \prod h_q$ is the right value, and the result's own factoring will find its factors. Examples: an add job's cells, sop's $R = P + Q$, pos's $S$ and $U$.
- **Where the time falls.** Every rewrite during a matrix's factoring falls in that matrix's build (measure 1) or its operation. Every rewrite at or inside a step falls in the operation. All are counted (§16.10).
- **No reverse index:** a rebuild scans the whole (f), as 09's "a split scans every row".
