# Problems that lean on associates, divisibility and (1+i)-adic valuation

Light web survey, November 2026. Question: which well-known or open problems over Z[i] spend most of their time on the operations where the representation x = (1+i)^k · u (u odd) wins: canonical associate, exact division by powers of 1+i or 2, divisibility tests, reading v_{1+i}, zero and parity tests, and unit operations. Sources are linked inline; items marked "not verified" were not reachable through our proxy.

## 1. Framing

Two facts frame everything below.

First, the winning operations are cheap in the ordinary pair (a, b) as well. v_{1+i}(a + bi) = 2·min(ctz a, ctz b) + [ctz a = ctz b] (see our adic-survey.md), division by (1+i)^2 = 2i is a shift and a rotation, and canonical associate is a few compares and swaps. Our gains are constant factors of 16 to 40 percent on operations that are already a handful of instructions. They matter only where those operations are the inner loop and nothing heavier (primality testing, factoring, FFT multiplication, graph search) sits beside them.

Second, most famous Z[i] computations are dominated by something heavier. Prime generation and primality dominate moat, walk and Goldbach work; factoring dominates divisor-function work; huge FFT squarings dominate Mersenne-type searches. The honest fit list is short.

## 2. Gaussian moat (Erdős #952)

Problem. Can one walk from the origin to infinity on Gaussian primes with bounded step length? Posed by Basil Gordon in 1962, attributed by Erdős to Motzkin, who expected the answer to be no. Open: [erdosproblems.com/952](https://www.erdosproblems.com/forum/thread/952), [MathWorld](https://mathworld.wolfram.com/GaussianMoatProblem.html). A 2024 claimed proof was withdrawn because "the width of the moat was not correctly computed" ([arXiv:2401.08441](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.08441v2)).

Records. Jordan and Rabung (1970) showed step length at least 4 is needed; Gethner, Wagon and Wick ([A Stroll Through the Gaussian Primes](https://maa.org/programs/maa-awards/writing-awards/a-stroll-through-the-gaussian-primes), Monthly 1998) found a moat of width √26 using Mathematica. Tsuchimura ([METR 2004-13](https://www.keisu.t.u-tokyo.ac.jp/data/2004/METR04-13.pdf); IEICE Trans. Fundamentals 88(5), 2005) is still the record:

| k² | farthest reachable prime | component size (first octant) |
|---|---|---|
| 26 | 1,015,638.8 | 1.45e10 |
| 32 | 2,823,054.5 | 1.04e11 |
| 34 | below 24,289,452 (bound only) | unknown |
| 36 | below 80,015,782 (bound only) | unknown |

He used Java and C on 38 Pentium III CPUs; k² = 32 took about 80 hours. Newer open-source code exists, such as Engberg's [gaussianprimes](https://pypi.org/project/gaussianprimes) (C++ wheel sieve with segmented moat classes, 2021), but I found no newer record. Note that the √36 result is an upper bound on the reach, not an exact farthest point.

Bottleneck. Tsuchimura says prime generation was the bottleneck until he parallelised it, after which "connectivity testing ... is now the bottleneck". Neither is an associate, divisibility or valuation operation. Primes are generated by sieving, and sieves avoid divisibility tests by construction. The first-octant restriction already handles associates through coordinates.

Fit. Low. A faster prime generator and union-find on modern hardware could plausibly extend the record (exact reach for k² = 34 or 36, or a first look at k² = 40), which would be an original computational result, but our representation contributes nothing to it.

## 3. Counting and sieving Gaussian primes

Problem. π_G(x), the number of Gaussian primes of norm up to x counted once per associate class (OEIS A091100, not fetched), and its error term; prime races between residue classes or sectors (Hecke angular equidistribution). The counting function itself is not an open problem in the way the rational one is: by splitting, π_G(x) is determined by π(x), π(x; 4, 1) and π(√x; 4, 3), so it can be read off rational prime counts to 10^25 and beyond. Sector and angular statistics are where genuine Gaussian computation happens. A recent experimental paper ([Cai, Diehl, Gasarch et al., arXiv:2510.15255](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.15255), 2025) counted only to r² = 10^7 and restricts to a, b ≥ 0 "to avoid counting each prime multiple times because of units".

Bottleneck. Sieving (additive lattice enumeration) or splitting rational primes p = a² + b² (Cornacchia or Hermite-Serret). Canonical associate is applied once per output prime.

Fit. Low. Associate normalisation is a per-output cost, not a per-candidate one.

## 4. Prime walks on lines and spirals

Lines. Magness, Nugent and Robertson ([arXiv:2001.05018](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.05018), 2020) study Gaussian primes on lines α₀ + nβ; whether any primitive line with α₀ ≠ 0 has infinitely many primes is open (related to Landau's n² + 1 problem). They verified a weak Bertrand-type conjecture with Sage on about 700,000 lines, 10^10 points each.

Spirals. The Gaussian prime spiral (move straight, turn left at each Gaussian prime) is conjectured always to close into a cycle; unproved ([Burkardt notes](https://people.sc.fsu.edu/~jburkardt/classes/python_2025/gaussian_primes/gaussian_primes.html), [Wolfram demonstration](https://demonstrations.wolfram.com/GaussianPrimeSpirals/)). No serious computational record found.

Bottleneck. Primality of each visited point (a norm primality test). Not ours. Fit: none.

## 5. Gaussian Goldbach (Holben and Jordan, 1968)

Problem. Every even Gaussian integer (divisible by 1+i, under the number-field definition) of norm above a small bound is a sum of two Gaussian primes, with variants restricting the arguments of the summands. Open; recent analytic work toward a Chen-type statement: [Banerjee and Rahaman, arXiv:2406.16311](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.16311v1). I found no documented numerical verification bound comparable to the rational 4·10^18.

Bottleneck. Prime-table lookups. The evenness test is a (1+i) divisibility test, but it is done once per target. Fit: low, though a verification bound would be a small original result regardless of representation.

## 6. Gaussian Mersenne primes

Problem. Primes of the form (1±i)^p − 1, equivalently primes among their norms 2^p ± 2^((p+1)/2) + 1. Infinitude is open. [OEIS A057429](https://oeis.org/A057429) lists 41 exponents up to 15,317,227 (Propper and Batalov, 2023); the search below 5,000,000 is exhaustive (Batalov, 2014). Work is coordinated on MersenneForum; norms are generalized unique primes tested with LLR or PRP. Berrizbeitia and Iskra give a Lucas-Lehmer-type test for them ([MathWorld](https://mathworld.wolfram.com/GaussianMersennePrime.html)).

Bottleneck. Million-digit FFT squarings on rational integers. Fit: none. Our representation does give (1+i)^p − 1 trivially, but that is never the cost.

## 7. Perfect, multiperfect, amicable Gaussian integers and aliquot sequences

Definitions. Spira (Amer. Math. Monthly 68, 1961) defined σ(η) = Π (π^(k+1) − 1)/(π − 1) over the prime factors π normalised to the first quadrant; the unit is dropped. η is perfect if σ(η) = (1+i)η, norm-perfect if N(σ(η)) = 2N(η) ([MathWorld](https://mathworld.wolfram.com/PerfectGaussianInteger.html)).

Known. McDaniel (Acta Arith. 25, 1974) proved even primitive perfect Gaussian integers are −i(1+i)^(p−1)·M_p with M_p a Gaussian Mersenne prime, p ≡ 1 mod 8 (smallest p = 73). Ward ([arXiv:0805.2092](https://arxiv.org/abs/0805.2092), 2008) showed odd norm-perfect ones have the form π^k γ² with k odd. Whether odd perfect Gaussian integers exist is open. A 2026 manuscript (Sanchez Solovieva, cited on MathWorld, unpublished) reports a perfect number under Pegg's alternative convention that is not perfect under Spira's. Tables: norm-multiperfect [A332318](https://oeis.org/A332318) (Eldar, 182 terms), multiperfect A102506, amicable [A102924](https://oeis.org/A102924) (Noe, 2005, 16 terms, smallest first-quadrant 8008 + 3960i), and a master's thesis ([Clark, EKU 2013](https://encompass.eku.edu/etd/158)) on amicable pairs.

Aliquot sequences. I found no study of Gaussian aliquot sequences z → s(z) = σ(z) − z or of Gaussian sociable cycles beyond amicable pairs. Rational aliquot work (for example [Varona's pages](https://www.unirioja.es/cu/jvarona/aliquot.html)) does not touch Z[i]. One complication is that Spira's σ is not associate-invariant in the obvious way (σ(z) ignores the unit of z, but s(z) subtracts z itself), so there are two natural maps: s on raw z, or s on the canonical associate of z each step. Both are untabulated as far as I can see.

Bottleneck. Factoring N(z) over Z, then normalising each prime factor and splitting off (1+i)^k. The canonical-associate and valuation operations appear several times per step but factoring dominates by orders of magnitude once norms pass about 10^12. At small norms (sieved factor tables), the per-step overhead of normalisation and σ evaluation is a meaningful share.

Fit. Moderate at small norms, low at large norms. Chance of original results: good, because the territory is thin.

## 8. Collatz-type maps and (1+i)-adic dynamics

Literature. Joseph ([Fibonacci Quarterly 36(4), 1998](https://www.mathstat.dal.ca/FQ/Scanned/36-4/joseph.pdf)) extends 3x+1 to Z₂[i], branching on the four classes mod 2 and dividing by 2; he proves the map is chaotic and topologically conjugate to T × T. It is purely theoretical, with no computations. Matthews-style generalized maps (search for cycles: [arXiv:1907.13302](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.13302); Lagarias overview [arXiv:2111.02635](https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.02635)) are over Z. I found no published systematic computation of Collatz-type maps on Z[i] that branch on divisibility by 1+i.

A heuristic worth stating, since it shapes any experiment. For the naive map "z/(1+i) if divisible, else αz + β", with an odd step followed by a run of v divisions (v geometric with mean 2), the log-drift is ln|α| − ln 2, so convergence needs N(α) < 4. The only such odd α are units, so every non-trivial naive Gaussian 3x+1 diverges heuristically (Joseph's map with α = 3 also has positive drift: (3/4)ln(3/2) − (1/4)ln 2 > 0). Contracting examples need finer branching, for example the Matthews form T(z) = (m_r z − r)/2 on the four classes r mod 2 with m_0 = 1 and m_r = α for the other three; the drift is (3/4)ln|α| − ln 2, negative for N(α) = 5 (α = 2 + i or 1 + 2i). Similar designs work modulo (1+i)^3 or (1+i)^4. These have real cycles to find and a convergence question to test.

Bottleneck. Per step: read the class mod (1+i)^d (low bits), multiply by a small constant, add, then exact division by (1+i)^v or 2. This is the closest match to our winning set of any problem here. But the odd step needs an add, which our representation does slower, and on odd values k = 0 so the representation is just (a, b) plus a valuation recompute. A careful accounting might show only a few percent net either way.

Related numeration. Base −1+i (an associate of 1+i) gives every Gaussian integer a unique 0/1 digit expansion (Knuth; the twindragon). Digit extraction is repeated exact division by 1+i after a parity test. It is a well-understood tool, not an open problem, but it is a clean benchmark for our division path.

Fit. High on paper, modest in practice. Originality: high, as the area is nearly empty.

## 9. Sums of two squares, r2(n), divisor functions

r2(n) = 4 Σ_{d|n} χ(d) is read off the factorisation of n; the summatory problem (Gauss circle) is lattice counting, and record computations of the error term are rational and analytic. Erdős problems on sums of two squares ([tag page](https://www.erdosproblems.com/tags/squares)) are rational questions about the set of norms. Computing an explicit representation n = a² + b² uses a modular square root and Cornacchia, or a Gaussian gcd. Fit: low, apart from the gcd step in section 10.

## 10. (1+i)-ary gcd and the quartic residue symbol (a tool, not an open problem)

Weilert's (1+i)-ary gcd (J. Symbolic Comput. 30, 2000, not verified) and the Damgård-Frandsen binary gcd and residuosity algorithms ([BRICS RS-03-8](https://brics.dk/RS/03/8), 2003, which states the method gives equally fast gcd and quartic residuosity in Z[i]) are made almost entirely of our winning operations: strip (1+i)^k, normalise to a primary associate, subtract, repeat. FLINT ships one (fmpzi_gcd_binary). This is not a research problem, but it sits inside several of the computations above (splitting p into a² + b², quartic residue tests used by Berrizbeitia-Iskra-type primality proofs). It is the strongest place to show the representation pays off end to end.

| walks on lines, spirals | yes | primality per point | no |
| Gaussian Goldbach | yes | prime lookups | no |
| Gaussian Mersenne primes | yes | FFT squaring | no |
| perfect, amicable, aliquot | yes | factoring; normalisation at small norms | partly |
| Collatz-type maps on Z[i] | barely studied | valuation, exact division, small mul and add | yes, modestly |
| (1+i)-ary gcd, quartic symbols | tool | valuation, associates, subtraction | yes |

## 12. Candidates

Ranked by fit with the representation first, then chance of an original result.

1. Contracting Collatz-type maps on Z[i]. Take the four-class map T(z) = z/2 on class 0 and (αz + β_r)/2 on classes r ∈ {1, i, 1+i} mod 2, with N(α) = 5 and β_r ≡ −αr mod 2, and the analogous map modulo (1+i)^3. First experiment: iterate from every z with N(z) ≤ 10^8, record all cycles, stopping times and any apparent divergence, and compare against the drift heuristic of section 8. Time the inner loop in both representations; that comparison is itself a clean benchmark result.

2. Gaussian aliquot sequences and sociable cycles. First experiment: for all z with N(z) ≤ 10^6 (sieved factor table of norms), iterate both the raw map z → σ(z) − z and the associate-normalised map, classify the endings (0 or unit, prime, perfect, amicable, longer cycle, escape past a bound), and check the amicable list against A102924. Any sociable cycle of length at least 3 would likely be new. The associate and valuation ops appear per step here; their share should be measured, not assumed.

3. Odd perfect and norm-perfect Gaussian integers. First experiment: a bounded search restricted to Ward's form π^k γ² (k odd), testing N(σ(η)) = 2N(η) first and σ(η) = (1+i)η second, plus tabulating even norm-perfect forms not covered by McDaniel. The odd-perfect question is open; a nonexistence bound in the style of the rational 10^1500 bound would be new, though factoring, not our layer, sets the pace.

4. Gaussian moat, exact reach for k² = 34 and 36. Highest profile and a plausible original result on modern hardware (Tsuchimura's record is 20 years old and gives only bounds there). Our representation does not help; the work would be a batched prime generator plus union-find. First experiment: reproduce k² = 26 (farthest point at distance 1,015,638.8) and time it.

5. Gaussian Goldbach verification. First experiment: verify every even Gaussian integer of norm up to 10^10 is a sum of two Gaussian primes, using a sieved prime bitmap, and record the hardest cases. Likely original as a published bound, but representation-neutral.

The (1+i)-ary gcd of section 10 is not a problem, but it is the best showcase of the layer and feeds candidates 2 and 3 indirectly through prime splitting.
