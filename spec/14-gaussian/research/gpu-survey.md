# Gaussian integers on GPUs: survey for the GPU check

Scope: how NVIDIA and AMD GPUs do the integer operations our type uses (32x32->64 products, carries, overflow masks, Barrett, exact division, count trailing zeros), how a sticky overflow flag is combined across threads, divergence in a data-dependent gcd loop, memory layout, existing exact-integer GPU libraries, and how to count instructions and test correctness with no GPU. Web survey only, November 2026. The CUDA Programming Guide page itself was not on our proxy allowlist; figures attributed to it below are from its throughput table as we know it ([anchor](https://docs.nvidia.com/cuda/cuda-c-programming-guide/index.html#arithmetic-instructions)) and are marked "recalled, verify". Everything else was read online.

## 1. NVIDIA: 32-bit and 64-bit integer arithmetic

### 1.1 PTX level

PTX (the virtual ISA that clang's nvptx64 backend emits) has the operations we need directly: `mul.lo`, `mul.hi` and `mul.wide` (32x32->64, signed and unsigned), `mad.lo/hi/wide`, and an explicit carry chain: `add.cc`, `addc`, `sub.cc`, `subc`, `mad.cc`, `madc` set and consume a single carry flag ([PTX ISA, integer and extended-precision arithmetic](https://docs.nvidia.com/cuda/parallel-thread-execution/index.html#extended-precision-integer-arithmetic-instructions)). CUDA C exposes the high halves as `__umulhi`, `__mulhi`, `__umul64hi`, and clang turns `(uint64_t)a * b` with 32-bit operands into `mul.wide.u32`. There is no signed-overflow flag at PTX or SASS level; the carry is unsigned. So our mask identity ((a XOR r) AND (b XOR r), sign bit) stays the way to detect signed overflow on GPUs too.

PTX is not what runs. ptxas lowers it to SASS, and "many PTX instructions are implemented as sequences of SASS instructions, some short, some lengthy", so PTX-level instruction counting is "largely meaningless"; compare SASS with `cuobjdump --dump-sass` instead (njuffa, [NVIDIA forum, long-integer multiplication](https://forums.developer.nvidia.com/t/long-integer-multiplication-mul-wide-u64-and-mul-wide-u128/51520/5)).

### 1.2 SASS level

On sm_70 and later (Volta through Ada, Hopper, Blackwell) the relevant SASS instructions are:

| Operation | SASS | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 32-bit add, 3-input add, add with carry | `IADD3`, `IADD3.X` | carry in and out travel in predicate registers |
| 32x32 low, high, full product (+ 64-bit addend) | `IMAD`, `IMAD.HI.U32`, `IMAD.WIDE(.U32)` | full 32-bit multiplier restored in sm_70 |
| any 3-input bitwise function | `LOP3.LUT` | (a^r)&(b^r) is one LOP3 |
| funnel and 64-bit shifts | `SHF.L/R(.W)` | |
| leading one, bit reverse, popcount | `FLO`, `BREV`, `POPC` | lower-throughput pipe |

njuffa describes `IMAD.WIDE.U32` as computing "the full product of two 32-bit operands, adds the result to a 64-bit operand", notes it "cannot be expressed directly at PTX level" (ptxas forms it from `mul.wide`/`mad.wide`), that "only the IMAD.LO.U32 can receive a carry, and only the IMAD.HI.U32 can produce a carry", and that other carry patterns need an extra `IADD3`. He also says "full 32-bit IMAD hardware was restored with the sm_70 architecture (Volta)" and that multi-word multiply on modern GPUs "is limited by dynamic instruction count" ([forum thread on a 256-bit modular kernel, sm_89](https://forums.developer.nvidia.com/t/squeezing-the-last-17-5-out-of-a-compute-bound-256-bit-modular-arithmetic-kernel-sm-89-82-5-sm-throughput/367788)). Maxwell and Pascal (sm_50 to sm_61) had only a 16x16 `XMAD`, which "re-uses the hardware of the single-precision floating-point multipliers", so a 32-bit multiply took about three instructions there ([same forum, XMAD](https://forums.developer.nvidia.com/t/long-integer-multiplication-mul-wide-u64-and-mul-wide-u128/51520/5); [XMAD meaning](https://forums.developer.nvidia.com/t/xmad-meaning/46653)). We should target sm_70 or later and ignore XMAD.

### 1.3 64-bit integers

There is no 64-bit integer ALU. A 64-bit add or subtract is two `IADD3` (one with `.X`), a 64-bit compare is two instructions, a 64-bit low product `mul.lo.u64` is one `IMAD.WIDE.U32` plus two `IMAD` for the cross terms, and `mul.hi.u64` (needed for a 64-bit Barrett) is a longer sequence of roughly eight to ten instructions. In the sm_89 thread above, one 64-bit PTX building block became 38 SASS compute instructions against 15 for its 32-bit version, and "ptxas does not exploit ILP across independent carry chains on sm_89". Our int64-halves tier will therefore cost roughly 3x to 4x the int32 tier per multiply on NVIDIA, more for anything involving 64-bit high products or division. That ratio is the main thing to measure.

### 1.4 Throughput relative to float

From the CUDA Programming Guide throughput table (recalled, verify): on compute capability 7.x and 8.0, 32-bit integer add, multiply, multiply-add, shift, compare and bitwise ops all run at 64 results per clock per SM, the same as FP32. On 8.6, 8.9 and 9.0, FP32 doubles to 128 while 32-bit integer stays at 64, so integer multiply is half the FP32 rate on consumer Ampere, Ada and on Hopper. Count leading zeros and popcount run at 16 per clock per SM (a quarter of IMAD), warp shuffle at 32. 64-bit integer operations are listed as "multiple instructions". Integer adds and logic can issue alongside FP work on sm_70+ (separate INT32 and FP32 pipes), but our kernels have no float work to overlap with. FP64 is irrelevant to us (2 to 4 per clock per SM on consumer parts, per [this forum thread](https://forums.developer.nvidia.com/t/relations-between-instruction-throughput-and-cuda-compute-capability/239005)).

### 1.5 Count leading and trailing zeros

PTX has `clz`, `brev`, `bfind` (index of most significant one) and `popc`; CUDA has `__clz`, `__clzll`, `__ffs`, `__ffsll`, `__brev`, `__popc` ([libdevice `__nv_clz`](https://docs.nvidia.com/cuda/libdevice-users-guide/__nv_clz.html)). There is no direct count-trailing-zeros instruction: ctz(x) is `__ffs(x) - 1` or `__clz(__brev(x))`, which in SASS is `BREV` followed by `FLO` (plus a fix-up), or `POPC((x & -x) - 1)`. Edge cases differ from C: `__clz(0)` is 32 and `__ffs(0)` is 0, whereas `__builtin_ctz(0)` is undefined. Both `FLO` and `BREV` sit on the quarter-rate pipe, so the ctz in our (1+i)-ary gcd costs about two quarter-rate instructions per step on NVIDIA.

### 1.6 Integer division

"NVIDIA GPUs have no hardware support for divisions. All division operations are emulated" (njuffa, [forum](https://forums.developer.nvidia.com/t/is-it-possible-to-replace-integer-division-by-floating-point-division-for-speed/209183)). Unsigned 32-bit division on Ampere is "an inlined sequence of 17 instructions" (7 of them IMAD, about 9 registers), signed costs more; the sequence starts from a float reciprocal `MUFU.RCP` ("a table-lookup plus interpolation on an internal ROM table") and corrects with integer multiplies. 64-bit division is a called subroutine of several dozen instructions; LLVM's NVPTX backend also inserts a "bypass slow division" check that uses the 32-bit path when both 64-bit operands fit in 32 bits (from LLVM's NVPTX target setup as we know it; confirm in our generated PTX). Division by a constant is turned into multiply-high and shift as on CPUs. Our own methods (multiply by the inverse mod 2^32 for exact division, precomputed reciprocal with correction for division with remainder) avoid all of this and map to `IMAD`/`IMAD.HI`, which is where GPUs are strongest.

## 2. AMD: GCN, CDNA and RDNA

The vector ALU has 32-bit `v_add_co_u32`/`v_addc_co_u32` and `v_sub_co_u32`/`v_subb_co_u32`, whose carry is a per-lane bit mask held in `VCC` or an SGPR pair (64 bits in wave64, 32 in wave32), plus `v_add3_u32`, `v_mul_lo_u32`, `v_mul_hi_u32`/`v_mul_hi_i32`, `v_mad_u64_u32`/`v_mad_i64_i32` (32x32+64 -> 64, gfx8 and later), the 24-bit `v_mul_u32_u24`/`v_mad_u32_u24`, and 3-input logic `v_xor3_b32`, `v_and_or_b32`, `v_or3_b32` (gfx9/10 and later). Leading and trailing zeros are `v_ffbh_u32` and `v_ffbl_b32` (renamed `v_clz_i32_u32` and `v_ctz_i32_b32` in RDNA3), with `v_bfrev_b32` and `v_bcnt_u32_b32`; `v_ffbl_b32` gives ctz directly and returns -1 for zero ([RDNA3 ISA guide](https://gpuopen.com/rdna3-isa-guide-now-available/), [PDF](https://docs.amd.com/v/u/en-US/rdna3-shader-instruction-set-architecture-feb-2023_0); LLVM's [VOP3 instruction definitions](https://llvm.googlesource.com/llvm-project/+/890c4bece26e005cd9fa5511fe0efa7307794de5/llvm/lib/Target/AMDGPU/VOP3Instructions.td)). There is no 64-bit integer multiply before gfx1250, for which LLVM added `v_add_u64`, `v_sub_u64` and `v_mul_u64` in 2025 ([llvm-commits](https://lists.llvm.org/pipermail/llvm-commits/Week-of-Mon-20250721/1688209.html)).

Throughput on GCN (CLRX measurements): adds, logic, `v_ffbh_u32`, `v_bfrev_b32` and the 24-bit `v_mad_u32_u24` are full rate, while `v_mul_lo_u32`, `v_mul_hi_u32` and `v_mad_u64_u32` are quarter rate (16 cycles against 4); 64-bit shifts run at the FP64 rate, which is 1/8 on many consumer GCN parts ([CLRX GcnTimings](https://mygithub.libinneed.workers.dev/CLRX/CLRX-mirror/wiki/GcnTimings)). We did not find an authoritative RDNA or CDNA integer-multiply rate; we expect 32-bit multiply to remain quarter rate on RDNA. So on AMD every 32x32->64 product is a quarter-rate instruction, while ctz is cheap: the opposite of NVIDIA's balance.

Integer division on AMDGPU is expanded inline by LLVM: 32-bit uses `v_rcp_iflag_f32` (float reciprocal) followed by `v_mul_hi_u32` and two correction steps, about 15 to 20 instructions; 64-bit is a much longer inline sequence, believed to run to over 100 instructions, with a 32-bit bypass (from LLVM's AMDGPU lowering as we know it; not verified online, so measure it in our own output). Again our multiply-based methods avoid it.

## 3. Combining a sticky overflow flag

Per thread, the flag should stay in a register and be ORed in the loop, exactly as on the CPU. On NVIDIA `flag |= (a ^ r) & (b ^ r)` is two `LOP3` (one computes the 3-input mask, the other ORs it in), on AMD three VALU ops or fewer with `v_and_or_b32`. The sign bit is extracted once at the end.

Across threads, the standard tools are warp vote and atomics ([NVIDIA blog, Using CUDA Warp-Level Primitives](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/using-cuda-warp-level-primitives/)). `__any_sync(0xffffffff, flag < 0)` is one `VOTE.ANY` and returns the same answer to every lane; `__ballot_sync` returns the 32-bit mask of lanes that overflowed, which also identifies which elements need redoing; `__reduce_or_sync` (sm_80+) reduces an integer across the warp in one `REDUX`. HIP has `__any` and `__ballot` (64-bit in wave64). For the whole grid, the cheapest pattern is: each thread ORs into its register for its whole grid-stride loop, then one vote per warp, then lane 0 of a warp whose vote is true does one `atomicOr` (or a relaxed atomic store of 1) to a global word that was zeroed before launch. Since overflow is rare, almost no atomics execute, and the vote plus a predicated branch cost a handful of instructions per warp per kernel. A shared-memory block reduction adds a `__syncthreads` and gains nothing here. The host reads the word after the kernel completes; this device-to-host round trip (microseconds) is the real cost of "check once per array", and it matters only for small arrays or many launches. If the flag must stop work early, threads can poll the global word between chunks, but for a rare event that is not worth it.

If we want to redo only the failing elements rather than the whole array, the known pattern is warp-aggregated atomics: `__ballot_sync` to get the mask, `__popc` for the count, one `atomicAdd` per warp to reserve slots in an index list, and `__popc(mask & lanemask_lt)` for each lane's slot ([NVIDIA blog, warp-aggregated atomics](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/cuda-pro-tip-optimized-filtering-warp-aggregated-atomics/)).

## 4. Divergence and gcd loops

A warp (32 lanes on NVIDIA, 32 or 64 on AMD) executes both sides of a branch that its lanes disagree on, with inactive lanes masked, and a loop with a data-dependent trip count runs until its slowest lane finishes. Volta's independent thread scheduling lets diverged lanes interleave but does not remove the serialisation cost; it is the reason the `_sync` variants of vote and shuffle exist ([NVIDIA blog](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/using-cuda-warp-level-primitives/)). Short branches are predicated by the compiler, so a select-style body (`r = cond ? x : y`) costs both sides but never diverges. The CUDA Best Practices Guide covers this in "Branching and Divergence" and "Branch Predication" ([contents](https://docs.nvidia.com/cuda/cuda-c-best-practices-guide/contents.html)).

For gcd specifically, Fujita, Nakano and Ito ("Bulk GCD Computation Using a GPU to Break Weak RSA Keys", IPDPSW 2015, [PDF](https://www.cs.hiroshima-u.ac.jp/cs/_media/apdcm15gcd.pdf)) run one gcd per thread, store operands column-wise so that loads coalesce, and attribute binary Euclid's poor GPU scaling to its three-way if-else branch. Their "approximate Euclidean" algorithm has one dominant path taken with probability above 1 - 10^-8, which keeps warps converged, and is about 8.5x faster than binary Euclid on the GPU against only 1.7x on the CPU. The lesson: on GPUs a loop body with one path matters more than the iteration count. For our (1+i)-ary gcd, that means writing the body branch-free (compute ctz, shift, conditional swap and subtract with selects), so the warp cost is the maximum iteration count over 32 lanes rather than the sum of distinct paths. If the spread of iteration counts is large, the fully fixed-count alternative is a constant-time divstep gcd in the style of Bernstein and Yang ("Fast constant-time gcd computation and modular inversion", TCHES 2019, [ePrint 2019/266](https://eprint.iacr.org/2019/266)), which removes divergence entirely at the price of a worst-case iteration count. Our existing gcd survey (gcd-survey.md) gives the candidate algorithms.

## 5. Memory layout

Structure of arrays is the standard GPU recommendation: consecutive threads reading consecutive 4-byte words form one coalesced transaction per warp per array ([Best Practices Guide, "Coalesced Access to Global Memory"](https://docs.nvidia.com/cuda/cuda-c-best-practices-guide/contents.html)). Our separate real and imaginary arrays coalesce perfectly; the int64 tier coalesces as 8-byte words. An interleaved `int2` layout with 8-byte vector loads would coalesce equally well, so SoA is not required for bandwidth, but it keeps the int32 and int64 tiers uniform and matches the NEON design. Our kernels are elementwise and compute-light (a Gaussian multiply is about 4 to 8 integer instructions per 16 bytes moved), so most of them will be memory-bound on a GPU and instruction counts will matter mainly for mod, division and gcd.

## 6. Exact integer and bignum libraries on GPUs

| Library or paper | What | Overflow and carries |
|---|---|---|
| [CGBN](https://github.com/NVlabs/CGBN) (NVlabs, "XMP 2.0", 2018) | fixed-size unsigned bignums, 32 to 32K bits, 4 to 32 threads per number | carries propagated across the thread group with PTX carry chains and warp shuffles; add and subtract return the carry or borrow out (API as we recall it) |
| [XMP 1.0](https://nvlabs.github.io/xmp) (NVlabs) | batched multiple-precision arithmetic, modular exponentiation | fixed sizes chosen by the caller |
| Emmart, [PhD thesis, UMass 2017](https://web.cs.umass.edu/publication/docs/2017/UM-CS-PhD-2017-006.pdf); Emmart and Weems, "Optimizing Modular Multiplication for NVIDIA's Maxwell GPUs" (ARITH 2016) | XMAD-era multiply, also FP64-based modular exponentiation | none needed; sizes fixed |
| Fujita, Nakano, Ito (IPDPSW 2015, above) | bulk 1024-bit gcd | fixed-size words |
| Oancea et al., DIKU, [arXiv:2405.14642](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.14642) | big-integer arithmetic in Futhark and CUDA | fixed sizes |
| GPU FHE and NTT: Jung et al., [arXiv:2012.01968](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.01968) ("100x" bootstrapping); [arXiv:2501.07535](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.07535) (multi-word modular code generation); cuFHE | NTT and RNS arithmetic with 32- or 64-bit word moduli | no runtime detection: moduli are bounded (for example below 2^30 or 2^62) so Barrett/Montgomery/Shoup and lazy reduction provably never overflow |

We found no GPU library for Gaussian integers, and none that detects overflow at run time and promotes. GPU libraries either fix the width up front (bignum) or bound the inputs so overflow cannot happen (NTT/FHE). Our detect-and-redo scheme has no direct precedent; the closest analogue is the CGBN carry-out return value.

## 7. Counting instructions and testing without a GPU

Compiling. clang can compile CUDA device code with no GPU and no CUDA runtime for codegen: `clang++ -x cuda --cuda-device-only --cuda-gpu-arch=sm_89 -nocudainc -nocudalib -S` emits PTX ([Compiling CUDA with clang](https://llvm.org/docs/CompileCudaWithLLVM.html)); plain C with `--target=nvptx64-nvidia-cuda -march=sm_89` also works. For AMD, `clang --target=amdgcn-amd-amdhsa -mcpu=gfx1100` (RDNA3), `gfx90a`/`gfx942` (CDNA) or `gfx906` (GCN) with `-S` emits the real ISA directly, since there is no virtual layer ([AMDGPU usage](https://llvm.org/docs/AMDGPUUsage.html)); `-Rpass-analysis=kernel-resource-usage` reports VGPR and SGPR counts.

NVIDIA tools. `ptxas` ships in the pip wheel [nvidia-cuda-nvcc](https://pypi.org/project/nvidia-cuda-nvcc) and `nvdisasm` in [nvidia-cuda-nvdisasm](https://pypi.org/project/nvidia-cuda-nvdisasm/), so no full toolkit install is needed. `ptxas -arch=sm_89 -v` gives register counts; `cuobjdump -sass` or `nvdisasm` on the cubin gives SASS ([CUDA Binary Utilities](https://docs.nvidia.com/cuda/pdf/CUDA_Binary_Utilities.pdf)). Compiler Explorer (godbolt.org) also shows SASS for nvcc and ISA for amdgcn. SASS opcodes are documented only by name, with no official latencies; microbenchmark papers (Jia et al., "Dissecting the NVIDIA Volta GPU Architecture via Microbenchmarking", [arXiv:1804.06826](https://arxiv.org/abs/1804.06826), and the Turing follow-up) give latencies and rates.

AMD tools. `llvm-objdump -d` or `llvm-mc --disassemble` on the code object; `llvm-mca -mtriple=amdgcn -mcpu=gfx...` gives a static throughput estimate from LLVM's AMDGPU scheduling models (which LLVM maintains per generation, for example [gfx950](https://lists.llvm.org/pipermail/all-commits/Week-of-Mon-20241118/190567.html)); AMD's [Radeon GPU Analyzer](https://github.com/GPUOpen-Tools/RGA) compiles offline for any target and reports ISA and register use. Note that llvm-mca models a single wave's straight-line code, not occupancy or quarter-rate pipes shared across waves, so its numbers are a lower bound.

Weighting. A raw instruction count misleads on GPUs because rates differ: a fair static cost is a weighted count, for example IADD3/LOP3/SHF = 1, IMAD = 1 (2 on sm_86/89/90 relative to FP32, irrelevant for us), FLO/BREV/POPC = 4 on NVIDIA; on GCN v_mul_*/v_mad_u64_u32 = 4, adds/logic/ffbl = 1.

Correctness without a GPU. The simplest route is to write each kernel body as a `__host__ __device__` function (or a plain C function compiled for both targets) taking one element index, and run it on the CPU over the whole array; the warp-level parts (vote, ballot, aggregated atomics) are emulated by a CPU loop over groups of 32 lanes that computes the same masks. Heavier options: [GPGPU-Sim / Accel-Sim](https://accel-sim.github.io/) functionally simulate PTX on a CPU (slow, but runs the PTX we generated); [chipStar](https://github.com/CHIP-SPV/chipStar/releases/tag/v1.0) runs HIP and CUDA through SPIR-V on OpenCL, including PoCL on a CPU; [hipCPU](https://github.com/illuhad/hipCPU) is a header-only HIP-on-CPU shim. None of these exercises ptxas's SASS, so a ptxas bug would go unseen, but that risk is small. Semantic traps when sharing code between CPU and GPU: shift counts of 32 or more (PTX `shl` clamps, x86 masks, C leaves it undefined), `__clz(0)`/`__ffs(0)` versus `__builtin_clz(0)`, division by zero, and signed overflow in C (undefined on both, so the mask identity must be computed on unsigned values, as on the CPU).

## Plan for our GPU check

What to compile. The int32-halves and int64-halves versions of: add/sub with overflow mask, multiply (four 32x32->64 products, combine, fits-in-32 check), Barrett mod n, exact division by an odd constant via inverse mod 2^32, division with remainder via reciprocal and correction, and the (1+i)-ary gcd. Each as a small elementwise kernel over SoA arrays with the sticky flag and a warp vote plus one atomicOr at the end. Targets: nvptx64 at sm_70, sm_89 and sm_90 through ptxas to SASS; amdgcn at gfx906 (GCN), gfx90a or gfx942 (CDNA) and gfx1100 (RDNA3). Also compile `/` and `%` on int32 and int64 as baselines, to show what our multiply-based division saves.

What to count. Per element, in the loop body only: on NVIDIA, IMAD (all forms, noting .WIDE and .HI), IADD3, LOP3, SHF, ISETP/SEL, FLO, BREV, POPC, MUFU, branches (BRA, BSSY/BSYNC mark divergence), and registers from `ptxas -v`; on AMD, v_mul_*/v_mad_u64_u32, v_add/addc, logic, v_ffb*/v_ctz, s_cbranch, VGPRs. Report a raw count and a rate-weighted count (section 7), and the int64/int32 ratio per operation. For gcd, also count instructions per iteration and check that the body compiles without divergent branches (no BSSY on NVIDIA, no exec-mask save/restore on AMD).

How to test correctness. Compile the same kernel bodies for the host and run them against our existing CPU reference on the existing test vectors, including the overflow edge cases; emulate the warp vote and ballot with a 32-lane CPU loop; optionally run the PTX once under Accel-Sim or chipStar/PoCL if the host-compiled path leaves doubt.

Design decisions that look likely to change on GPUs.

1. The int64 tier is relatively more expensive: 64-bit products and high products are emulated (roughly 3x to 4x the int32 cost, more for 64-bit Barrett), and on AMD every 32-bit product is already quarter rate. Redoing the whole array at int64 on a rare overflow is still fine, but if the overflow-frequency data show that failures are sparse, redoing only failing elements via a ballot-compacted index list (section 3) may beat a full redo.
2. "Check once per array" becomes one device-to-host round trip per launch. Keep the flag in a register per thread, combine with one vote per warp and at most one atomic per warp; avoid shared-memory block reductions. For many small arrays, fuse launches or check the flag on the device.
3. The mask identity stays (GPUs have no signed-overflow flag), and it is cheaper than on the CPU: one LOP3 plus one OR on NVIDIA.
4. Count trailing zeros flips cost: quarter rate (BREV plus FLO) on NVIDIA, full rate and direct (v_ffbl_b32) on AMD. The gcd should use ctz as rarely as possible on NVIDIA, for example one ctz per step and shift by it, not bit-at-a-time loops.
5. The gcd loop body must be branch-free (selects, not ifs); per-thread branching that is fine on CPUs costs both paths on GPUs. If iteration counts vary widely across lanes, consider a fixed-count divstep variant.
6. Barrett and exact division by inverse map well to IMAD/IMAD.HI on NVIDIA; on AMD they cost quarter-rate multiplies but still far less than the compiler's division. Our no-floating-point rule could be reconsidered on GPUs only for division with remainder, where the hardware float reciprocal (MUFU.RCP, v_rcp_f32) plus exact integer correction is what the vendors' own integer division uses; this is an option to measure, not a recommendation.
7. SoA layout and the one-bit shrink margin carry over unchanged.
