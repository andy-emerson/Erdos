# Collatz maps on R/U: the analogue of N

Working notes. Nothing here is in the paper drafts. Definitions, rotated and mirrored maps, and Theorems 2.1, 3.1, 4.4 and 4.7 are those of `theorems.md`. The computation is `assoctest.py` with output in `logs/assoctest.txt`, and the Lean proofs are in `lean/GaussianCollatz/Quotient.lean` (Section 9).

## 1. Summary

The positive integers are the nonzero integers up to sign, $\mathbb{N} = (\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\})/\{\pm 1\}$. In a domain $R$ with a finite unit group $U$, the corresponding object is $R/U$, the nonzero elements up to associates. These notes make that precise for Collatz maps.

1. A map $T$ on $R$ induces a map $\bar T$ on $R/U$ with $\bar T[z] = [T z]$ exactly when $T$ sends associates to associates: $T(uz) \sim T(z)$ for all units $u$ and all $z$. Commuting with the units implies this, and in general the condition is strictly weaker (Section 2).
2. If $T$ sends associates to associates, $T$ is a Collatz map on $R$ (every orbit from the domain reaches a cycle containing a unit) if and only if $\bar T$ is a Collatz map on $R/U$ in Collatz's own sense: every orbit reaches the class $[1]$, and $[1]$ is periodic (Theorem 3.2).
3. A cycle of $\bar T$ of length $p$ lifts to cycles of $T$ of lengths $p r$, where the $r$ are the cycle lengths of the self-map $T^p$ of one class. For a commuting map there are exactly $|U|/\mathrm{ord}(\varepsilon)$ lifted cycles, each of length $p\,\mathrm{ord}(\varepsilon)$, where $T^p(z) = \varepsilon z$; for a map that only respects associates, $r$ can be any number from $1$ to $|U|$, for instance $3$ on $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ (Section 4).
4. For the maps of our form (one multiplier $\alpha$, digits by residue class modulo $\pi^k$), sending associates to associates is the same as commuting with the units, for $T$ and for $S$ (Theorem 5.1). So there is no twisted version $S(uz) = \chi(u)uS(z)$ with $\chi \neq 1$. The computation over all digit pairs with small digits at moduli $(1+i)^3$, $(1+i)^4$ and $4, 8, 16$ found no exception, as the theorem predicts. If the multiplier may depend on the class, twisted maps do exist, and each is a class-wise unit multiple of a commuting map with the same quotient map (Proposition 5.3). An example is $3z+1$ on $z \equiv 1$ and $-3z+1$ on $z \equiv 3 \pmod 4$, a Collatz map on $\mathbb{Z}$ that respects associates without commuting.
5. Corollaries (Section 6): the four rotations of a commuting map are one map on $R/U$; the mirrored map induces the conjugate of that map by the automorphism of $R/U$ induced by conjugation; the trivial cycles and the unit cycles of Theorems 2.1 and 3.1 are the lifts of the single class $[1]$, and the stabilizer $U \cap C_1$ of Theorem 2.1 is the group generated by $T^p(1)$.
6. On $\mathbb{Z}$ (Section 7), Barina's $7x \pm 1$ commutes with $-1$, and the map it induces on $\mathbb{N}$ is his map on the positive integers. The map $3x+1$ does not send associates to associates ($5 \mapsto 16$, $-5 \mapsto -14$), and no map of our form at modulus $2$ does. Collatz's map is a map on a fundamental domain, the positives; its two natural lifts to $\mathbb{Z}$ that respect associates are $3z + \mathrm{sgn}(z)$ and $3|z| + 1$ on odd $z$, neither of our form, and its extension to $\mathbb{Z}$ by the same formula is a different problem.

## 2. Associates and the quotient

*Setting.* $R$ is an integral domain and $U$ its group of units, assumed finite. Elements $x, y$ are associates, $x \sim y$, if $y = ux$ for some $u \in U$. This is an equivalence relation (take $u = 1$, $u^{-1}$, and products), the class of $0$ is $\{0\}$, and the class of $z \neq 0$ is $Uz = \{uz : u \in U\}$, with $|U|$ distinct elements because $uz = u'z$ forces $u = u'$ in a domain. Write $[z]$ for the class and $R/U$ for the set of classes of nonzero elements; it is convenient to keep the class $[0] = \{0\}$ as well. The class $[1]$ is $U$.

For $R = \mathbb{Z}$, $[z] \mapsto |z|$ identifies $R/U$ with the positive integers $\mathbb{N}$. For $\mathbb{Z}[i]$, every nonzero class has exactly one element $a + bi$ with $a > 0$, $b \ge 0$ (`exists_unit_primary` in `Soundness.lean` gives existence), so $\mathbb{Z}[i]/U$ is the quarter plane, which is the fundamental domain the drop-below-start check runs on.

*Definition 2.1.* A map $T : R \to R$ respects associates (sends associates to associates) if $T(uz) \sim T(z)$ for all $u \in U$ and all $z \in R$. It commutes with the units if $T(uz) = uT(z)$ for all $u, z$.

*Proposition 2.2 (descent).* (1) There is a map $\bar T : R/U \to R/U$ (on all classes, including $[0]$) with $\bar T[z] = [T(z)]$ for every $z$ if and only if $T$ respects associates. It is then unique, and $\bar T^n[z] = [T^n z]$ for all $n$.

(2) If $T$ commutes with the units, it respects associates.

(3) The converse of (2) fails: $T(z) = z^2$ respects associates on $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ but $T(i \cdot 1) = -1 \neq i = iT(1)$. On $\mathbb{Z}$, $T(z) = 3|z| + 1$ for odd $z$ and $z/2$ for even $z$ respects associates and $T(-1) = 4 \neq -4$.

(4) Every map $\bar T$ induced by a map that respects associates is also induced by a map that commutes with the units: choose one element $f$ in each class, and put $T'(uf) = uT(f)$. If $T(0) = 0$, put $T'(0) = 0$.

*Proof.* (1) If $T$ respects associates and $[x] = [y]$, then $y = ux$ and $[T y] = [T(ux)] = [T x]$, so $[z] \mapsto [Tz]$ is well defined. Conversely, if such $\bar T$ exists, then $[T(uz)] = \bar T[uz] = \bar T[z] = [T z]$, which is the condition. Uniqueness holds because every class is some $[z]$, and the iterate formula follows by induction: $\bar T^{n+1}[z] = \bar T^n[Tz] = [T^n(Tz)]$. (2) $T(uz) = uT(z) \sim T(z)$. (3) $(uz)^2 = u^2 z^2$, and $u^2$ is a unit. For the integer map, $-z$ is odd when $z$ is, $T(-z) = 3|z| + 1 = T(z)$ for odd $z$ and $T(-z) = -T(z)$ for even $z$. (4) Every nonzero $z$ is $uf$ for exactly one representative $f$ and, in a domain, exactly one $u$, so $T'$ is well defined; $T'(vuf) = vuT(f) = vT'(uf)$, and $[T'(uf)] = [T f] = [T(uf)]$. $\square$

So, at the level of $R/U$, respecting associates gives no new maps; what it changes is the lift (Section 4), and whether the commuting replacement $T'$ is again of our form (Section 5).

The norm is constant on classes, $N(uz) = N(z)$, so $N[z]$ is defined, and parity is too, since $uz$ is odd exactly when $z$ is. For an expression, $S$ maps odd elements to odd elements or to $0$, so for an associate-respecting $S$ the induced map acts on the odd classes and $[0]$.

## 3. The transfer theorem

*Definition 3.1.* Let $X \subseteq R$ be a set of nonzero elements closed under units ($uX = X$) with $1 \in X$, for instance all nonzero elements (for $T$) or all odd elements (for $S$). A map $f$ on $R$ is a Collatz map on $X$ if every $z \in X$ has an iterate $f^n(z)$ that is periodic with a cycle containing a unit. This is `Trivial` of `Dynamics.lean` for $X$ the odd elements, and for $X = R \setminus \{0\}$ it is the property asked for: every nonzero orbit reaches a cycle containing a unit. It excludes divergent orbits, orbits reaching $0$, and cycles without a unit through a point of $X$ (a periodic point is an iterate of itself, so its own cycle must contain a unit).

A map $F$ on $R/U$ is a Collatz map on $X/U$ in Collatz's sense if every $[z]$ with $z \in X$ has an iterate $F^n[z] = [1]$, and $[1]$ is periodic, $F^p[1] = [1]$ for some $p \ge 1$. When $F$ maps $X/U$ into itself the second condition follows from the first applied to $F[1]$.

*Theorem 3.2 (transfer).* Let $T$ respect associates, with induced map $\bar T$, and let $X$ be as above. Then $T$ is a Collatz map on $X$ if and only if $\bar T$ is a Collatz map on $X/U$ in Collatz's sense.

*Proof.* Suppose $T$ is a Collatz map. For $z \in X$, let $x = T^n(z)$ be periodic with $T^j(x) = w$ a unit. Then $\bar T^{n+j}[z] = [w] = [1]$. For periodicity apply this to $z = 1 \in X$: we get a unit $w = T^m(1)$ which lies on a cycle (an iterate of a periodic point is periodic), say $T^q(w) = w$ with $q \ge 1$. Since $[w] = [1]$, $\bar T^q[1] = \bar T^q[w] = [T^q w] = [w] = [1]$.

Conversely, let $\bar T^p[1] = [1]$ with $p \ge 1$, and put $g = T^p$. For every unit $v$, $[g(v)] = \bar T^p[v] = \bar T^p[1] = [1]$, so $g(v)$ is a unit: $g$ maps the finite set $U$ into itself. For $z \in X$ take $n$ with $\bar T^n[z] = [1]$, so that $T^n(z) = v$ is a unit. The $|U| + 1$ points $v, g(v), \ldots, g^{|U|}(v)$ lie in $U$, so $g^i(v) = g^j(v)$ for some $i < j \le |U|$. Then $y = g^i(v) = T^{n + pi}(z)$ is a unit with $T^{p(j-i)}(y) = y$ and $p(j - i) \ge 1$: it is periodic and its cycle contains the unit $y$. $\square$

The finiteness of $U$ is used once, in the pigeonhole step, and it is necessary there: the unit reached need not itself be periodic (the twisted map of Section 5.3 sends $-1$ to $1$ and never returns), only some unit further along.

*Remark 3.3.* No other cycles and no divergence on either side. Every cycle of $T$ through $X$ projects to a cycle of $\bar T$ (Section 4), and the projection of a trivial cycle is the cycle of $[1]$; so "the only cycle of $\bar T$ on $X/U$ is that of $[1]$ and no orbit diverges" is the statement "every cycle of $T$ through $X$ contains a unit and no orbit diverges", which by Theorem 3.2 is the Collatz property.

## 4. Lifting cycles

Let $T$ respect associates, $z \neq 0$, and suppose $[z]$ is periodic for $\bar T$ with least period $p$. Then $\bar T^p[z] = [z]$ says $T^p(z) = \varepsilon z$ for a unit $\varepsilon$. Say that a cycle $C$ of $T$ lies over the cycle of $[z]$ if $\{[c] : c \in C\}$ is the cycle of $[z]$, equivalently if $C$ meets the class $Uz$. Every cycle of $T$ lies over a cycle of $\bar T$, because $T^q(c) = c$ gives $\bar T^q[c] = [c]$.

*Theorem 4.1 (associate-respecting maps).* Put $g = T^p$.

1. $g$ maps the class $Uz$ (with $|U|$ elements) into itself.
2. A point $y \in Uz$ is periodic for $T$ if and only if it is periodic for $g$, and then its least $T$-period is $p r$, where $r$ is its least $g$-period. Every $T$-period of $y$ is a multiple of $p$.
3. $C \mapsto C \cap Uz$ is a bijection from the cycles of $T$ lying over the cycle of $[z]$ to the cycles of $g$ in $Uz$, and $|C| = p\,|C \cap Uz|$. There is at least one such cycle and at most $|U|$, and the sum of $|C \cap Uz|$ over them is the number of $g$-periodic points of $Uz$, at most $|U|$.
4. Nothing more can be said in general: on $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ the map with $1 \mapsto -i \mapsto -1 \mapsto 1$, $i \mapsto -i$, and $z \mapsto z$ for non-units respects associates, $[1]$ is fixed ($p = 1$, $\varepsilon = -i$), and the only cycle over it has length $3$, which divides no unit order. A class may also contain points that are not periodic ($i$ here).

*Proof.* (1) For $y \in Uz$, $[g(y)] = \bar T^p[y] = \bar T^p[z] = [z]$. (2) If $T^q(y) = y$ with $q \ge 1$, then $\bar T^q[z] = [z]$, so $p \mid q$: write $q = sp + t$ with $0 \le t < p$; then $[z] = \bar T^q[z] = \bar T^t[z]$, and $t = 0$ by minimality of $p$. So $q = sp$ and $g^s(y) = y$. Conversely $g^r(y) = y$ gives $T^{pr}(y) = y$. The least $T$-period is therefore $p$ times the least $s \ge 1$ with $g^s(y) = y$. (3) If $y \in C \cap Uz$, then $T^i(y) \in Uz$ exactly when $\bar T^i[z] = [z]$, that is, when $p \mid i$, by the argument of (2). So $C \cap Uz = \{g^s(y)\}$ is the $g$-cycle of $y$, and $C$ has $p$ times as many elements, by (2). Distinct cycles of $T$ are disjoint, so they give distinct $g$-cycles, and every $g$-cycle in $Uz$ consists of $T$-periodic points and lies in the $T$-cycle of any of its points. A self-map of a finite nonempty set has at least one cycle. (4) Each class is mapped into itself, so the map respects associates; the rest is direct. $\square$

*Theorem 4.2 (commuting maps).* If $T$ commutes with the units, then in addition:

1. $T^{pm}(vz) = \varepsilon^m vz$ for all units $v$ and all $m \ge 0$, so every point of $Uz$ is periodic, with least period $p\,\mathrm{ord}(\varepsilon)$;
2. the cycles lying over the cycle of $[z]$ are the sets $vC$, $v \in U$, where $C$ is the cycle of $z$; and $vC = C$ if and only if $v \in \langle\varepsilon\rangle$;
3. so there are exactly $|U|/\mathrm{ord}(\varepsilon)$ of them, each of length $p\,\mathrm{ord}(\varepsilon)$, and the stabilizer of $C$ in $U$ is $\langle \varepsilon \rangle$.

*Proof.* (1) $g(vz) = vT^p(z) = v\varepsilon z$, and induction. In a domain $\varepsilon^m vz = vz$ exactly when $\varepsilon^m = 1$, so the least $g$-period of $vz$ is $\mathrm{ord}(\varepsilon)$, and Theorem 4.1(2) gives the $T$-period. (2) $T^j(vz) = vT^j(z)$, so the cycle of $vz$ is $vC$. By Theorem 4.1(3), $C \cap Uz$ is the $g$-cycle $\{\varepsilon^m z\}$; so $vC = C$ iff $vz \in C$ iff $vz = \varepsilon^m z$ for some $m$ iff $v \in \langle \varepsilon\rangle$. (3) The cycles over $[z]$ are the cosets of $\langle\varepsilon\rangle$. $\square$

For $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ the count is $4$, $2$ or $1$ as $\varepsilon = 1$, $-1$ or $\pm i$; for $\mathbb{Z}$ it is $2$ or $1$ as $\varepsilon = 1$ or $-1$. In the language of `rotation-prior-art.md` 3.12, $T^p(z) = \varepsilon z$ is a relative periodic orbit and $|U|/\mathrm{ord}(\varepsilon)$ is the multiplicity $|G|/|G_C|$; Theorem 4.2 is the standard equivariant fact specialized to a free action of a cyclic group, and Theorem 4.1 is what remains without equivariance.

## 5. For our maps, respecting associates is commuting

### 5.1 The theorem

Let $(\alpha, k, d)$ be an expression on $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ (Definition 1.1 of `theorems.md`; $\alpha$ odd, so $\alpha \neq 0$, and any $k \ge 0$, any table), with $w(z) = \alpha z + d(z)$ for odd $z$. For an odd class $c$ modulo $\pi^k$ and a unit $u$, put $e = e_u(c) = d(uc) - u\,d(c)$, which depends only on $c$ and $u$. For odd $z \in c$,
$$w(uz) = u\,w(z) + e.$$

*Lemma 5.0.* Let $W, W' \in \mathbb{Z}[i]$ with $W' = uW + e$.

1. If $W' \sim W$ and $N(W) > N(e)$, then $e = 0$ and $W' = uW$.
2. If $W$ and $W'$ are nonzero with associate odd parts, and $N(W) > N(e)$ and $N(W') > N(e)$, then $e = 0$.

*Proof.* (1) Let $W' = \varepsilon W$. Then $(\varepsilon - u)W = e$. If $\varepsilon \neq u$, the nonzero element $\varepsilon - u$ has norm at least $1$, and $N(e) \ge N(W)$, a contradiction. So $\varepsilon = u$ and $e = 0$.

(2) Write $W = \pi^a o$ and $W' = \pi^b o'$ with $o, o'$ odd and $o' = \varepsilon o$. Then $\pi^a W' = \varepsilon \pi^b W$. If $a = b$, cancelling gives $W' = \varepsilon W$ and (1) applies. If $a < b$, cancelling $\pi^a$ from $\pi^a(uW + e) = \varepsilon\pi^b W$ gives $(\varepsilon\pi^{b-a} - u)W = e$; the coefficient is nonzero, since $N(\varepsilon\pi^{b-a}) = 2^{b-a} \neq 1$, so $N(e) \ge N(W)$, a contradiction. If $a > b$, then $\pi^{a-b}W' = \varepsilon W = \varepsilon u^{-1}(W' - e)$, so $(\varepsilon u^{-1} - \pi^{a-b})W' = \varepsilon u^{-1} e$ with a nonzero coefficient, and $N(e) \ge N(W')$, a contradiction. $\square$

*Lemma 5.0'.* In every odd class $c$ and for every unit $u$ and bound $B$, there is $z \in c$ with $N(w(z)) > B$ and $N(w(uz)) > B$.

*Proof.* Take $z_0 \in c$, $\beta = \alpha\pi^k \neq 0$, and $z = z_0 + \pi^k\bar\beta n$ with $n$ a positive integer. Then $z \equiv z_0$, $w(z) = w(z_0) + N(\beta)n$ and $w(uz) = w(uz_0) + uN(\beta)n$, using that the digits are those of the classes of $z_0$ and $uz_0$. One coordinate of each moves by $\pm N(\beta)n$ while the other is fixed, so both norms exceed $B$ for large $n$. $\square$

*Theorem 5.1.* For an expression on $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ the following are equivalent:

1. the table commutes with the units, $d(uz) = u\,d(z)$ for odd $z$ (the expression is symmetric);
2. $T$ commutes with the units;
3. $T$ respects associates;
4. $S$ commutes with the units;
5. $S$ respects associates.

Moreover, (3) and (5) may be weakened to: for every odd class $c$ and unit $u$, the condition holds at one odd $z \in c$ with $N(w(z)), N(w(uz)) > N(d(uc) - u\,d(c))$.

*Proof.* (1) implies (2) and (4) by Lemma 1.4 of `theorems.md`; (2) implies (3) and (4) implies (5) by Proposition 2.2(2). For odd $z$, $T(z) = w(z)$, and $S(z)$ is the odd part of $w(z)$ (or $0$ if $w(z) = 0$, and then $S(uz)$ must be $0$ too). If (3) or (5) holds at a point $z$ as in the last sentence, Lemma 5.0 gives $e_u(c) = 0$, and Lemma 5.0' supplies such $z$ in every class. So $d(uc) = u\,d(c)$ for every odd class and unit, which is (1). $\square$

The proof uses only that the norm is multiplicative with values in the nonnegative integers, is $0$ only at $0$, is $1$ exactly on units, and that $N(\pi) > 1$. So the theorem holds verbatim on $\mathbb{Z}$ ($N(z) = z^2$, $\pi = 2$, $U = \{\pm 1\}$) and on the Eisenstein integers and every imaginary quadratic order with its prime $P$ above $2$, with the same proof (for a non-principal $P$, $v_P$ replaces the exponent of $\pi$ and the same inequalities go through with $N(P)^{b-a}$).

*Corollary 5.2.* (1) No expression at level $k$ respects associates when some unit $u \neq 1$ is $\equiv 1 \pmod{\pi^k}$, by Theorem 1 of `integer-census.md`: on $\mathbb{Z}$ at modulus $2$ (so no map $3x + d$ does) and on $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ at $\pi$ and $\pi^2$ (so $E_2$ of Remark 3.2 does not). (2) There is no twisted commuting: if $S(uz) = \chi(u)uS(z)$ for a character $\chi$ of $U$ (or any function to $U$), then $S$ respects associates, hence commutes, so $\chi(u) = 1$ (since $S(z) \neq 0$ for some odd $z$ by Lemma 5.0'). The same for $T$. (3) The class of commuting tables is closed under rotation ($d^v(uz) = d(vuz) = u\,d^v(z)$), so "commuting up to rotating $\alpha$" is again just commuting.

### 5.2 Computation

`assoctest.py` tests the claim directly, so that it does not rest on the proof alone. Since $S(uz) \sim S(z)$ for $z$ in a class $c$ involves only the two digits $d(c)$ and $d(uc)$, it suffices to test pairs: for each odd class $c$, unit $u \neq 1$, digit $\delta_1$ in the class of $-\alpha c$ and $\delta_2$ in the class of $-\alpha u c$, check $S(uz) \sim S(z)$ and $T(uz) \sim T(z)$ at all odd $z \in c$ in a norm ball. A table respects associates on the ball exactly when all its pairs pass, so a non-commuting associate-respecting table, minimal or not, could exist only if a pair with $\delta_2 \neq u\delta_1$ passed.

| ring, modulus | multipliers | digits | test points | pairs | commuting pairs | non-commuting pairs passing |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\mathbb{Z}[i]$, $\pi^3$ | all $96$ odd $\alpha$, $N(\alpha) \le 61$ | $N \le 65$ | odd $z$, $N(z) \le 400$ | $903168$ | $32256$ (all pass) | $0$ for $S$, $0$ for $T$ |
| $\mathbb{Z}[i]$, $\pi^4$ | same | $N \le 65$ | same | $451584$ | $32256$ (all pass) | $0$, $0$ |
| $\mathbb{Z}$, $4$ | odd $m$, $\lvert m\rvert \le 63$ | $\lvert d\rvert \le 65$ | odd $\lvert z\rvert \le 3000$ | $139392$ | $4224$ (all pass) | $0$, $0$ |
| $\mathbb{Z}$, $8$ | same | same | same | $69760$ | $4224$ | $0$, $0$ |
| $\mathbb{Z}$, $16$ | same | same | same | $34944$ | $4224$ | $0$, $0$ |

These digit bounds contain every minimal digit table (at $\pi^4$ the least norms in odd classes are at most $9$) and many non-minimal ones. The same runs at $\pi$ and $\pi^2$ ($N(\alpha) \le 29$, digits $N \le 41$) and at modulus $2$ on $\mathbb{Z}$ also found no passing non-commuting pair; there the "commuting" pairs are not consistent with a single table, since $uc$ and $c$ are the same class. A separate check of Lemma 5.0(2) at levels $1$ and $3$ ($N(\alpha) \le 29$, odd $z$ with $N(z) \le 200$, digits with coordinates at most $5$ in absolute value, all even $e \neq 0$ with coordinates at most $4$) tested $139733568$ triples $(z, u, e)$ with $N(w(z)), N(w(uz)) > 4N(e)$ (a weaker hypothesis than the lemma's $N(e)$) and found no case where the odd parts of $w(z)$ and $w(uz)$ were associates.

So the answer to the test is no: for maps of our form, associate-respecting and commuting coincide, and none of the found maps is new.

### 5.3 Class-dependent multipliers: twisted maps exist

Allow the multiplier to depend on the class, $T(z) = \alpha_c z + d(c)$ for odd $z \in c$, with $\pi^k \mid \alpha_c z + d(c)$ (a generalized Collatz map in the sense of Matthews and Watts, with division by $\pi$ on even inputs). Then twisted maps occur.

*Proposition 5.3.* (1) Such $T$ respects associates if and only if for every odd class $c$ and unit $u$ there is a unit $\varepsilon = \varepsilon_u(c)$ with $\alpha_{uc}u = \varepsilon\alpha_c$ and $d(uc) = \varepsilon\,d(c)$. (2) If all $\alpha_c$ are associates of one $\alpha$, $\alpha_c = s(c)\alpha$, then $T = s \cdot T'$ on odd elements, where $T'$ is the expression with multiplier $\alpha$ and table $d'(c) = s(c)^{-1}d(c)$; $T$ respects associates if and only if $T'$ commutes with the units, and then $\bar T = \bar T'$, so $T$ and $T'$ are Collatz maps together.

*Proof.* (1) $T(uz) = \alpha_{uc}uz + d(uc)$ and $T(z) = \alpha_c z + d(c)$. Suppose $T$ respects associates. Each $z \in c$ has a unit $\varepsilon_z$ with $T(uz) = \varepsilon_z T(z)$. The class $c$ is infinite and $U$ is finite, so two distinct points $z \neq z'$ of $c$ have the same $\varepsilon$. Subtracting the two equations gives $\alpha_{uc}u(z - z') = \varepsilon\alpha_c(z - z')$, so $\alpha_{uc}u = \varepsilon\alpha_c$, and then $d(uc) = \varepsilon\,d(c)$. Conversely these two equations give $T(uz) = \varepsilon T(z)$ for every $z \in c$, and even $z$ cause no trouble since $T(uz) = uz/\pi = uT(z)$. (2) $T(z) = s(c)(\alpha z + s(c)^{-1}d(c)) = s(c)T'(z)$, and $\pi^k \mid \alpha z + d'(c)$ because $s(c)$ is a unit. So $T(uz) \sim T'(uz)$ and $T(z) \sim T'(z)$: $T$ respects associates iff $T'$ does, iff $T'$ commutes (Theorem 5.1), and the induced maps agree. Theorem 3.2 gives the last statement. $\square$

*Example 5.4.* On $\mathbb{Z}$ at modulus $4$, let $T(z) = 3z + 1$ for $z \equiv 1$, $T(z) = -3z + 1$ for $z \equiv 3 \pmod 4$, and $T(z) = z/2$ for even $z$. Then $T(-z) = T(z)$ for odd $z$ and $T(-z) = -T(z)$ for even $z$: $T$ respects associates, does not commute, and is twisted commuting with $\chi(-1) = -1$ on odd inputs. Here $T'$ is $3x \pm 1$ ($3z + 1$ for $z \equiv 1$, $3z - 1$ for $z \equiv 3$), which commutes, and $\bar T = \bar T'$ is the map $n \mapsto 3n \pm 1$, $n \mapsto n/2$ on $\mathbb{N}$. That map is a Collatz map on $\mathbb{N}$ for a trivial reason: for odd $n > 1$ the odd step gives a multiple of $4$, so the accelerated map sends $n$ to at most $(3n+1)/4 < n$, and $1 \to 4 \to 2 \to 1$. By Theorem 3.2 both $T$ and $T'$ are Collatz maps on $\mathbb{Z}$, but their lifts of $[1]$ differ, as Theorems 4.1 and 4.2 allow. For $T'$, $T'^3(1) = 1$, $\varepsilon = 1$, and there are $2/1 = 2$ trivial cycles, $\{1, 4, 2\}$ and $\{-1, -4, -2\}$. For $T$, $T(-1) = 4$, so $-1$ is not periodic and there is one trivial cycle, $\{1, 4, 2\}$. The same twist applied to Barina's $7x \pm 1$ gives $7z + 1$ on $z \equiv 1$, $-7z + 1$ on $z \equiv 3 \pmod 4$, with the same map on $\mathbb{N}$.

So the twisted condition does appear, but only by allowing the multiplier to vary by a unit across classes, and it is then a class-wise unit multiple of a commuting single-multiplier map. On $R/U$ it gives nothing new.

## 6. Corollaries

### 6.1 Rotation

*Corollary 6.1.* Let $(\alpha, k, d)$ be an expression and $u$ a unit. If $S_\alpha$ respects associates (by Theorem 5.1, if the table commutes), then $S_{u\alpha}$ respects associates and induces the same map on $R/U$: $\bar S_{u\alpha} = \bar S_\alpha$. So the four rotations of a commuting map are one map on $R/U$, and they are Collatz maps together.

*Proof.* By Proposition 4.2 of `theorems.md`, $S_{u\alpha}(z) = S_\alpha(uz) \sim S_\alpha(z)$. So $S_{u\alpha}(vz) \sim S_\alpha(vz) \sim S_\alpha(z) \sim S_{u\alpha}(z)$, and $[S_{u\alpha}(z)] = [S_\alpha(z)]$. The last statement is Theorem 3.2 applied to both. $\square$

The lifts differ. Under commuting, $S_{u\alpha}^p(1) = u^pS_\alpha^p(1)$, so with $\varepsilon = S_\alpha^p(1)$ and $p$ the period of $[1]$, the rotation by $u$ has $|U|/\mathrm{ord}(u^p\varepsilon)$ trivial cycles (Theorem 4.2). For $H$, $S(1) = -1$, so $p = 1$, $\varepsilon = -1$; the rotations by $1, i, -1, -i$ have $\varepsilon = -1, -i, 1, i$, hence $2, 1, 4, 1$ trivial cycles, all of them unit cycles. Theorem 4.4 of `theorems.md` is this statement together with the identity $S_{u\alpha}^n = u^nS_\alpha^n$, which is the lift.

### 6.2 Reflection

Complex conjugation $\sigma$ is an automorphism with $\sigma(U) = U$, so $\bar\sigma[z] = [\bar z]$ is a well-defined involution of $R/U$, fixing $[1]$ and preserving parity and norm. More generally any automorphism $\sigma$ of $R$ maps units to units and induces a bijection $\bar\sigma$ of $R/U$.

*Corollary 6.2.* If $S_\alpha$ respects associates, then so does $S_{\bar\alpha}$, and $\bar S_{\bar\alpha} = \bar\sigma\,\bar S_\alpha\,\bar\sigma$. So the mirrored map induces the conjugate of $\bar S_\alpha$ by the automorphism $\bar\sigma$ of $R/U$, the two are Collatz maps together, and the norm sequence of $[z]$ under $\bar S_{\bar\alpha}$ is that of $[\bar z]$ under $\bar S_\alpha$.

*Proof.* By Proposition 4.6 of `theorems.md`, $S_{\bar\alpha}(z) = (-i)^v\,\overline{S_\alpha(\bar z)} \sim h(z)$ with $h(z) = \overline{S_\alpha(\bar z)}$. The map $h$ respects associates: $h(uz) = \overline{S_\alpha(\bar u\bar z)} \sim \overline{S_\alpha(\bar z)}$, because conjugation maps associates to associates. So $S_{\bar\alpha}(uz) \sim h(uz) \sim h(z) \sim S_{\bar\alpha}(z)$, and $[S_{\bar\alpha}(z)] = [h(z)] = \bar\sigma\bar S_\alpha\bar\sigma[z]$. A conjugacy by a bijection fixing $[1]$ and preserving parity carries the Collatz property of $\bar S_\alpha$ on odd classes to that of $\bar S_{\bar\alpha}$, and Theorem 3.2 lifts it. Norms are preserved by $\bar\sigma$. $\square$

So the eight variations of a commuting map (four rotations, each with or without mirror) induce at most two maps on $R/U$, $\bar S_\alpha$ and $\bar\sigma\bar S_\alpha\bar\sigma$, and these are conjugate. The factor $(-i)^v$ of Theorem 4.7, which needed the whole iterate formula, disappears on $R/U$. For an automorphism $\sigma$ with $\sigma(\pi) = \eta\pi$ the same argument applies with $\eta^v$ in place of $(-i)^v$.

### 6.3 Trivial cycles and unit cycles are the lift of $[1]$

A cycle of $T$ contains a unit exactly when it meets the class $[1] = U$, that is, when it lies over the cycle of $[1]$. So the trivial cycles are exactly the lifts of the cycle of $[1]$, and Theorem 4.1 and Theorem 4.2 with $z = 1$ describe them. Let $p$ be the least period of $[1]$ under $\bar T$ and $\varepsilon = T^p(1)$, a unit.

1. Theorem 2.1(a) of `theorems.md` (at most $|U|$ trivial cycles, for any map) is, for an associate-respecting map, Theorem 4.1(3): the trivial cycles correspond to the cycles of the self-map $T^p$ of $U$.
2. Theorem 2.1(b) is Theorem 4.2 at $z = 1$: the trivial cycles are $vC_1$, there are $|U|/\mathrm{ord}(\varepsilon)$ of them, each of length $p\,\mathrm{ord}(\varepsilon)$, and the stabilizer $H = U \cap C_1$ is $\langle\varepsilon\rangle = \langle T^p(1)\rangle$. This identifies $H$, which Theorem 2.1(b) only showed to be a subgroup.
3. Theorem 3.1 is the case $p = 1$. All elements of $C_1$ are units exactly when the cycle of $[1]$ is $\{[1]\}$, that is, when $w = T(1)$ is a unit; then $\varepsilon = w$, and the unit cycles have length $\mathrm{ord}(w)$ and number $|U|/\mathrm{ord}(w)$. If $T(1)$ is not a unit, $p > 1$ or $[1]$ is not periodic, and there is no unit cycle.
4. Remark 3.2 of `theorems.md` ($E_2$, a unit cycle of length $3$ with the unit $i$ off it) is the behaviour of Theorem 4.1(4). $E_2$ itself does not respect associates (Corollary 5.2), but on $U$ it acts as the example of Theorem 4.1(4).

## 7. The integers

$\mathbb{N} = (\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\})/\{\pm 1\}$ with $[z] \mapsto |z|$. A map $T$ on $\mathbb{Z}$ that respects associates induces the map $n \mapsto |T(n)|$ on $\mathbb{N}$; if $T$ commutes with $-1$ (an odd map) and maps the positives to the positives, this is just $T$ restricted to the positives. Conversely every map $F$ on $\mathbb{N}$ comes from the commuting map $z \mapsto \mathrm{sgn}(z)F(|z|)$ (Proposition 2.2(4) with the positives as representatives).

*Barina's $7x \pm 1$.* $B(n) = 7n + 1$ for $n \equiv 1$, $7n - 1$ for $n \equiv 3 \pmod 4$, $n/2$ for even $n$ (D. Barina, arXiv:1807.00908). It is the expression with $m = 7$ and the minimal symmetric digits $\pm 1$ at modulus $4$: if $n \equiv 1$ then $-n \equiv 3$ and $B(-n) = -7n - 1 = -B(n)$, and $B(-n) = -B(n)$ for even $n$. So $B$ commutes with $-1$, and since $B(n) > 0$ for $n > 0$, the induced map on $\mathbb{N}$ is Barina's map on the positive integers. By Theorem 3.2, Barina's conjecture (every positive orbit reaches $1$) is equivalent to $B$ being a Collatz map on $\mathbb{Z}$. By Theorem 4.2, with $B^4(1) = 1$ ($1 \to 8 \to 4 \to 2 \to 1$), $p = 4$ and $\varepsilon = 1$, there are exactly two trivial cycles, $\{1, 8, 4, 2\}$ and its negative, which is the remark on Barina's OEIS page that negative orbits are negated positive ones.

*Collatz's $3x + 1$.* $C(5) = 16$ and $C(-5) = -14$, which are not associates, so $C$ does not respect associates and defines no map on $\mathbb{Z}/\{\pm 1\}$. This is not an accident of the digit: by Corollary 5.2, no map of our form at modulus $2$ respects associates, because $1 \equiv -1 \pmod 2$ forces $d(-1) = d(1)$, while respecting associates forces $d(-1) = -d(1)$, hence $d(1) = 0$, which is even. Collatz's map is a map on a fundamental domain: it is defined on the positives, which represent $\mathbb{N}$, and the induced object on $\mathbb{N}$ is just $n \mapsto C(n)$. Its lifts to $\mathbb{Z}$ that respect associates are the commuting lift $z \mapsto 3z + \mathrm{sgn}(z)$ on odd $z$, $z/2$ on even $z$, and the non-commuting lift $z \mapsto 3|z| + 1$ on odd $z$ (Proposition 2.2(3)). The digit of either depends on the sign of $z$, not on a residue class, so neither is of our form; by Theorem 3.2 each is a Collatz map on $\mathbb{Z}$ if and only if the Collatz conjecture holds. The extension of $3x + 1$ to $\mathbb{Z}$ by the same formula is a different problem: on the negatives it is $3x - 1$ on the positives (via $x \mapsto -x$, `rotation-prior-art.md` 3.1), with the cycles through $-1$, $-5$ and $-17$, so it is not a Collatz map on $\mathbb{Z}$, and it is not a lift of any map on $\mathbb{N}$.

So within the maps of our form, the first level at which a map induces a map on $\mathbb{N}$ is modulus $4$, and there the quotient picture holds exactly; Collatz's map itself lives only on the fundamental domain.
