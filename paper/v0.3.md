# Adding Rationals with Shared Greatest Common Divisors

*Draft v0.3.*

## 1. The problem

A rational number is a quotient $a/b$ of integers with $b > 0$. Two rationals are added exactly by the identity

$$
\frac{a}{b} + \frac{c}{d} = \frac{ad + cb}{bd}.
$$

On a machine, integers are stored in words of fixed width. Larger integers are stored as sequences of words, and arithmetic on them costs more as they grow. We count the cost of arithmetic on integers of $n$ bits:
- **addition and subtraction:** $O(n)$;
- **multiplication:** $M(n)$, and $M(n, u)$ for an $n$-bit number times a $u$-bit number;
- **division with remainder:** $D(n)$, and $D(n, s)$ for an $n$-bit number by an $s$-bit one;
- **greatest common divisor:** $G(n)$.

These costs depend on the algorithm used. Each cost below is therefore given in three forms:
- **symbolically,** in terms of $M$, $D$ and $G$;
- **with schoolbook algorithms,** the simple methods;
- **with the fastest known algorithms.**

The last two are each the worst-case bound of their algorithms. Together they mark the range in which practical implementations lie. Appendices A–C describe the algorithms.

| Operation | Schoolbook | Fastest known |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $M(n)$ (App. A) | $O(n^2)$ | $O(n \log n)$ |
| $M(n, u)$, $u \le n$ | $O(nu)$ | $O(n \log u)$ |
| $D(n)$ (App. B) | $O(n^2)$ | $O(n \log n)$ |
| $D(n, s)$, $s \le n$ | $O(ns)$ | $O(n \log s)$ |
| $G(n)$ (App. C) | $O(n^2)$ (Euclid) | $O(n \log^2 n)$ |

Logarithms are read as at least 1, so $O(n \log u)$ is $O(n)$ when $u$ is small. $O$-notation hides constant factors, and they matter here: even when $G$ and $M$ have the same order, a gcd costs several multiplications in practice.

**Sizes.** Operands need not have the same size, which is why $M(n, u)$ and $D(n, s)$ appear above. This paper nevertheless assumes that the input rationals have numerators and denominators of one common size, $n$ bits; it does not analyse unequal input sizes. What *is* tracked by size are the parts the algorithms produce (gcds, residuals, cofactors, base elements), because that is where the methods differ.

**Arithmetic and structure.** Each method's cost is split into three categories:
- **arithmetic per operation:** the computation once the operands are in hand;
- **structure per operation:** retrieving the operands and any lookups;
- **structure, one-time or amortized:** building and maintaining the storage, paid per new denominator or per collection rather than per operation.

The identity needs three multiplications and one addition, and no storage beyond the two values:

| Identity | Symbolic | Schoolbook | Fastest |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Arithmetic per operation | $3M(n) + O(n)$ | $O(n^2)$ | $O(n \log n)$ |
| Structure per operation | $O(1)$ | $O(1)$ | $O(1)$ |
| Structure, one-time | none | none | none |

That is cheap per operation, but the result is larger than its operands. $bd$ has about $2n$ bits, and a sequence of $T$ additions produces denominators of about $Tn$ bits, even when the true value is small. For example, $\tfrac{1}{6} + \tfrac{1}{10} = \tfrac{16}{60}$, which is $\tfrac{4}{15}$. Left alone, the integers grow without bound, every later operation pays for their size, and the computation eventually exceeds the machine.

## 2. Lowest terms and the greatest common divisor

The standard remedy is to keep every rational in **lowest terms**: $\gcd(a, b) = 1$ and $b > 0$ [Knuth, §4.5.1]. Every rational then has exactly one representation, and its integers are as small as its value allows. The gcd is computed by Euclid's algorithm or one of its descendants [Euclid; Knuth, §4.5.2] (Appendix C).

### 2.1 The direct method

Compute the identity's result, then divide both parts by their greatest common divisor:

$$
h = \gcd(ad + cb,\ bd), \qquad \frac{a}{b} + \frac{c}{d} = \frac{(ad + cb)/h}{bd/h}.
$$

| Direct method | Symbolic | Schoolbook | Fastest |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Arithmetic per operation | $3M(n) + G(2n) + 2D(2n)$ | $O(n^2)$ | $O(n \log^2 n)$ |
| Structure per operation | $O(1)$ | $O(1)$ | $O(1)$ |
| Structure, one-time | none | none | none |

This is *more* work per operation than the identity alone. What the gcd buys is not speed but boundedness: it keeps the integers small, and so keeps long computations feasible. The gcd becomes the dominant cost of exact rational arithmetic, and the target of every further optimisation.

### 2.2 The refined method: a chain of gcds

The gcd in the direct method takes a sum, $ad + cb$, and a product, $bd$. A sum cannot be broken apart, but a product can. The refined method, due to Henrici [Henrici] and presented by Knuth [Knuth, §4.5.1], breaks the one large gcd into a chain of two smaller ones.

Let $g = \gcd(b, d)$ and write $b = g\,b'$ and $d = g\,d'$. Then

$$
\gcd(ad + cb,\ bd) = g \cdot \gcd\!\left(\tfrac{ad + cb}{g},\ g\right).
$$

Two things happen in this equation.
- **$g$ comes out once.** It divides both arguments: $ad + cb = g\,(ad' + cb')$, and $bd = g^2\, b' d'$ contains $g$ twice. So $\gcd(ad + cb,\ bd) = g \cdot \gcd(ad' + cb',\ g\, b' d')$, and the quotient $(ad + cb)/g$ is simply $ad' + cb'$, computed directly without forming $ad + cb$.
- **The residuals drop out.** $b'$ and $d'$ share nothing with $(ad + cb)/g$, so they leave the remaining gcd entirely, and only $g$ is left.

*Proof that the residuals drop out.* Modulo $b'$, the quotient $ad' + cb'$ is congruent to $a d'$. Now $a$ is coprime to $b'$, since $a/b$ is in lowest terms and $b'$ divides $b$; and $d'$ is coprime to $b'$, since $g$ took everything $b$ and $d$ share. So $\gcd(ad' + cb',\ b') = 1$, and likewise $\gcd(ad' + cb',\ d') = 1$. A number coprime to $b'$ and to $d'$ has the same gcd with $g\,b'd'$ as with $g$. $\square$

With $h = \gcd\big((ad + cb)/g,\ g\big)$, the sum in lowest terms is

$$
\frac{a}{b} + \frac{c}{d} = \frac{(ad' + cb')/h}{b' \cdot (d/h)},
$$

since $b'\,d'\,g/h = b' \cdot d/h$.

**The gain:** one gcd on the $n$-bit denominators, then one gcd against $g$, at most $n$ bits and often small, instead of one gcd on $2n$-bit numbers. Write $\gamma$ for the size of $g$ in bits, and $u$ for the larger size of the residuals $b'$ and $d'$, about $n - \gamma$. The second gcd costs one remainder of the quotient modulo $g$, then a gcd on $\gamma$ bits. The three multiplications, $a d'$, $c b'$ and $b' \cdot (d/h)$, each have one factor of only $u$ bits.

**The case $g = 1$.** When the denominators share nothing,

$$
\gcd(ad + cb,\ bd) = 1,
$$

the identity's result is already in lowest terms, and the second gcd is skipped.

**Examples.** For $\tfrac{1}{6} + \tfrac{1}{10}$: $g = 2$, $b' = 3$, $d' = 5$, the quotient is $1 \cdot 5 + 1 \cdot 3 = 8$, and $h = \gcd(8, 2) = 2$. The sum is $\tfrac{8/2}{3 \cdot (10/2)} = \tfrac{4}{15}$. For $\tfrac{1}{2} + \tfrac{1}{2}$, everything cancels: $g = 2$, $b' = d' = 1$, the quotient is $1 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 1 = 2$, $h = \gcd(2, 2) = 2$, and the sum is $\tfrac{2/2}{1 \cdot (2/2)} = \tfrac{1}{1}$.

| Refined method | Symbolic | Schoolbook | Fastest |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Arithmetic per operation | $G(n) + 2D(n, \gamma) + 2M(n, u) + D(2n, \gamma) + G(\gamma) + M(n, u)$, plus exact divisions by $h$ | $O(n^2)$ | $O(n \log^2 n)$ |
| Structure per operation | $O(1)$ | $O(1)$ | $O(1)$ |
| Structure, one-time | none | none | none |

The terms are, in order: the first gcd, the two residuals, the numerator, the second gcd, and the denominator. The order is unchanged, but the constants fall, because the operands are smaller. The first gcd, $\gcd(b, d)$ on the full denominators, is now the largest single cost of an addition. Note also that finding $g = 1$ is not free: one cannot know that the denominators share nothing without computing that first gcd. Section 4 removes this cost from the addition: there $g$, and with it the case $g = 1$, is known without a gcd at addition time; the gcds move to building the base (Sections 4.3 and 5.3).

## 3. A shared denominator

When both operands are written over the same denominator $L$, the refined method's $g$ is $L$ itself, and the numerators simply add:

$$
\frac{m_x}{L} + \frac{m_y}{L} = \frac{m_x + m_y}{L}.
$$

That is one integer addition, with no multiplication and no gcd. What remains is the refined method's second gcd, $\gcd(m_x + m_y, L)$, now on the full $L$. Rather than pay it at every addition, a whole collection of values over one $L$ is kept in lowest terms *as a whole*, and reduced only occasionally, for example at the end of a computation, by $\gcd(L, m_1, \ldots, m_N)$. Postponing reduction in this way is called **lazy reduction**.

This calls for a different storage. The collection is held in a **common-denominator representation**: $L$ once, and an array of integer numerators. Rationals $a_i / b_i$ are brought into this form by taking $L = \text{lcm}(b_1, \ldots, b_N)$ and $m_i = a_i \cdot L / b_i$. Write $\ell$ for the size of $L$ in bits; $n \le \ell \le Nn$. Each lcm step is $\text{lcm}(L, b_i) = L \cdot \big(b_i / \gcd(L, b_i)\big)$, and its gcd costs one remainder $L \bmod b_i$ and then a gcd on $n$ bits. Per element, the conversion is a gcd, a few divisions of $\ell$ bits by $n$ bits, and two multiplications of $\ell$ bits by $n$ bits.

| Shared denominator | Symbolic | Schoolbook | Fastest |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Arithmetic per operation | $O(\ell)$ | $O(\ell)$ | $O(\ell)$ |
| Structure per operation | $O(1)$ | $O(1)$ | $O(1)$ |
| Structure, one-time: conversion of $N$ values | $N\big(G(n) + 3D(\ell, n) + 2M(\ell, n)\big)$ | $O(N \ell n)$ | $O\big(N(\ell \log n + n \log^2 n)\big)$ |
| Structure, amortized: one lazy reduction of $N$ values | $N\,G(\ell) + (N + 1)\,D(\ell)$ | $O(N \ell^2)$ | $O(N \ell \log^2 \ell)$ |

A new value whose denominator does not divide $L$ enlarges $L$, and every stored numerator must then be rescaled: another $N$ multiplications.

The method works when the data share a scale, such as amounts in cents over $100$: then $\ell \approx n$, and the conversion is paid once. It fails when denominators are unrelated, because their lcm grows quickly: the lcm of $1, 2, \ldots, N$ grows like $e^N$. Every numerator is then scaled up to that size, and arithmetic on small numbers becomes arithmetic on enormous ones. The method is all or nothing: either the values share one denominator, or we are back to Section 2.

## 4. Sharing the gcds ahead of time

Sections 2 and 3 are two ends of one idea. The refined method computes what two denominators share, $\gcd(b, d)$, at the moment they meet. The shared-denominator method knows it in advance, because it is everything. Most real data lies in between: denominators share some factors without being equal, as $12$, $18$ and $30$ do.

The idea of this work is to **do the refined method's first gcd ahead of time, for the whole collection at once.** Instead of computing $\gcd(b, d)$ whenever two values meet, we keep every denominator broken into the parts that denominators share. Then $\gcd(b, d)$ for any pair is simply the product of the parts the two have in common, already in hand.
- On the arithmetic side, this is the refined gcd method.
- On the storage side, it is a generalisation of the common-denominator representation: each value shares with every other value exactly the part of its denominator they have in common.

### 4.1 A coprime base from gcds and residuals

Start from the denominators. Take two of them, $b$ and $d$, compute $g = \gcd(b, d)$, and replace $b$ and $d$ by $g$, $b/g$ and $d/g$, dropping any 1s and merging duplicates. Repeat on any two numbers in the set that still share a factor, until no two do. The result is a set of pairwise coprime integers greater than 1, $p = (p_1, \ldots, p_k)$, called a **coprime base**, and every denominator is a product of powers of its elements. Only gcds and exact divisions are used, never factoring into primes. Replacing numbers by finer pairwise coprime parts in this way is called **factor refinement** [BDS]; a coprime base can be computed in time essentially linear in the total size of the input [Bernstein].

**Example.** For $12$ and $18$: $\gcd(12, 18) = 6$, with residuals $2$ and $3$, so the set is $\{6, 2, 3\}$. Then $6$ and $2$ share $2$; replacing them by $2$, $3$ and $1$ (dropped) and merging duplicates leaves $\{2, 3\}$. The base is $(2, 3)$, with $12 = 2^2 \cdot 3$ and $18 = 2 \cdot 3^2$.

### 4.2 Denominators as exponent vectors

Over a coprime base, each denominator is described by an **exponent vector** $\alpha \in \mathbb{N}^k$. In multi-index notation,

$$
p^{\alpha} = p_1^{\alpha_1} p_2^{\alpha_2} \cdots p_k^{\alpha_k}.
$$

Because the base is pairwise coprime, this description is unique, and the gcd and lcm of denominators become operations on vectors. Write $\alpha \vee \beta$ and $\alpha \wedge \beta$ for the componentwise maximum and minimum:

$$
\gcd(p^{\alpha}, p^{\beta}) = p^{\alpha \wedge \beta}, \qquad \text{lcm}(p^{\alpha}, p^{\beta}) = p^{\alpha \vee \beta}, \qquad p^{\alpha}\, p^{\beta} = p^{\alpha + \beta}.
$$

So if two denominators have exponent vectors $\alpha$ and $\beta$, the refined method's $g$ is $p^{\alpha \wedge \beta}$, and its residuals are $p^{\alpha - (\alpha \wedge \beta)}$ and $p^{\beta - (\alpha \wedge \beta)}$. All of these are read off the vectors, not computed. Write $\pi_i$ for the size of $p_i$ in bits, and $\pi$ for the largest.

### 4.3 Addition

Let $x = m_x / p^{\alpha}$ and $y = m_y / p^{\beta}$, each in lowest terms. Then

$$
x + y = \frac{m_x\, p^{(\alpha \vee \beta) - \alpha} + m_y\, p^{(\alpha \vee \beta) - \beta}}{p^{\alpha \vee \beta}}.
$$

This is the refined method's arithmetic. The cofactors $p^{(\alpha \vee \beta) - \alpha}$ and $p^{(\alpha \vee \beta) - \beta}$ are exactly its residuals $d'$ and $b'$: the parts of each denominator the other lacks. So $u$, the larger of their sizes, is the same as in Section 2.2; the more the denominators share, the smaller $u$. Recall that the refined method costs $G(n) + 2D(n, \gamma) + 2M(n, u) + D(2n, \gamma) + G(\gamma) + M(n, u)$ per addition. The numerator products, $2M(n, u)$, are unchanged. Setting them aside, here is what changes:
- **Removed: the first gcd and the residual divisions,** $G(n) + 2D(n, \gamma)$. $g = p^{\alpha \wedge \beta}$ and the residuals are read off the vectors.
- **Removed: the denominator product,** $M(n, u)$. The denominator is never multiplied out: it is the vector $\alpha \vee \beta$.
- **Smaller: the second gcd.** In the refined method, cancellation can only come from $g$. Here it can only come from base elements shared at *equal* power, those with $\alpha_i = \beta_i$.

  To see why, suppose $\alpha_i > \beta_i$. Then $p_i$ divides the second term of the numerator, since its cofactor contains $p_i^{\alpha_i - \beta_i}$. And $p_i$ is coprime to the first term: to $m_x$, because $x$ is in lowest terms and $p_i$ divides $p^{\alpha}$; and to its cofactor, because the base is coprime. So $\gcd(\text{sum}, p_i) = \gcd(\text{first term}, p_i) = 1$: neither $p_i$ nor any divisor of it can cancel. The argument does not need $p_i$ to be prime, so it covers composite base elements too.

  The reduction therefore works only against $\sigma = \prod_{\alpha_i = \beta_i} p_i^{\alpha_i}$, the part shared at equal power, which divides $g$. Write $s \le \gamma$ for its size in bits, $e$ for the number of its elements, and $s_i$ for the size of $p_i^{\alpha_i}$, so that $\sum_i s_i = s$. For each of these elements separately, one remainder of the numerator modulo $p_i^{\alpha_i}$, then one gcd on $s_i$ bits, finds what cancels against that element, and an exact division removes it. Working element by element shows which element a cancellation belongs to. If what cancels divides a base element only partly, that element is refined (Section 5.3): a structure cost incurred during the operation.

The structure costs are new. Per operation, the two exponent vectors are read, and the cofactors are formed as products of base elements, unless they are kept from earlier operations. Write $r$ for the number of base elements the two values use. A partial cancellation triggers a refinement: a gcd and divisions on at most $\pi$ bits, and an update of the $v$ stored values that use the refined element. Once per new denominator, the denominator is refined against the base (Section 5.3): it is tested against $\tau$ base elements, each test a remainder and a gcd, and its exponents are extracted by exact divisions, $\lambda$ of them, where $\lambda \le n$ is the sum of its exponents.

| Shared gcds | Symbolic | Schoolbook | Fastest |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Arithmetic per operation | $2M(n, u) + O(n) + \sum_i \big(D(n, s_i) + G(s_i)\big)$, plus exact divisions | $O(nu + ns)$ | $O(n \log u + e\, n \log s + s \log^2 s)$ |
| Structure per operation | $O(r)$, plus $O\big(M(u) \log r\big)$ if cofactors are not kept, plus $G(\pi) + D(\pi) + O(v)$ per refinement triggered | $O(r + u^2)$, plus $O(\pi^2 + v)$ per refinement | $O(r + u \log u \log r)$, plus $O(\pi \log^2 \pi + v)$ per refinement |
| Structure, one-time per new denominator | $(\tau + \lambda)\,D(n, \pi) + \tau\, G(\pi)$, plus refinement | $O\big((\tau + \lambda)\, n \pi\big)$ | $O\big((\tau + \lambda)\, n \log \pi + \tau\, \pi \log^2 \pi\big)$ |

**The central trade-off.** The refined method's first gcd has moved from the arithmetic row, where it is paid at every addition, to the one-time structure row, where it is paid once per new denominator. That one-time cost is not small. A new denominator may have to be tested against every base element it could share with, and without an index to narrow the candidates, $\tau$ is the whole base, $k$, which grows with the collection. So the method pays off when each denominator is reused in enough operations to amortise that cost: when the saving per addition, about $G(n) + M(n, u)$ less the structure cost per operation, times the number of additions that use the denominator, exceeds the one-time cost of bringing it in.

Compared with the refined method, the arithmetic loses the gcd on full denominators and the denominator product. What remains is the reduction against the equal-power shared part, and it shrinks with $s$.
- **Fastest model:** the order falls from $O(n \log^2 n)$ to $O(n \log n)$ or below whenever $s$ is small. With lazy reduction, the remaining term is deferred altogether.
- **Schoolbook model:** the cost is $O(nu + ns)$. It falls from $O(n^2)$ towards $O(n)$ when the denominators share most of their factors at *different* powers (small $u$ and small $s$), or when the equal-power reduction is made lazy.

**Example.** Over the base $(2, 3)$, $12$ has vector $(2, 1)$ and $18$ has $(1, 2)$. So $\alpha \vee \beta = (2, 2)$, the common denominator is $36$, and the cofactors are $3$ and $2$:

$$
\frac{1}{12} + \frac{5}{18} = \frac{1 \cdot 3 + 5 \cdot 2}{36} = \frac{13}{36}.
$$

No component is equal in the two vectors, so $\sigma = 1$, nothing can cancel, and no reduction is done: the result is already in lowest terms.

### 4.4 The spectrum

The arithmetic cost now depends on how much the operands share. The structure costs are those of Section 4.3.

| Sharing | What happens | Arithmetic: symbolic | Schoolbook | Fastest |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Everything ($\alpha = \beta$) | cofactors are 1; the sum is $(m_x + m_y)/p^{\alpha}$, the shared-denominator case | $O(n)$ with lazy reduction | $O(n)$ | $O(n)$ |
| Nothing ($\alpha \wedge \beta = 0$) | cofactors are the whole other denominator; the result is already in lowest terms, with no gcd and no reduction | $2M(n) + O(n)$ | $O(n^2)$ | $O(n \log n)$ |
| In between | cost falls as the shared part grows | $2M(n, u) + O(n) + \sum_i \big(D(n, s_i) + G(s_i)\big)$ | $O(nu + ns)$ | $O(n \log u + e\, n \log s + s \log^2 s)$ |

At the "nothing shared" end, the cofactors are the whole other denominator as an integer. If those integers are kept with the values, the arithmetic is $2M(n)$, below the identity's $3M(n)$, because the denominator itself is never multiplied out. If they have to be built from the base elements, that is a structure cost of about $M(n)$ more. Either way no gcd is needed at addition time to know that nothing cancels: this is the case $g = 1$ of Section 2.2, now known in advance.

The all-or-nothing choice of Section 3 becomes a matter of degree.

### 4.5 Multiplication

In lowest-terms arithmetic, a product is reduced by two gcds: each numerator against the *other* operand's denominator, $\gcd(a, d)$ and $\gcd(c, b)$, since nothing else can cancel [Henrici; Knuth, §4.5.1]. With shared gcds the denominators are exponent vectors, and those cancellations are found by reducing each numerator against the base elements, at their powers, of the other operand's denominator:

$$
x \cdot y = \frac{m_x\, m_y}{p^{\alpha + \beta}}, \quad \text{after cancelling } m_x \text{ against } p^{\beta} \text{ and } m_y \text{ against } p^{\alpha}.
$$

Cancelling $m_x$ against $p^{\beta}$ takes, for each base element with $\beta_i > 0$, a remainder modulo $p_i^{\beta_i}$, of $\beta_i \pi_i$ bits, and a gcd on that many bits; symmetrically for $m_y$ against $p^{\alpha}$. The sums below run over both. As with addition, a cancellation that divides a base element only partly refines that element (Section 5.3), at a structure cost during the operation.

| Multiplication | Category | Symbolic | Schoolbook | Fastest |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Lowest terms (two cross gcds) | arithmetic per operation | $2G(n) + 4D(n) + 2M(n)$ | $O(n^2)$ | $O(n \log^2 n)$ |
| | structure per operation | $O(1)$ | $O(1)$ | $O(1)$ |
| | structure, one-time | none | none | none |
| Shared gcds | arithmetic per operation | $M(n) + \sum_i \big(D(n, \beta_i \pi_i) + G(\beta_i \pi_i)\big)$, plus exact divisions | $O(n^2)$ | $O(r\, n \log n + n \log^2 n)$ |
| | structure per operation | $O(r)$, plus $G(\pi) + D(\pi) + O(v)$ per refinement triggered | $O(r)$, plus $O(\pi^2 + v)$ per refinement | $O(r)$, plus $O(\pi \log^2 \pi + v)$ per refinement |
| | structure, one-time | as for addition | as for addition | as for addition |

The denominator is a vector sum, $\alpha + \beta$, and is never multiplied out. For multiplication the gain is mainly in constant factors: the cancellations are found against known, usually small, base elements rather than by gcds on whole denominators.

## 5. Storing the shared gcds

Section 4 assumes that, when two values meet, their exponent vectors over a common coprime base are at hand. This section describes a storage that keeps them.

### 5.1 Why the base must be coprime

The rules of Section 4.2 hold only if the base elements are pairwise coprime. Over a base that is not coprime, both fail:
- **Uniqueness fails.** Over $(2, 4)$, the denominator $16$ is $2^4$, $4^2$ and $2^2 \cdot 4$, so it has no unique exponent vector.
- **The gcd and lcm rules fail.** Over $(4, 6)$, $4$ and $6$ have vectors $(1, 0)$ and $(0, 1)$, whose maximum $(1, 1)$ means $24$, while $\text{lcm}(4, 6) = 12$.

Factor refinement (Section 4.1) restores coprimality whenever two elements are found to share something.

### 5.2 The structure

The stored values and the base together form a weighted bipartite graph:
- **on one side, the values,** each carrying its integer numerator;
- **on the other, the base elements;**
- **an edge** from each value to each base element in its denominator, weighted by the exponent.

Equivalently, the denominators form a sparse matrix: one row per value, one column per base element, with the exponents as entries. Row $i$ is the exponent vector of value $i$, and alongside the matrix is a vector of numerators. Addition and multiplication of two values read two rows and the base elements in their columns, nothing else: this is the $O(r)$ structure cost per operation. Values with identical rows can share one row, which is the common-denominator representation of Section 3 inside this structure.

### 5.3 Building it incrementally

When a new denominator $d$ arrives, it is refined against the base:
- **The part of $d$ that shares nothing with the base** becomes new base elements.
- **When $d$ shares part of an existing element $q$,** that is, when $\gcd(d, q)$ is neither 1 nor $q$, the element $q$ is refined into $\gcd(d, q)$ and its residual, and the process repeats until the base is pairwise coprime again.
- **Every value that used a refined element** receives the corresponding exponents on its parts, so its denominator is unchanged. For example, when $15$ is refined into $3$ and $5$, an exponent $w$ on $15$ becomes $w$ on $3$ and $w$ on $5$.

This is where the refined method's first gcd is now paid. Testing $d$ against $\tau$ base elements costs a remainder $d \bmod p_i$ and a gcd on at most $\pi$ bits for each, $\tau\,\big(D(n, \pi) + G(\pi)\big)$ in all; extracting the exponents of $d$ costs $\lambda$ further exact divisions. Without an index that narrows the candidates, $\tau$ is the whole base, $k$, which grows with the collection. Each refinement adds a few gcds and divisions on the sizes of the elements involved, and an update of the rows that use the refined element. These costs are paid when a denominator first appears, not when values are added, and they pay off only if the denominator is then used in enough operations (Section 4.3). When the whole collection is known in advance, its coprime base can instead be built at once: in time quadratic in the total size with simple methods [BDS], and in essentially linear time with fast ones [Bernstein].

An arithmetic operation touches only its two operands and the base elements they use, so, apart from refinements it triggers, its cost does not depend on how many values are stored.

## 6. One method across the whole spectrum

The methods compared, for adding two values with $n$-bit numerators and denominators. Here $\gamma$ is the size of $\gcd(b, d)$; $u$ the size of the parts of the denominators that are not shared; $\ell$ the size of a shared denominator; $s$ the size of the part shared at equal power, made of $e$ elements of sizes $s_i$; $r$ the number of base elements two values use; $\pi$ the size of the largest base element; $v$ the number of values using a refined element; and $\tau$ and $\lambda$ the number of base elements a new denominator is tested against and the sum of its exponents.

| Method | Category | Symbolic | Schoolbook | Fastest |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Identity (§1) | arithmetic per operation | $3M(n) + O(n)$ | $O(n^2)$ | $O(n \log n)$ |
| | structure per operation | $O(1)$ | $O(1)$ | $O(1)$ |
| | structure, one-time | none | none | none |
| Direct gcd (§2.1) | arithmetic per operation | $3M(n) + G(2n) + 2D(2n)$ | $O(n^2)$ | $O(n \log^2 n)$ |
| | structure per operation | $O(1)$ | $O(1)$ | $O(1)$ |
| | structure, one-time | none | none | none |
| Refined gcd (§2.2) | arithmetic per operation | $G(n) + 2D(n, \gamma) + 3M(n, u) + D(2n, \gamma) + G(\gamma)$ | $O(n^2)$ | $O(n \log^2 n)$ |
| | structure per operation | $O(1)$ | $O(1)$ | $O(1)$ |
| | structure, one-time | none | none | none |
| Shared denominator (§3) | arithmetic per operation | $O(\ell)$ | $O(\ell)$ | $O(\ell)$ |
| | structure per operation | $O(1)$ | $O(1)$ | $O(1)$ |
| | structure, one-time (conversion of $N$ values) | $N\big(G(n) + 3D(\ell, n) + 2M(\ell, n)\big)$ | $O(N \ell n)$ | $O\big(N(\ell \log n + n \log^2 n)\big)$ |
| | structure, amortized (lazy reduction of $N$ values) | $N\,G(\ell) + (N + 1)\,D(\ell)$ | $O(N \ell^2)$ | $O(N \ell \log^2 \ell)$ |
| Shared gcds (§4) | arithmetic per operation | $2M(n, u) + O(n) + \sum_i \big(D(n, s_i) + G(s_i)\big)$ | $O(nu + ns)$ | $O(n \log u + e\, n \log s + s \log^2 s)$ |
| | structure per operation | $O(r)$, plus $O\big(M(u) \log r\big)$ if cofactors are not kept, plus $G(\pi) + D(\pi) + O(v)$ per refinement | $O(r + u^2)$, plus $O(\pi^2 + v)$ per refinement | $O(r + u \log u \log r)$, plus $O(\pi \log^2 \pi + v)$ per refinement |
| | structure, one-time per new denominator | $(\tau + \lambda)\,D(n, \pi) + \tau\, G(\pi)$, plus refinement | $O\big((\tau + \lambda)\, n \pi\big)$ | $O\big((\tau + \lambda)\, n \log \pi + \tau\, \pi \log^2 \pi\big)$ |

The shared-gcd method contains the others as special cases:
- **Values with identical exponent vectors** share one denominator exactly. They can be stored together in the common-denominator representation, as a block of numerators over one row of exponents, and added with plain integer arithmetic and lazy reduction. Nothing is lost on data that share one scale.
- **Values whose vectors share nothing** add by two multiplications and an addition, with the result already in lowest terms. Nothing is lost on unrelated denominators either.
- **Everything in between,** which neither the gcd methods nor the shared denominator serves well, costs in proportion to what the denominators do *not* share, plus a reduction limited to what they share at equal power.

What makes this possible is a change of storage: each denominator is kept not as an integer but as an exponent vector over a coprime base shared by all values, built and refined by gcds as the data require. The refined method's first gcd, which the gcd methods pay in the arithmetic of every addition, becomes a structure cost, paid once, when a denominator first appears. That cost grows with the base, so the method pays off when denominators are reused across enough operations to amortise it.

## Appendix A. Multiplication

An integer is stored in base $W = 2^w$, where $w$ is the word width: $x = \sum_{i=0}^{\mu-1} x_i W^i$ with digits $0 \le x_i < W$.

**Schoolbook multiplication** [Knuth, §4.3.1]. To multiply $x$ ($\mu$ words) by $y$ ($\nu$ words), accumulate each digit product $x_i y_j$ at position $i + j$:

```
z ← 0                                   (μ + ν words)
for i = 0 .. μ − 1:
    carry ← 0
    for j = 0 .. ν − 1:
        t ← z[i+j] + x[i] · y[j] + carry
        z[i+j] ← t mod W
        carry ← ⌊t / W⌋
    z[i+ν] ← carry
```

Since $t \le (W - 1) + (W - 1)^2 + (W - 1) = W^2 - 1$, every $t$ fits in two words. The algorithm performs $\mu\nu$ digit multiplications, so for an $n$-bit by a $u$-bit number it costs $O(nu)$, and $M(n) = O(n^2)$.

**Toward the fastest methods.** Each step below reduces the number of digit products by doing more additions.
- **Karatsuba's splitting** [Karatsuba]. For factors of at most $\mu$ words, let $B = W^{\lceil \mu/2 \rceil}$ and split each factor in halves, $x = x_1 B + x_0$ and $y = y_1 B + y_0$. Then
  $$
  xy = x_1 y_1 B^2 + \big((x_1 + x_0)(y_1 + y_0) - x_1 y_1 - x_0 y_0\big) B + x_0 y_0,
  $$
  which needs three half-size products instead of four. Applied recursively, the cost $C(n)$ satisfies $C(n) = 3C(n/2) + O(n)$, so $M(n) = O(n^{\log_2 3}) \approx O(n^{1.585})$.
- **Toom–Cook** [Toom; Cook]. Split each factor into $\kappa$ parts, read them as polynomials, evaluate at $2\kappa - 1$ points, multiply the values and interpolate; this gives $O(n^{\log_\kappa (2\kappa - 1)})$, with an exponent that tends to 1 as $\kappa$ grows.
- **FFT-based multiplication** [SS]. When the evaluation points are roots of unity, evaluation and interpolation at $P$ points take $O(P \log P)$ operations, using the fast Fourier transform. Schönhage and Strassen do this exactly, in a ring of integers modulo $2^{K} + 1$, where $2$ is a root of unity, and apply the method recursively to the pointwise products: $M(n) = O(n \log n \log \log n)$.
- **The $O(n \log n)$ bound** [HvdH]. Harvey and van der Hoeven remove the $\log \log n$ factor, using multidimensional transforms. $O(n \log n)$ is widely believed to be optimal, though no matching lower bound is known.

An $n$-bit by $u$-bit product, $u \le n$, is done as $n/u$ products of size $u$, giving $M(n, u) = O\big((n/u)\,M(u)\big)$, which is $O(n \log u)$ with the fastest method.

**Fastest known versus fastest in practice.** The asymptotically faster methods carry larger constants and overheads, so each wins only above a crossover size. Practical implementations switch by size: schoolbook for small numbers, Karatsuba and Toom–Cook variants for moderate ones, FFT-based methods for large ones [BZ, Ch. 1; Knuth, §4.3.3]. The $O(n \log n)$ algorithm wins only for numbers far larger than any used in practice. The crossovers depend on the machine and the implementation.

## Appendix B. Division

**Schoolbook long division** [Knuth, §4.3.1]. To divide $x$ ($\mu + \nu$ words) by $y$ ($\nu$ words), first **normalise**: shift both left so that the leading digit of $y$ is at least $W/2$; this does not change the quotient. Then produce the quotient one digit at a time, from the top:
1. Estimate the next quotient digit $\hat{q}$ from the leading two digits of the current remainder and the leading digit of $y$, capped at $W - 1$.
2. Subtract $\hat{q}\, y\, W^{i}$ from the remainder, where $i$ is the position of the digit.
3. If the remainder became negative, add $y\,W^{i}$ back and decrease $\hat{q}$ by one; repeat if needed.

Normalisation guarantees that the estimate exceeds the true digit by at most 2, so step 3 runs at most twice. Each digit costs $O(\nu)$ word operations, and there are $\mu + 1$ digits, so dividing an $n$-bit number by an $s$-bit one costs $O(s(n - s + 1))$, within $D(n, s) = O(ns)$ and $D(n) = O(n^2)$.

**Fast division: reduction to multiplication** [BZ, Ch. 1; Knuth, §4.3.3]. Compute an approximate reciprocal $z \approx 1/y$ by Newton's iteration for $f(z) = 1/z - y$:
$$
z \leftarrow z + z\,(1 - y z).
$$
If $1 - yz = \varepsilon$, the new error is $\varepsilon^2$, so each step doubles the number of correct bits. Running each step at only the precision it needs makes the total a geometric series dominated by the last step, a constant number of multiplications of size $n$. The quotient is then $\lfloor x z \rfloor$, corrected by at most a few additions or subtractions of $y$. Hence $D(n) = O(M(n))$. To divide by a smaller $s$-bit number, divide in blocks of $s$ bits: $D(n, s) = O\big((n/s)\,M(s)\big)$, which is $O(n \log s)$ with the fastest multiplication.

**Exact division.** When the divisor is known to divide exactly, as with the residuals $b/g$ and $d/g$ and the cancellations by $h$ in Section 2.2, there is no remainder to find or correct. For odd $y$, the quotient can be computed from the low end, as $x \cdot y^{-1} \bmod 2^{K}$ for any $K$ larger than the size of the quotient; even divisors first have their factors of 2 shifted out. This is cheaper than general division by a constant factor.

**Fastest known versus fastest in practice.** Newton-based division inherits the crossovers of the multiplication it uses, and has its own overhead, so schoolbook division remains faster for small and moderate divisors.

## Appendix C. The greatest common divisor

**Euclid's algorithm** [Euclid; Knuth, §4.5.2]. For integers $a \ge b \ge 0$:
```
while b ≠ 0:
    (a, b) ← (b, a mod b)
return a
```
- **Correctness.** Since $a \bmod b = a - qb$, the pairs $(a, b)$ and $(b, a \bmod b)$ have the same common divisors, so the gcd is unchanged at each step; and $\gcd(a, 0) = a$.
- **Termination.** The second entry is a non-negative integer that strictly decreases at each step, so it reaches 0.
- **Step bound.** Two steps at least halve the remainder: if $r_{i+1} \le r_i / 2$ this is immediate, and otherwise $r_{i+2} = r_i \bmod r_{i+1} = r_i - r_{i+1} < r_i / 2$. So there are at most about $2 \log_2 b$ steps. The worst case is consecutive Fibonacci numbers, which gives about $1.44 \log_2 b$ steps [Knuth, §4.5.3].
- **Bit cost.** A step with quotient $q_i$ divides numbers of at most $n$ bits at cost $O\big(n (1 + \log q_i)\big)$. Since $r_{i-1} \ge q_i r_i$, the product of all quotients is at most $a$, so $\sum_i \log q_i \le \log a \le n$. With $O(n)$ steps, the total is $O(n^2)$.

Recording the quotients gives the **extended** algorithm, which also returns cofactors $x, y$ with $ax + by = \gcd(a, b)$, at the same order of cost.

**Binary gcd** [Stein]. Using only shifts and subtractions: $\gcd(2a, 2b) = 2\gcd(a, b)$; $\gcd(2a, b) = \gcd(a, b)$ for odd $b$; and for $a > b$ both odd, $\gcd(a, b) = \gcd\big((a - b)/2,\ b\big)$. Each step removes at least one bit, so there are $O(n)$ steps of $O(n)$ cost: $O(n^2)$, with small constants on a binary machine.

**Toward the fastest methods.**
- **Lehmer's idea** [Lehmer; Knuth, §4.5.2]. The quotients of Euclid's algorithm depend mostly on the leading digits of the numbers. Run Euclid's algorithm on the leading word of each number only, accumulating the steps as a $2 \times 2$ matrix of single-word cofactors, and stop as soon as the leading words no longer determine the quotients. Then apply the matrix to the full numbers at once. Many full-length divisions are replaced by single-word operations and one matrix application. The order stays $O(n^2)$, with a much smaller constant.
- **The half-gcd recursion** [Schönhage; Möller; BZ, Ch. 1]. Lehmer's idea, applied recursively. The steps that reduce $n$-bit numbers to about $n/2$ bits are determined by roughly the top half of their bits. So compute those steps recursively from the top halves, as a matrix; apply the matrix to the full numbers, at a cost of $O(M(n))$; then continue in the same way on the reduced numbers. The cost satisfies $C(n) = 2C(n/2) + O(M(n))$, so $G(n) = O(M(n) \log n)$, which is $O(n \log^2 n)$ with the fastest multiplication. The cofactors come out of the same computation. The delicate point is the boundary, where the top halves may not determine the last few quotients and corrections are needed; Möller gives a simpler formulation that handles it.

**Fastest known versus fastest in practice.** A gcd is several times the cost of a multiplication of the same size at every scale. Binary and Euclidean methods suit word-sized numbers, Lehmer's method moderate sizes, and the half-gcd only large numbers, above a crossover that is typically well beyond that of fast multiplication. Practical implementations switch between them by size.
