# Collatz maps on a fundamental domain

Working notes, 6 November 2026. Nothing here is in the paper drafts or the catalog. Vocabulary and the maps $(\alpha, k, d)$ are those of `catalog.md` (F2, F5, F8, A1); the quotient results cited as A2 to A14 are in `catalog.md` Section 7 and `quotient.md`. Code: `fdbasics.py` (Sections 2 to 5), `fdsearch.c` and `fdrun.py` (the search), `fdsummary.py`, `fdexamples.py` (independent check of the examples), `fdint.py` (integers). Logs: `logs/fdbasics.txt`, `logs/fd_<F>_k<k>_n<maxnorm>.txt`, `logs/fdsummary.txt`, `logs/fdexamples.txt`, `logs/fd_examples_1e7.txt`, `logs/fdint.txt`. Nothing here is in Lean.

## 1. Summary

1. The positive integers are $\mathbb{Z}/\{\pm 1\}$, and Collatz's map is a map on them defined by a formula applied to the positive representative of each class. In general, choose a fundamental domain $F$ (one representative of each nonzero class of associates) and put $S_F(z) = \mathrm{rep}_F\big((\alpha z + d(z))/\pi^{v}\big)$ for odd $z \in F$, with $d$ depending on $z \bmod \pi^k$. Every such map is a map on $R/U$. The maps of our form that commute with the units are the special case in which the table commutes (Theorem 4.2), and for a fundamental domain such as a quadrant or a cone they are the only ones that come from a map of our form.
2. 3x+1 on $\mathbb{N}$ is the fundamental-domain map with $F$ the positives, modulus $2$ and digit $1$. It is the quotient of the commuting map $3z + \mathrm{sgn}(z)$, whose digit is not a function of $z \bmod 2^j$ for any $j$, and it is induced by no map of our form at any modulus (Corollary 4.3). On the other natural fundamental domain of the odd integers, $n \equiv 1 \pmod 4$, the same formula gives the map $3x \pm 1$, which is trivially a Collatz map (Section 4.3).
3. Two simplifications: $(\alpha, d)$ and $(u\alpha, ud)$ are the same map on $F$, so the associates of a multiplier give nothing new on any fundamental domain; and $(F, d)$ and $(uF, d^u)$ induce the same map on $R/U$. Conjugation carries the family on $F$ to the family on $\bar F$ with conjugate multipliers (Proposition 2.3).
4. The choice of $F$ matters. On the first quadrant $Q$ and on the cone $C$ ($-45^\circ < \arg \le 45^\circ$) there are Collatz maps on $\mathbb{Z}[i]/U$ at the first modulus $(1+i)^2$ with the smallest possible norm $5$: 16 of 968 maps on $Q$, 30 of 968 on $C$, verified to norm $10^6$ and the examples to $10^7$. On $C$ the smallest-digit table already works: $\alpha = 2+i$, digit $-1$ on the class of $i$ and $-i$ on the class of $1$ modulo $2$, with $[1]$ fixed and its mirror for $2-i$. On the primary elements ($z \equiv 1 \pmod{2+2i}$) every fundamental-domain map at modulus $(1+i)^k$, $k \ge 3$, is the quotient of a commuting map of our form, so there the answer is the old one: no Collatz map below norm 29, then the 5+2i map and its mirror (G26, G27), and at nominal modulus $(1+i)^2$ only maps that are really at $(1+i)^3$ and below the window (G48).
5. The window and the drift are unchanged on any cone, because $\mathrm{rep}_F$ preserves norms and a cone meets every residue class with the same density. On the primary domain the representatives all lie in one class modulo $\pi^3$, so the effective modulus is at least $\pi^3$ and the window must be read there (Section 5).
6. Counts of Collatz maps to $10^6$ (digits of bounded norm, Section 7): at $(1+i)^3$ (multipliers 3, $3 \pm 2i$), 245 of 43,923 on $Q$ and 210 on $C$, none commuting, and none with the smallest table, which is $U$; at $(1+i)^4$, 13 of the 112 smallest-digit maps on $Q$ and 18 on $C$, and with digits of norm at most 13, 1243 and 1342 of 28,672, among which only the 5+2i map and its mirror commute. The multiplier 3, the weakest for residue-class maps (G21), gives 113 Collatz maps on $Q$; the multiplier 5 gives none on any domain tested.
7. On $\mathbb{N}$ (positives, moduli 2, 4, 8, digits $|d| \le 15$, $|d| \le 31$ at modulus 2): only 3x+1 at modulus 2, only Barina's $7x \pm 1$ at modulus 4, and 25 Collatz maps at modulus 8, of which two commute with $-1$. So on $\mathbb{N}$ the fundamental-domain family adds 3x+1 and the least-positive-digit maps to the odd maps, and on $\mathbb{Z}[i]/U$ it adds Collatz maps at every modulus from $(1+i)^2$ on.
8. Prior art (Section 10): the quadrant is the standard set of representatives for Gaussian divisors (OEIS A078908 to A078911) and the domain of our drop-below-start checks; Joseph's and Kucinski's map, the REU maps and Siegel's Hydra maps are defined on all of $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ or on a cone that is invariant rather than a set of representatives; Monks's survey slides have a section on $\mathbb{Z}_2/\!\sim$ with $x \sim \Omega(x)$, $\Omega$ the Monks and Yazinski autoconjugacy (corrected; see `monks-slides.md`). No Collatz map defined on a set of representatives of $\mathbb{Z}[i]/U$ was found.

## 2. Definitions and basic facts

### 2.1 The family

*Setting.* $R$ is $\mathbb{Z}$ or $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ (the definitions work for any domain with finite unit group $U$ and a prime $\pi$ of residue field $\mathbb{F}_2$), $[z] = Uz$, and $R/U$ is the set of classes of nonzero elements (A1). Parity and norm are constant on classes.

*Definition 2.1.* A fundamental domain is a set $F \subset R \setminus \{0\}$ containing exactly one element of each class, or, for maps that act only on odd classes, exactly one element of each odd class. For $w \neq 0$ (odd, in the second case) $\mathrm{rep}_F(w)$ is the element of $F$ associate to $w$.

*Definition 2.2.* A fundamental-domain map is given by $F$, an odd multiplier $\alpha$, a modulus $\pi^k$ and a digit table $d$ on the odd classes modulo $\pi^k$ that meet $F$, with $\pi^k \mid \alpha f + d(f)$ for every odd $f \in F$. On odd $f \in F$,
$$S_F(f) = \mathrm{rep}_F\big((\alpha f + d(f))/\pi^{v}\big),\qquad v = v_\pi(\alpha f + d(f)),$$
and $S_F(f) = 0$ if $\alpha f + d(f) = 0$. When $F$ contains even elements one also has $T_F(f) = \mathrm{rep}_F(f/\pi)$ for even $f$ and $T_F(f) = \mathrm{rep}_F(\alpha f + d(f))$ for odd $f$; $T_F$ reaches the representative of the odd part of $f$ from every even $f$, so as for residue-class maps (F7) the Collatz property can be read off $S_F$. A fundamental-domain map is a Collatz map if every odd $f \in F$ has an iterate equal to $\mathrm{rep}_F(1)$ and $\mathrm{rep}_F(1)$ is periodic, which is Collatz's sense on $R/U$ (A4).

The map $[f] \mapsto [S_F(f)]$ is a map on the odd classes of $R/U$, and every map on $R/U$ arises from a fundamental domain by choosing representatives (A3). What is restricted here is the form: one multiplier, a digit read from the residue of the representative.

*Fundamental domains used.* On $\mathbb{Z}[i]$: the first quadrant $Q = \{a > 0, b \ge 0\}$; the mirrored quadrant $R' = \{a \ge 0, b > 0\}$, which is $i\bar Q$; the cone $C = \{a > 0, -a < b \le a\}$ ($-45^\circ < \arg z \le 45^\circ$); and, for odd elements, the primary elements $P = \{z \equiv 1 \pmod{2+2i}\}$, that is $a \equiv 1, b \equiv 0$ or $a \equiv 3, b \equiv 2 \pmod 4$. Each odd class meets $P$ exactly once because the four units lie in the four odd classes modulo $\pi^3$ (U6). On $\mathbb{Z}$: the positives, the negatives, and, for odd elements, $\{n \equiv 1 \pmod 4\}$. So on $\mathbb{Z}$ the positives and negatives are not the only fundamental domains (any choice of sign per class is one); they are the only ones that are cones, and they are exchanged by $z \mapsto -z$.

### 2.2 Symmetries of the family

*Proposition 2.3.* Let $F$ be a fundamental domain and $u$ a unit.

1. (Associates of the multiplier.) $(\alpha, d)$ and $(u\alpha, ud)$ define the same map on $F$. The tables of $u\alpha$ are exactly the $ud$, and smallest digits go to smallest digits.
2. (Rotating the domain.) With $d^u(z) = u\,d(u^{-1}z)$, the maps $(F, \alpha, d)$ and $(uF, \alpha, d^u)$ induce the same map on $R/U$.
3. (Conjugation.) With $\sigma$ complex conjugation and $\bar\sigma$ the induced involution of $R/U$ (A12), $\bar\sigma\,\bar S_{F,\alpha,d}\,\bar\sigma = \bar S_{\sigma F, \bar\alpha, \sigma d \sigma}$, and the norm sequence of $[z]$ under the second is that of $[\bar z]$ under the first.

*Proof.* (1) $u\alpha f + u d(f) = u(\alpha f + d(f))$, so the odd parts are associates and $\mathrm{rep}_F$ takes the same value. The class of $-u\alpha c$ is $u$ times the class of $-\alpha c$ and multiplication by $u$ preserves norms. (2) $\mathrm{rep}_{uF}(uz) = u\,\mathrm{rep}_F(z)$, and for $f \in F$, $\alpha uf + d^u(uf) = u(\alpha f + d(f))$, so $S_{uF}(uf)$ is associate to $S_F(f)$. (3) $\overline{(\alpha f + d(f))/\pi^v} = (\bar\alpha\bar f + \bar d(f))/\bar\pi^v$ and $\bar\pi = -i\pi$, so the conjugate of the odd part of $\alpha f + d(f)$ is associate to the odd part of $\bar\alpha \bar f + \sigma d\sigma(\bar f)$, and $\bar f \in \sigma F$. $\square$

*Consequences for the search.* By (1) it suffices to take one multiplier from each class of associates; associates of $\alpha$ never give different maps on a fixed $F$, unlike the residue-class case, where $2-i$ and $2+i$ at $(1+i)^2$ have opposite verdicts (R9, G5). This is the computation asked for in the task: `fdbasics.py` compares $(\alpha, d)$ and $(u\alpha, ud)$ on $Q$, $C$ and $P$ for random tables at $(1+i)^2$, $(1+i)^3$, $(1+i)^4$, 885,600 comparisons, no difference, and (2) for $F = Q$, 147,600 comparisons, no difference (`logs/fdbasics.txt`). By (2), the four rotations of a domain give one family of maps on $R/U$, so $Q$ stands for all four quadrants. By (3), conjugate multipliers are related through $\sigma F$:

1. $\sigma Q = \{a > 0, b \le 0\} = -iR'$, so by (2) the maps on $Q$ with multiplier $\bar\alpha$ are the mirrors of the maps on $R'$ with multiplier $\alpha$. $Q$ and $R'$ differ only on the odd elements of the axes (the classes of odd real integers are represented by $n > 0$ in $Q$ and by $in$ in $R'$), but that changes the digit read for those classes, so on $Q$ the conjugate multipliers $2+i$ and $2-i$ give different families (7 and 9 Collatz maps, Section 7.1). Running $R'$ reproduces the $Q$ counts with conjugate multipliers exactly, at every modulus run (Section 7.2), which checks (3) and the code.
2. $\sigma C = \{a > 0, -a \le b < a\}$ differs from $C$ only on the rays $b = \pm a$, which contain no odd elements. So on odd elements $\sigma C = C$, the family on $C$ is closed under the mirror, and $(\alpha, d)$ is a Collatz map on $C$ exactly when $(\bar\alpha, \sigma d\sigma)$ is. The search confirms this for all 1560 Collatz maps found on $C$ (`logs/fdsummary.txt`).
3. $P$ is closed under conjugation ($\bar z \equiv 1$ when $z \equiv 1 \pmod{2+2i}$, since $\overline{2+2i} = -i(2+2i)$).

Different tables give different maps on $F$ when the class where they differ meets $F$ in elements of large norm (Lemma 5.0 of `quotient.md` with $u = 1$), so counts of tables are counts of maps.

## 3. 3x+1 on $\mathbb{N}$ is a fundamental-domain map

Take $R = \mathbb{Z}$, $F = \{n > 0\}$, $\alpha = 3$, $k = 1$ and $d(1) = 1$. For odd $n > 0$, $3n + 1 > 0$, so $\mathrm{rep}_F$ does nothing and $S_F(n) = (3n+1)/2^{v}$ is the accelerated Collatz map. On all positive integers $T_F$ is Collatz's map. Checked for odd $n \le 2\cdot 10^5$ in `fdbasics.py`.

The commuting map with the same quotient (A3, with the positives as representatives) is $L(z) = \mathrm{sgn}(z)\,C(|z|)$, that is $L(z) = 3z + \mathrm{sgn}(z)$ for odd $z$ and $z/2$ for even $z$. It commutes with $-1$ and $|L(n)| = C(n)$ (checked for odd $|n| \le 2\cdot 10^5$). Its digit $D(z) = \mathrm{sgn}(z)$ is not a function of $z \bmod 2^j$ for any $j$: $1 \equiv 1 - 2^j \pmod{2^j}$ and $D(1) = 1 \neq -1 = D(1 - 2^j)$. So $L$ is not of our form, and neither is the non-commuting lift $3|z| + 1$ (A3, A13). Corollary 4.3 below shows more: no map of our form, at any modulus, induces 3x+1 on $\mathbb{N}$.

By contrast the formula extended to $\mathbb{Z}$ ($C(5) = 16$, $C(-5) = -14$) does not respect associates and is not a lift of any map on $\mathbb{N}$ (A13). In the language of this note, the Collatz problem is a fundamental-domain map at modulus 2, the first modulus, with multiplier 3, the only multiplier in the window there; Barina's $7x \pm 1$ is the first fundamental-domain map on $\mathbb{N}$ that comes from a map of our form (A14).

## 4. Which fundamental-domain maps come from maps of our form

### 4.1 The commuting extension

Given $(F, \alpha, k, d)$, define $D$ on odd $z \in R$ by $D(uf) = u\,d(f)$ for $f \in F$, $u \in U$, and $L(z) = $ odd part of $\alpha z + D(z)$. Then $D(z) \equiv -\alpha z \pmod{\pi^k}$ (since $D(uf) = ud(f) \equiv -u\alpha f$), $L$ commutes with the units, and $\bar L = \bar S_F$. So every fundamental-domain map is the quotient of a commuting map whose digit is read from the pair (residue of the representative, unit), as for $3z + \mathrm{sgn}(z)$. The question is when $D$ is a residue-class table.

### 4.2 The criterion

Consider two conditions on $F$:

(A) for every $f \in F$ and every $J$, $F$ contains at least two elements congruent to $f$ modulo $\pi^J$;

(B) for every $J$, every odd class modulo $\pi^J$ and every unit $u$, the set $uF$ contains an element of that class.

Quadrants and cones satisfy both (a quarter plane contains infinitely many elements of every residue class, in every direction). $P$ satisfies (A), since $P$ is a union of classes modulo $\pi^3$, and not (B): $uP$ meets only the classes $\equiv u \pmod{\pi^3}$.

*Theorem 4.1.* Let $(F, \alpha, k, d)$ be a fundamental-domain map with $F$ satisfying (A), and suppose a map $(\alpha', k', d')$ of our form induces the same map on the odd classes of $R/U$. Then $\alpha' = \varepsilon\alpha$ for a unit $\varepsilon$, $d' = \varepsilon D$, so $D$ is a function of $z \bmod \pi^{k'}$ with $\pi^{k'} \mid \alpha z + D(z)$, and the inducing map is a rotation of the commuting map $(\alpha, k', D)$. Conversely, if $D$ is a function of $z \bmod \pi^{k'}$ with $\pi^{k'} \mid \alpha z + D(z)$, then $(\alpha, k', D)$ is a map of our form that commutes with the units and induces $\bar S_F$.

*Proof.* The inducing map $S'$ respects associates, so it commutes with the units (A8), and $L$ commutes too; both induce $\bar S_F$, so for every odd $z$ the odd parts of $\alpha' z + d'(z)$ and of $\alpha z + D(z)$ are associates. Fix odd $z_0$, write $z_0 = uf_0$ with $f_0 \in F$, and take $J$ larger than $k$, $k'$, $v_\pi(\alpha z_0 + D(z_0))$ and $v_\pi(\alpha' z_0 + d'(z_0))$. For $z = uf$ with $f \in F$, $f \equiv f_0 \pmod{\pi^J}$, the digits $D(z) = u\,d(f_0)$ and $d'(z) = d'(z_0)$ are constant, and so are the two exponents $b$ and $a$, since $\alpha(z - z_0)$ and $\alpha'(z - z_0)$ are divisible by $\pi^J$. By (A) and induction (apply (A) at a larger $J$ to a second point, and so on) there are infinitely many such $f$; as $U$ is finite, two of them, $z_1 \ne z_2$, have the same unit $\eta$ in $(\alpha' z + d'(z_0))/\pi^a = \eta(\alpha z + D(z_0))/\pi^b$. Subtracting, $\alpha'\pi^b = \eta\alpha\pi^a$; $\alpha$ and $\alpha'$ are odd, so $a = b$ and $\alpha' = \eta\alpha$, and then $d'(z_0) = \eta D(z_0)$. Since $\alpha' = \eta\alpha$ determines $\eta = \varepsilon$, the unit does not depend on $z_0$, and $d' = \varepsilon D$ everywhere; $d'$ is a residue-class table modulo $\pi^{k'}$, hence so is $D$, and $\pi^{k'} \mid \alpha'z + d'(z) = \varepsilon(\alpha z + D(z))$. The converse is direct: $(\alpha, k', D)$ is then of our form, commutes because $D(vz) = vD(z)$ by construction, and its odd parts are those of $L$. $\square$

*Theorem 4.2.* If $F$ satisfies (A) and (B), the fundamental-domain map $(F, \alpha, k, d)$ is induced by a map of our form if and only if its table commutes, $d(uc) = u\,d(c)$ for all odd classes $c$ modulo $\pi^k$ and units $u$; and then it is induced by $(\alpha, k, d)$ itself. In particular, on $Q$, $R'$, $C$ (and on the positives of $\mathbb{Z}$) the quotient maps of the commuting maps of our form are exactly the fundamental-domain maps with commuting tables, and every other table gives a map on $R/U$ that no map of our form induces.

*Proof.* If such a map exists, Theorem 4.1 makes $D$ a function of $z \bmod \pi^{K}$, $K = \max(k, k')$. For a class $c$ modulo $\pi^K$ and a unit $u$, (B) gives $z \in F \cap c$ and $z' \in uF \cap c$; then $d(c) = D(z) = D(z') = u\,d(u^{-1}c)$, with classes reduced modulo $\pi^k$. So $d$ commutes, $D(uf) = u\,d(f) = d(uf)$, that is $D = d$, and the map is $(\alpha, k, d)$. Conversely, a commuting table gives $D = d$, a residue-class table modulo $\pi^k$ with $\pi^k \mid \alpha z + d(z)$. $\square$

The condition of the task, "the digit table, extended by $d(uz) = u\,d(z)$, is well defined and commutes", is the statement that $D$ is a residue-class table: well defined means that $u\,d(f) = u'\,d(f')$ whenever $uf \equiv u'f'$; under (B) that is the same as $d$ commuting, and for $P$ it is automatic (next subsection). So the residue-class maps that commute with the units are the special case of the family in which $D$ is a residue-class table, and the threshold of U1 ($k \ge 3$ in $\mathbb{Z}[i]$, $k \ge 2$ in $\mathbb{Z}$) is the first modulus at which the special case is nonempty. Below the threshold no table commutes (U4, U7), so on a quadrant or a cone every fundamental-domain map at $(1+i)^2$, and on the positives every map at modulus $2$, is new.

*Corollary 4.3.* No map of our form, at any modulus and with any multiplier, induces 3x+1 on $\mathbb{N}$. More generally, for $F$ the positives, a fundamental-domain map at modulus $2$ is never induced by a map of our form.

*Proof.* The positives satisfy (A) and (B); at modulus $2$ there is one odd class, so a commuting table would need $d(1) = d(-1) = -d(1)$, $d(1) = 0$, which is even. $\square$

The direct version of this argument for 3x+1 (along an arithmetic progression the identity $(m n + e)/2^a = \pm(3n+1)/2^b$ forces $m = \pm 3$ and a constant digit $e = m/3$, which cannot commute) gives the same conclusion without A8.

### 4.3 The primary domain

*Proposition 4.4.* Let $F = P$ in $\mathbb{Z}[i]$. Every odd $z$ is $uf$ with $f \in P$ and $u \equiv z \pmod{\pi^3}$ the unit in the class of $z$ modulo $\pi^3$. Hence $D(z) = u\,d(u^{-1}z)$ depends only on $z \bmod \pi^{\max(k,3)}$.

1. If $k \ge 3$, $(\alpha, k, D)$ is a map of our form that commutes with the units and induces $\bar S_P$; conversely every commuting table restricts to a table on $P$. The table on $P$ has one digit for each orbit of $U$ on the odd classes modulo $\pi^k$ (the class of the orbit that is $\equiv 1 \pmod{\pi^3}$), which is the parametrization of commuting tables in U1. So the fundamental-domain maps on $P$ at modulus $(1+i)^k$, $k \ge 3$, are exactly the commuting maps of our form, and by G26 (Lean) none with $N(\alpha) < 29$ inside the window is a Collatz map.
2. If $k \le 2$, all of $P$ lies in one class modulo $\pi^2$ and the table is a single digit $\delta$ with $\pi^2 \mid \alpha + \delta$. If $\pi^3 \mid \alpha + \delta$, the map is the commuting map $(\alpha, 3, \delta U)$ at modulus $(1+i)^3$; for $N(\alpha) = 5$ this is below the window, and the case $\alpha = 1 + 2i$, $\delta$ a unit, is G48, a Collatz map. If $v_\pi(\alpha + \delta) = 2$, then $v = 2$ for every $f \in P$ (because $\alpha(f - 1)$ is divisible by $\pi^3$), so $N(S_P(f)) = N(\alpha f + \delta)/4 > N(f)$ as soon as $|f| > |\delta|/(\sqrt 5 - 2)$, and every orbit from large norm increases forever.

*Proof.* The units are in distinct classes modulo $\pi^3$ and cover the four odd ones (U6); $uf \equiv u \pmod{\pi^3}$ for $f \in P$. Everything else follows from Theorem 4.1 and the computation of $v$. $\square$

`fdbasics.py` confirms that the commuting extension is a residue-class table for 40 of 40 random tables on $P$ at $k = 3, 4$, and for none of 40 random (non-commuting) tables on $Q$ or $C$. The search on $P$ (Section 7.4) reproduces exactly the predictions of Proposition 4.4.

The same holds on $\mathbb{Z}$ with $F = \{n \equiv 1 \pmod 4\}$: the extension is $D(z) = \pm\delta$ according to $z \equiv \pm 1 \pmod 4$, a commuting table modulo $4$. For 3x+1 ($\delta = 1$), $3n + 1 \equiv 0 \pmod 4$ on $F$, and the map is the commuting $3x \pm 1$ at modulus 4, below the window ($3 < 4$), which is a Collatz map for the trivial reason of A10 ($(3n \pm 1)/4 < n$ for $n > 1$); with $\delta = -1$, $v = 1$ always and orbits grow. So on the 2-adic fundamental domain the Collatz formula is the solved problem, and the Collatz problem lives on the archimedean one (`logs/fdint.txt`, last lines).

## 5. The window

$N(\mathrm{rep}_F(w)) = N(w)$, so the norm along an orbit of $S_F$ is the norm along the orbit of the commuting extension $L$: one odd step multiplies the norm by $N(\alpha)/2^{v}$ up to the digit, exactly as for a residue-class map. What can change is the distribution of $v$.

*On a cone.* For $F$ a quadrant or a cone, every residue class modulo $\pi^j$ has the same density in $F$, so the heuristic of W3 applies: $v - k$ is geometric with mean $1$, the drift is $\log_2 N(\alpha) - (k+1)$ bits per odd step, and the window is $2^k < N(\alpha) < 2^{k+1}$, as for maps of our form. So the window and the drift are the same for the whole family on $Q$, $R'$ and $C$; what the fundamental domain removes is the constraint that the table commute, and with it the threshold.

*On $P$.* All of $P$ lies in the class of $1$ modulo $\pi^3$, so at nominal modulus $\pi^2$ the exponent is $2$ on all of $P$ or at least $3$ on all of $P$ (Proposition 4.4(2)). The effective modulus is $\pi^{\max(k,3)}$ and the window must be read there; the norm-5 maps on $P$ are below the window of $\pi^3$, the Gaussian analogue of the $x+1$ problem (G48). For $k \ge 3$ the residues of $P$ are equidistributed among the classes modulo $\pi^j$ that are $\equiv 1 \pmod{\pi^3}$, and $v - k$ is again geometric with mean $1$: the window is unchanged, as it must be for commuting maps.

*On $\mathbb{Z}$.* The same: the positives are a cone, 3x+1 has drift $\log_2 3 - 2 = -0.415$ bits and lies in the window at modulus 2; on $n \equiv 1 \pmod 4$ the effective modulus is 4 and 3x+1 falls below the window.

## 6. Method

`fdsearch.c` reads a fundamental domain ($Q$, $R'$, $C$ or $P$), a bound $B$, a step cap, and a list of maps $(\alpha, d)$, with $d$ given on the eight odd classes modulo $4 = -\pi^4$ (enough for $k \le 4$). One step is $w = \alpha f + d(f)$, division by $\pi$ while even, and $\mathrm{rep}_F$ by rotating by $i$ until the point lies in $F$; arithmetic in 128 bits, escape when a coordinate exceeds $2^{60}$ (so norms stay below $2^{122}$).

Stage 1 (full classification, $B = 10^5$, cap $10^5$ odd steps): iterate from $\mathrm{rep}_F(1)$ and find its cycle by Brent's method; if the cycle does not pass through $\mathrm{rep}_F(1)$, the map fails ("$[1]$ not periodic"). Mark the cycle, then run every odd $f \in F$ with $N(f) \le B$ in increasing norm until it reaches a marked point (mark the path), closes a new cycle (fail, the cycle is reported), reaches $0$, escapes or hits the cap (fail). This is the Collatz property of Definition 2.2 for all classes of norm at most $B$.

Stage 2 (drop below start, $B < N(f) \le 10^6$, and for the examples to $10^7$): every odd $f \in F$ in the range must reach a point of smaller norm. Soundness on the quotient is the argument of V1 with norms of classes: if every class of norm in $(N_0, B]$ has an iterate of smaller norm and every class of norm at most $N_0$ reaches $[1]$, induction on the norm shows that every class of norm at most $B$ reaches $[1]$. A cycle whose least norm exceeds $N_0$ is caught because its least element never drops. In stage 2, 4 maps hit the step cap (the one rechecked, on $C$ with $\alpha = 2+i$ and digits $-3+2i$, $-4-3i$, has a cycle of 22 odd steps with least element $569-206i$ of norm 366,197, found again in Python), and 6 maps had a start escaping past $2^{60}$.

Digits: for each odd class $c$ modulo $\pi^k$ (one representative per class), all elements of the class of $-\alpha c$ with norm at most a bound, ordered by norm. Multipliers: one per class of associates (Proposition 2.3(1)), with conjugates: $2 \pm i$ at $(1+i)^2$; $3$, $3 \pm 2i$ at $(1+i)^3$; $4 \pm i$, $5$, $4 \pm 3i$, $5 \pm 2i$ at $(1+i)^4$ (W12). On $P$ only the classes meeting $P$ carry a digit. `fdrun.py` builds the tables, runs both stages and writes `logs/fd_<F>_k<k>_n<maxnorm>.txt`; `fdsummary.py` adds commuting counts and checks the mirror symmetry; `fdexamples.py` re-implements the map in pure Python and classifies the examples to norm $2\cdot 10^4$ (all agree). The total cost was a few minutes on one core; a run at $(1+i)^4$ with digits of norm at most 17 (458,752 maps per domain) was started and stopped as too heavy for this study.

### 7.1 The first quadrant

At $(1+i)^2$ the odd classes are those of $1$ ($a$ odd, $b$ even) and $i$; for $\alpha = 2+i$ the digit on the class of $1$ lies in the class of $i$ and conversely. With digits of norm up to 25 there are 16 Collatz maps. The first by total digit norm (6):

Then $S(1) = \mathrm{rep}_Q((2+i) - i) = \mathrm{rep}_Q(-i) = 1$ ($2 = -i\pi^2$), so $[1]$ is fixed. ### 7.2 The mirrored quadrant

$R'$ was run as a check of Proposition 2.3(3): at each of the three moduli its counts are those of $Q$ with $\alpha$ replaced by $\bar\alpha$. Before a bug was fixed (the search started the orbit of $[1]$ from $1$, which is not in $R'$; the representative is $i$) the $R'$ counts were all zero; the fix restored the mirror relation, and the same correction does not affect $Q$, $C$, $P$, which contain $1$.

### 7.3 The cone

The cone has the advantage that conjugation is a symmetry of its odd elements, so its family is closed under the mirror, like the commuting maps. At $(1+i)^2$ the smallest-digit table works:

$\alpha = 2+i$ on $C$, $d = -1$ on the class of $i$ and $d = -i$ on the class of $1$ modulo $2$ (digits of norm 1, total 2). $(2+i)\cdot 1 - i = 2 = -i\pi^2$, so $S(1) = \mathrm{rep}_C(-i) = 1$: $[1]$ is fixed. Sample orbits: $[7+4i]$: $7+4i$, $6+i$, $5+4i$, $6-3i$, $7$, $5-2i$, $3$, $2-i$, $1$ (norms 65, 37, 41, 45, 49, 29, 9, 5, 1); $[11+6i]$: $11+6i$, $11-8i$, $9+6i$, $4+i$, $3$, $2-i$, $1$; $[27+10i]$: $27+10i$, $23-22i$, $34-11i$, $39+6i$, $36+25i$, $33+10i$, $14+13i$, $20-7i$, $13-10i$, $9-2i$, $3-2i$, $4-i$, $4+i$, $3$, $2-i$, $1$. Every class of norm at most $10^7$ reaches $[1]$ (stage 1 to $10^5$, drop below start to $10^7$, longest 65 odd steps from $1800+1731i$, largest excursion 3.83 bits; independent Python classification to $2\cdot 10^4$, longest 24 odd steps). The drift is $\log_2(5/8) = -0.68$ bits per odd step. The mirror is $\alpha = 2-i$ with $d = -1$ on the class of $i$ and $i$ on the class of $1$, with conjugate orbits. Its commuting extension $L$ to $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ has $L(1) = -i$ ($\varepsilon = -i$), so by A6 the lift has one trivial cycle, the unit cycle $1 \to -i \to -1 \to i \to 1$, like the rotation $-2+5i$ of the 5+2i map (R11); but its digit is read from the sector of $z$, not from a residue class.

So on the cone, the Gaussian counterpart of 3x+1 exists in the most literal form: the first modulus, the least norm in the window ($5$, against $3$ for 3x+1), digits of norm $1$, one class $[1]$ fixed, a fundamental domain defined by the argument as $\mathbb{N}$ is by the sign. The other 28 Collatz maps on $C$ at $(1+i)^2$ use larger digits; of the 15 for $2+i$, 12 fix $[1]$ and 3 have $[1]$ on a 2-cycle (for instance $1 \to 2+i \to 1$ with digits $-3$ on the class of $i$ and $2+i$ on the class of $1$), and the 15 for $2-i$ are their mirrors.

At $(1+i)^3$ the first cone maps have total digit norm 8, for $3 \pm 2i$: $\alpha = 3+2i$ with digits $-i$, $i$, $-1$, $-1-2i$ on the classes of $i$, $-i$, $1$, $-1$ modulo $\pi^3$ (drop check to $10^7$: longest 344 odd steps from $2932+365i$, excursion 14.13 bits, as expected for norm 13 against 16); for $\alpha = 3$ the first has total digit norm 16 (digits $-2-i$, $-i$, $-1-2i$, $1-2i$). At $(1+i)^4$ the first for $4+i$ is $d = 1$, $-1$, $-i$, $-2-i$, $1+2i$, $-1+2i$, $i$, $2+i$ on the classes of $i$, $-i$, $1$, $1+2i$, $2+i$, $2-i$, $-1$, $-1-2i$ modulo $4$ (to $10^7$, 24 odd steps, excursion 0.88 bits).

### 7.4 The primary elements

The search on $P$ is a check of Proposition 4.4. At $(1+i)^2$, of the 44 single-digit maps exactly two are Collatz maps, $\alpha = 2+i$ with $\delta = i$ and $\alpha = 2-i$ with $\delta = -i$, the two with $\pi^3 \mid \alpha + \delta$ and $\delta$ a unit; they are the commuting maps $(2 \pm i, 3, U)$, below the window and contracting ($|S(z)| < |z|$ for $|z| > 1.69$, G48), with $[1]$ fixed. The tables with $v_\pi(\alpha+\delta) = 2$ escape, as predicted. At $(1+i)^3$ none of the 33 maps is a Collatz map, as G26 requires. At $(1+i)^4$ exactly the 5+2i map and its mirror pass among the smallest tables. So on $P$ the first Collatz map inside the window is the 5+2i map, norm 29 at $(1+i)^4$, and this is a theorem (G26 with Proposition 4.4) up to the verification of the 5+2i map itself (H8).

### 7.5 How the answer depends on $F$

1. On a cone (any quadrant, the mirrored quadrant, the $45^\circ$ cone) the first Collatz map on $\mathbb{Z}[i]/U$ is at norm 5, modulus $(1+i)^2$, the least norm and modulus possible inside the window (G1: no multiplier at $1$ or $1+i$). On $C$ it has smallest digits; on $Q$ it needs a digit of norm 5. There are Collatz maps at every multiplier norm in the window at $(1+i)^2$, $(1+i)^3$ and $(1+i)^4$ except the multiplier $5$, which fails on every domain tested ($Q$ and $C$, 4096 tables each, and the smallest tables on $R'$ and $P$; G38 found divergent-looking orbits for the commuting maps).
2. On the primary domain the family is the commuting maps, and the first inside the window is the 5+2i map.
3. Between the two: the quadrant and the cone give similar counts (16 and 30, 245 and 210, 1243 and 1342) but different individual maps; the smallest tables pass more often on $C$ (2 of 8 at $(1+i)^2$ against 0 of 8 on $Q$; 18 of 112 against 13 at $(1+i)^4$). On $Q$, conjugate multipliers have unequal counts (7 and 9, 99 and 33, 252 and 231, 347 and 368), because $Q$ is not symmetric under conjugation on odd elements; on $C$ they are equal.

### 7.6 Comparison with the 2−i family and with 5+2i

The 2−i maps $E_2$ (G5, G7) are residue-class maps at $(1+i)^2$ that are Collatz maps on $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ but do not respect associates (A9), so they define no map on $R/U$; and for them the associates of the multiplier matter (five of the eight associates and conjugates pass). The fundamental-domain maps at $(1+i)^2$ are maps on $R/U$ by construction, and for them the associate of the multiplier does not matter. The table of $E_2$ itself ($d = i$ on the class of $1$, $1$ on the class of $i$, $\alpha = 2-i$) fails on both $Q$ and $C$, with a 2-cycle through $2+i$; on $C$ the passing smallest table for $2-i$ is $d = i$ on the class of $1$ and $-1$ on the class of $i$. So the norm-5 Collatz maps of the two families are different objects: $E_2$ is a Collatz map on $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ whose quotient does not exist, the cone map is a Collatz map on $\mathbb{Z}[i]/U$ whose lift to $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ is not of our form.

The 5+2i map is in every fundamental-domain family (it commutes) and, with its mirror, it is the only commuting Collatz map among the tables searched at $(1+i)^4$, as in G28. On cones it is neither first nor alone: there are Collatz maps at norms 5, 9, 13, 17 and 25 below it, and 347 other Collatz maps with multiplier $5+2i$ on $Q$ with digits of norm at most 13. What the 5+2i map keeps is what Theorem 4.2 says: it is the first map in the window that is a quotient of a map of our form, exactly as Barina's $7x \pm 1$ is the first such map on $\mathbb{N}$ while 3x+1 comes before it as a fundamental-domain map. The parallel is exact: 3x+1 (modulus 2, multiplier 3) is to $7x \pm 1$ (modulus 4) on $\mathbb{N}$ what the cone map $2+i$ (modulus $(1+i)^2$, norm 5) is to the 5+2i map (modulus $(1+i)^4$, norm 29) on $\mathbb{Z}[i]/U$, with the difference that in $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ two moduli separate them, because the threshold is $(1+i)^3$ and $(1+i)^3$ carries no commuting Collatz map.

## 8. What changes and what does not

1. Below the threshold, the family is new, and these are the maps that answer the question of the task: maps on $R/U$ of Collatz's own form at lower moduli and smaller norms than 5+2i exist, at every norm in the windows of $(1+i)^2$, $(1+i)^3$, $(1+i)^4$ (5, 9, 13, 17, 25, 29); only the multiplier $5$ has none (norm 25 is reached by $4 \pm 3i$).
2. The price is the dependence on $F$: the choice of representatives is part of the map. In $\mathbb{Z}$ this choice is invisible because the positives are canonical (and, Section 4.3, the other natural choice turns 3x+1 into a solved problem). In $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ there is no canonical cone; the quadrant, the cone and their rotations are equally natural geometrically, and the number theorist's canonical choice, the primary elements, gives back the residue-class answer. Rotating the domain by a unit gives nothing new (Proposition 2.3(2)), but domains that are not unit rotations of each other ($Q$, $R'$, $C$) give different maps.
3. Nothing changes in the window, the drift, the verification method (drop below start on $F$ is the check we already ran for commuting maps), or the transfer theorem: a fundamental-domain map is a Collatz map on $R/U$ if and only if its commuting extension $L$ is a Collatz map on $R$ (A4 applied to $L$), so each Collatz map found here is also a Collatz map on $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ commuting with the units, with a digit that depends on the sector.

## 9. The integer check

`fdint.py`, $F$ the positives, $S(n) = |mn + d(n \bmod 2^k)|/2^v$ for odd $n > 0$, $m > 0$ in the window (by Proposition 2.3(1), $(-m, d)$ is $(m, -d)$), digits $d \equiv -mc \pmod{2^k}$ with $|d| \le 31$ at modulus 2 and $|d| \le 15$ at moduli 4 and 8; stage 1 every odd $n \le 10^5$, stage 2 drop below start to $10^6$ (`logs/fdint.txt`).

1. Modulus 2, $m = 3$, 32 digits: only $3x+1$ is a Collatz map on $\mathbb{N}$. The others have a second cycle ($3x - 1$: $5 \to 7 \to 5$; $3x + 5$: a cycle of 3 odd steps through 19; $3x + 11$, $3x + 13$, $3x + 17$, $3x + 29$, $3x - 5$, $3x - 7$, and so on), or reach $0$ ($3x - 3$, $3x - 9$, $3x - 27$), or leave $[1]$ ($3x + 3$: $1 \to 3$, fixed). This matches the literature on $3x + k$ (Lagarias) and Z3.
2. Modulus 4, $m = 5, 7$, 64 tables each: the only Collatz map on $\mathbb{N}$ is Barina's $7x \pm 1$, which commutes (A14). No table with $m = 5$ passes, so no $5x + d$ map on $\mathbb{N}$ with $|d| \le 15$ is a Collatz map.
3. Modulus 8, $m = 9, 11, 13, 15$, 256 tables each: 8, 9, 4 and 4 Collatz maps on $\mathbb{N}$, of which two commute with $-1$: $11x$ with $\pm 1$, $\pm 13$ ($d = 13, -1, 1, -13$ on $n \equiv 1, 3, 5, 7$) and $13x$ with $\pm 1$, $\pm 5$ ($-5, 1, -1, 5$), both found in Z24 among the commuting non-smallest maps. The others include the least-positive-digit map $9n + (8 - (n \bmod 8))$, digits $7, 5, 3, 1$, which never leaves $\mathbb{N}$ and is the map with a single positive cycle of Z28, and $15x$ with digits $-7, -5, -3, -1$ (the least negative residues). The smallest-digit commuting maps at modulus 8 have at least two cycles (Z14: none at modulus 8), so on $\mathbb{N}$, as on $\mathbb{Z}[i]/U$, the fundamental-domain family has Collatz maps at a modulus where the smallest commuting maps have none.

So the integer pattern is the Gaussian one: 3x+1 is a fundamental-domain map at the first modulus, outside the residue-class family; the first map of the residue-class family on $\mathbb{N}$ is $7x \pm 1$ at the threshold modulus 4; and the least-positive-digit maps of Z27 and Z28 are fundamental-domain maps (on the positives, with $\mathrm{rep}_F$ never needed), the natural generalization of 3x+1 in this setting. Unlike $\mathbb{Z}[i]$, modulus 4 on $\mathbb{N}$ has no fundamental-domain Collatz map other than the commuting one in the range searched.

## 10. Prior art

Quick search of 6 November 2026 (WebSearch and WebFetch with summaries by a small model, so fragments are short), added to what `rotation-prior-art.md`, `literature.md` and `quotient.md` Section 10 record.

1. The first quadrant as the set of representatives of Gaussian integers up to units is standard in number theory and in the OEIS: A078908 to A078911 (Sloane, January 2003) sum "distinct first-quadrant Gaussian integers dividing $n$", with the definition "$x > 0$ and $y \ge 0$; exactly one of $z, -z, iz, -iz$ lies in that quadrant". The primary normalization $z \equiv 1 \pmod{2+2i}$ is the standard choice for Gaussian primes (biquadratic reciprocity; not searched further). Neither is used for a Collatz map in the sources found.
2. Joseph (Fibonacci Quart. 36 (1998)) and Kucinski (Scranton preprint, 2000): Joseph's map is defined on all of $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ by residues modulo 2, dividing by 2; Kucinski's quadrant results are that the second and fourth quadrants contain no cyclic points (G54, `literature.md` 2.2). The quadrants there are regions of the plane where cycles do or do not lie, not a set of representatives, and the map does not respect associates. Monks's survey slides (monks.scranton.edu, "The 3x+1 Problem", talk slides) list Kucinski's 77 cycles and Joseph's map.
3. Monks's survey slides ("The 3x+1 Problem", monks.scranton.edu/files/talks/CollatzSurvey/Slides.pdf) have a section titled "Parity Neutral Collatz". Corrected after reading (`monks-slides.md`): the relation is $x \sim y$ iff $x = y$ or $x = \Omega(y)$, with $\Omega$ the Monks and Yazinski autoconjugacy of $T$ (Discrete Math. 275 (2004), 219 to 236), not $x \sim -x$; the induced map on classes is $T$ itself, and the Collatz conjecture is restated through the auxiliary map $\xi$ (x/2 on evens, $\Omega(x)$ on odds). It is prior art for the general idea of a Collatz map on a quotient by a symmetry, not for $\mathbb{Z}/\{\pm 1\}$ or for quotients by units.
4. Alvarado and the 2017 CSU Channel Islands REU (G3, `literature.md` 2.3): maps $(2+i)z + u$ at modulus $1+i$ on all of $\mathbb{Z}[i]$; the associates proposition (R14) relates the maps for different unit digits, with no reduction to representatives. The REU paper itself was not found (as before).
5. Siegel's Hydra maps on rings of integers (arXiv:2111.07882; `quotient.md` 10 item 4) live on a basis-dependent positive cone $\mathcal{O}^+$, an invariant subset of non-negative combinations of a basis. For $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ with basis $1, i$ that cone is the closed first quadrant, which contains associates ($1$ and $i$), so it is not a fundamental domain, and the maps preserve it rather than projecting to it. The page fetched gave only the abstract; the definitions are as recorded in `quotient.md`.
6. Lagarias's overview (arXiv:2111.02635) and his 1985 survey: no reduction of extensions to rings by units (as in `quotient.md` 10 item 3).
7. Polynomial analogues: Nichols (arXiv:1610.02545, 2016) works in function rings of curves over $\mathbb{F}_2$, where the polynomial ring has one unit; in $\mathbb{F}_q[x]$ with $q > 2$ the monic polynomials would be the fundamental domain, but the analogues we know of (Hicks, Mullen, Yucas and Zavislak, Amer. Math. Monthly 2008, over $\mathbb{F}_2$, from memory, not reread) do not need one. Not searched further.
8. Garg (Zenodo 2026, G56) partitions $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ by residues modulo $1+i$; no quadrants or associates on the page read.

Not found: any Collatz-type map on $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ (or another ring with nontrivial finite units) defined on a set of representatives of the classes of associates, or applying a normalization $\mathrm{rep}_F$ after each step; the observation that 3x+1 is the quotient of $3z + \mathrm{sgn}(z)$ and is induced by no residue-class map at any modulus (Corollary 4.3); Theorems 4.1 and 4.2 and Proposition 4.4; the cone map $2+i$. Not reached: the full text of Monks's "Parity Neutral Collatz" source; the REU paper; Kucinski beyond the earlier text extraction; Wirsching's book.
