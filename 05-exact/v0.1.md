# Exact: Unsigned Fixed-Width Rationals, Specification v0.1

Oct 27, 2026 · Andrew Emerson

This spec defines `Exact<uW>`, a family of data types for exact non-negative rationals in a fixed width, and the vector form in which arrays of them are computed. It records decisions already made in discussion and in the research of [04](../04-division-algebra-family/); nothing here is new design. Where a question is still open, it is listed under [Open decisions](#open-decisions) rather than settled.

**Relation to earlier specs.** `Exact` is the magnitude part of [Q v0.3](../03-q/v0.3.md) taken on its own: the same slash encoding, without the unit. Signs and the imaginary unit move to the next spec, which treats them as symbols acting on magnitudes and adds them in its own type. The scalar arithmetic, conversions and vector form here are what that spec builds on.

Changes from Q v0.3:
- **No unit in the tag.** The tag is the slash alone; `Exact` is unsigned.
- **No subtraction.** A difference of magnitudes is defined as the absolute difference; signed subtraction belongs to the next spec.
- **Any power-of-two width.** The family is defined for every word width from 16 to 65,536 bits, with the slash tag sized to the width.
- **The vector form stores magnitudes.** Phase one measured sign and magnitude kept apart as the best layout ([research](research/README.md)); this spec defines the magnitude column, and the next spec adds the signs.
- **Measured implementation rules:** one threshold between small and large routines (1,024 bits), 64-bit storage with geometric slack, and the multimodular IFMA kernel for matrix products.

## Purpose and scope

`Exact` is for computation that must not round. Its scalar is compact and every bit pattern is a value. Its speed comes from the vector form: arrays whose values share a denominator compute as dense integer arrays, which beat GMP and FLINT in every linear-algebra test and in every element-by-element operation measured (see [Evidence](#evidence)).

In scope: the scalar encoding and its family of widths, canonical form, arithmetic semantics, bounds computed from the slash, conversions from integers and floats, the vector form, and the implementation rules the research established.

Out of scope: signs and the imaginary unit (the next spec), rounding and approximation, syntax and language bindings, transcendental functions, and I/O formats.

## Notation

For a positive integer $n$, $\operatorname{bits}(n) = \lfloor \log_2 n \rfloor + 1$ is its length in binary, and $\operatorname{bits}(0) = 0$. A U$W$ is an unsigned integer of $W$ bits, numbered $W-1$ (most significant) down to 0. $W$ is always a power of two.

## The scalar

An **`Exact<uW>`** is a pair $(s, u)$: a **slash** $s$ and a U$W$ word $u$. The word is an unmodified unsigned integer; the slash is stored outside it and says how to read it:

$$
p = \left\lfloor \frac{u}{2^{W-1-s}} \right\rfloor, \qquad q = \left(u \bmod 2^{W-1-s}\right) + 1, \qquad x = \frac{p}{q}.
$$

The numerator $p$ is the top $s+1$ bits of $u$. The remaining $W-1-s$ bits store the **denominator minus one**, so a field of zeros means $q = 1$ and no pattern has a zero denominator. At $s = W-1$ the whole word is the numerator and the value is an integer.

**The slash tag.** $s$ ranges over $0 \ldots W-1$, so it needs exactly $\log_2 W$ bits. It is stored in the smallest whole number of bytes:

| Type | Slash bits | Tag | Spare tag bits | Largest value | Smallest positive value |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `Exact<u16>` | 4 | 1 byte | 4 | $2^{16}-1$ | $2^{-15}$ |
| `Exact<u32>` | 5 | 1 byte | 3 | $2^{32}-1$ | $2^{-31}$ |
| `Exact<u64>` | 6 | 1 byte | 2 | $2^{64}-1$ | $2^{-63}$ |
| `Exact<u128>` | 7 | 1 byte | 1 | $2^{128}-1$ | $2^{-127}$ |
| `Exact<u256>` | 8 | 1 byte | 0 | $2^{256}-1$ | $2^{-255}$ |
| `Exact<u512>` … `Exact<u65536>` | 9 … 16 | 2 bytes | 7 … 0 | $2^{W}-1$ | $2^{-(W-1)}$ |

Spare tag bits are zero in canonical form and ignored on decode. Their use is an open decision.

**Every pattern is a value.** All bit patterns decode; there are no reserved codes, infinities or not-a-number values.

**Zero.** When the numerator bits are all zero, $p = 0$ and the value is zero, whatever the rest of the pattern holds.

**Range.** A nonzero value $p/q$ in lowest terms is representable in `Exact<uW>` exactly when

$$
\operatorname{bits}(p) + \operatorname{bits}(q - 1) \le W.
$$

Examples at $W = 64$: $1/2$ has $p = 1$, $q = 2$, slash 0 and $u = 2^{63} + 1$. The integer 10 has $p = 1010_2$, slash 3 and $u = 10 \cdot 2^{60}$. $3/4$ has slash 1 and $u = 3 \cdot 2^{62} + 3$.

### Canonical form

The canonical encoding of a nonzero value has $p$ and $q$ coprime and $s = \operatorname{bits}(p) - 1$, so the numerator's leading 1 is bit $W-1$ of the word. The canonical encoding of zero has every bit of the word and the slash clear. Every value has exactly one canonical encoding, operations return canonical results, and equality of values is equality of canonical encodings. Non-canonical patterns are valid inputs and decode to their values.

### Storage

A scalar is a word and a tag, so `Exact<u64>` is 9 bytes. In arrays the tags are stored as a separate column from the words, so the words stay aligned; phase one measured this as fast as any other arrangement of word and tag ([S1](../04-division-algebra-family/research/phase1-variants.md#s1-value-layouts-a-b-c)). Arrays meant for computation use the vector form below instead.

## Arithmetic semantics

Every operation is exact and returns a canonical result.

- **Addition and multiplication** are those of the rationals.
- **Division** multiplies by the reciprocal $q/p$. **Division by zero is an error**; there is no infinite or undefined result to return.
- **Difference.** `Exact` is not closed under subtraction. The difference of two values is defined as the **absolute difference** $|a - b|$, returned together with their order ($a < b$, $a = b$ or $a > b$). Signed subtraction, which needs a sign, is defined by the next spec on top of these two results.
- **Comparison.** Values are totally ordered.

Henrici's algorithms are the recommended way to add and multiply. They take gcds of the smaller cross terms before multiplying, so intermediate values stay small. This is informative, not normative.

### Exactness and promotion

A conforming implementation never rounds, and has no rounding mode or inexact flag. A result that does not fit its operands' width is promoted to the narrowest `Exact<uW>` that holds it, as in Q v0.3; beyond the largest width it is an arbitrary-precision rational, whose representation this spec does not fix. Whether promotion should instead be fixed by the types (so that the result type of an operation never depends on values) is an open decision.

An approximation is a new value that a program asks for explicitly, through a function outside this spec.

### Bounds from the slash

The slash alone gives each operand's size, before any arithmetic:

- **Numerator length exactly:** in canonical form $\operatorname{bits}(p) = s + 1$.
- **Denominator length bounded:** $q \le 2^{W-1-s}$, so $\operatorname{bits}(q) \le W - s$.
- **Value range:** a nonzero canonical value lies in $[\,2^{2s+1-W},\ 2^{s+1})$.

These bounds decide, without computing, whether a product or a sum is certain to fit a width:

- **Product:** $\operatorname{bits}(p_1 p_2) \le \operatorname{bits}(p_1) + \operatorname{bits}(p_2)$, and likewise for the denominators.
- **Sum:** $\operatorname{bits}(p_1 q_2 + p_2 q_1) \le \max\!\big(\operatorname{bits}(p_1) + \operatorname{bits}(q_2),\ \operatorname{bits}(p_2) + \operatorname{bits}(q_1)\big) + 1$, with denominator $q_1 q_2$.

Reduction can only shrink a result, so a bound that fits is a proof of fit; a bound that does not fit means compute, reduce, then decide. Implementations use these bounds to choose a fast path and a result width in advance.

**Comparison from the slashes.** Two canonical values whose ranges above do not overlap are ordered by their slashes alone, with no multiplication. Whether this shortcut pays in practice has not been measured.

## Conversions

**From integers.** An unsigned integer of $K$ bits converts exactly to `Exact<uK>` (slash $\operatorname{bits}(p) - 1$, denominator field zero). A signed integer converts to its **absolute value**, which also fits `Exact<uK>`: $|-2^{K-1}| = 2^{K-1}$ has $K$ bits.

**From floats.** Every finite float is an integer times a power of two, so it converts exactly. The width is fixed by the type, large enough for every finite value of that type; the worst case is the smallest subnormals, which have the longest denominators:

| Source | Worst case, $\operatorname{bits}(p) + \operatorname{bits}(q-1)$ | Converts to |
| --- | --- | --- |
| f16 | 10 + 24 = 34 | `Exact<u64>` |
| bf16 | 7 + 133 = 140 | `Exact<u256>` |
| f32 | 23 + 149 = 172 | `Exact<u256>` |
| f64 | 52 + 1074 = 1126 | `Exact<u2048>` |

A float converts to its absolute value; $\pm 0$ converts to zero. **NaN and the infinities have no exact value**, and converting them is an error.

**From signed or complex values.** Converting any value that carries a sign or unit (a negative number, an imaginary or complex value from the next spec's types) gives its exact absolute value. For a single term of the next spec, a unit times a coefficient, that is the absolute value of the coefficient.

**Between widths.** Widening is always exact. Narrowing is exact when the value fits and an error otherwise.

**To floats.** Rounding to a float is an approximation, outside this spec.

### Compared with f64 (informative)

`Exact<u64>` with its slash outside the word, set against f64:

- **Range.** f64 spans about $2^{-1074}$ to $2^{1024}$; `Exact<u64>` spans $2^{-63}$ to $2^{64} - 1$.
- **Count.** `Exact<u64>` has about $2^{68}$ distinct positive values, against about $2^{63}$ for f64: more values, in a much narrower range.
- **Which doubles it holds exactly.** A double in $[2^E, 2^{E+1})$ with a full 53-bit significand has a denominator of $2^{52-E}$, and fits exactly when $E \ge 41$. Below that, the share that fits falls to about $2^{(E-41)/2}$: about 1 in 1.5 million doubles near 1. Over all positive doubles, about 1% fit.
- **Spacing.** f64's relative spacing is a uniform $2^{-52}$. `Exact<u64>`'s is uneven: near fractions with small denominators the gaps are as large as about $2^{-32}$, elsewhere far smaller than f64's.

`Exact` is not a replacement for f64. It is for values that must stay exact, of moderate size, with moderate denominators.

## The vector form

An array for computation is stored in the **vector form**: one shared denominator $d \ge 1$ and, per element, a non-negative integer numerator $m_i$. Element $i$ is $m_i / d$.

- **Signs.** The vector form here is the magnitude column of a sign-and-magnitude layout. The next spec attaches signs as a separate column, which phase one found fastest (two's complement, as in Q v0.3, is replaced).
- **Canonical form.** $\gcd(d, m_1, \ldots, m_n) = 1$. An all-zero vector has $d = 1$.
- **Width.** All numerators of a vector have one width. The vector widens as a whole to the narrowest width a proven bound allows, and narrows when every element fits.
- **Storage.** Numerators are stored in multiples of 64 bits, and a growing vector reserves 1.5× the width it needs.
- **Conversion.** Scalars with different denominators convert over the least common multiple of their denominators; a vector converts back by reducing each element.
- **Operations.** Equality, ordering and results are those of the values; the layout changes only speed.
  - Addition aligns two vectors to a common denominator, then adds integers, with no gcd.
  - Multiplication multiplies integers over $d \cdot d'$.
  - Sums and dot products are integer sums.
  - Overflow is detected, never wrapped: a result is proven to fit from the operands' bit lengths, or recomputed at the wider width.
  - After arithmetic the vector is reduced by the common factor $g$ of $d$ and all elements. The division is exact, so it may shift out the power of two in $g$ and multiply by the inverse of its odd part (Jebelean's exact division).

### Implementation rules

These are the rules the research measured as fastest. They do not change any result.

- **One threshold.** Up to and including 1,024 bits, numerators use the **small set**: the vector's length in limbs is a compile-time constant, with GMP's assembly kernels where those are faster. Above 1,024 bits they use the **large set**: the length is read at run time, and arithmetic uses GMP's `mpn` kernels (`mpn_mul_n`, `mpn_mul_1`, `mpn_addmul_1`, `mpn_submul_1`, `mpn_divexact_1`). Compare, zero test and negate are inline C.
- **Matrix products** use the multimodular IFMA kernel: 50-bit primes, AVX-512 IFMA, BLIS-style blocking. Where IFMA is missing, the bounded-memory float kernel (`mmgc`) is used. This is a portability condition, not a size condition.
- **Linear solves** use multimodular Cramer on the same IFMA residues. Matrix products and solves need signs, so they apply once the next spec adds them; they are listed here because they are kernels on this layout.
- **Residues** are not a storage representation. They are used only inside matrix products and solves.

## Determinism and platform requirements

Results are bit-identical on every platform: arithmetic is exact and canonical form is unique. A conforming implementation needs only 64-bit integer arithmetic and a 64 × 64 → 128-bit multiply. The fast matrix kernel needs AVX-512 IFMA; without it, results are the same and slower.

## Evidence

All measurements are in [04](../04-division-algebra-family/), summarised here; [research/README.md](research/README.md) maps each decision to its source. One thread on one machine (2-core Xeon with AVX-512 and IFMA).

- **The threshold** ([threshold test](../04-division-algebra-family/research/threshold-storage-libraries.md#1-the-threshold-test)): at exactly 1,024 bits, the complete small set against the complete large set, 12 paired rounds with a null control and a rule fixed before the run. Result: a tie, leaning small by 3–4%, with no operation favouring the large set; so 1,024 is the last small size.
- **Storage** ([storage granularity](../04-division-algebra-family/research/threshold-storage-libraries.md#2-storage-granularity)): 64-bit multiples were fastest. Byte multiples saved about 4% of memory and cost 6–8% in speed; powers of two wasted about 25% of memory. Limbs with 1.5× slack were the fastest growth policy (7–17% faster than exact limbs).
- **Against GMP and FLINT, element by element** ([phase two](../04-division-algebra-family/research/threshold-storage-libraries.md#element-by-element)): the vector form was fastest or tied in every operation at every width, from 1.5–5× at 128–256 bits to near parity (0.95–1.42×) at 2,048, where both run GMP's kernels.
- **Linear algebra:** the vector form with the IFMA kernel was faster in every cell: dense products 1.5–5.4×, solves 2.3–6.5×, matrix-vector and sparse products up to 3.7× and 10× at small widths.
- **Single values** ([single values](../04-division-algebra-family/research/threshold-storage-libraries.md#single-values)): a packed scalar was 5–35% slower than FLINT's `fmpq` at add and multiply, and 1.1–7.5× faster than GMP's `mpq_t`. The scalar's advantage is compact storage, not speed.

The comparisons were posed in the format's favour in ways listed in the research note (one shared denominator, exact division by a known factor, widths fixed in advance). The code and results are in [04's test folder](../04-division-algebra-family/test/).

## Prior art

The encoding is a **floating-slash rational** (Matula and Kornerup, "Finite Precision Rational Arithmetic: Slash Number Systems", *IEEE Trans. Computers*, 1985). Storing the denominator minus one, so that every pattern is a value, is carried over from Q v0.3. Henrici's algorithms, Jebelean's exact division, GMP, FLINT, and Ozaki-style exact matrix products on floating-point hardware are the other components, as reviewed in [Q v0.3](../03-q/v0.3.md#prior-art).

## Open decisions

- **Promotion.** By value, as now, or fixed by the types.
- **A hidden bit.** The leading bit of a canonical nonzero numerator is always 1, so it need not be stored, which gives one more data bit. Zero would then need a reserved pattern.
- **Spare tag bits.** At widths below 256 the tag has spare bits (4 at u16, 2 at u64). They could carry a sign or other tags for the next spec, or stay reserved.
- **NaN and the infinities.** Conversion is an error now. A reserved pattern is the alternative; at u256 the tag has no spare bits, so it would have to be a reserved slash value.
- **The f64 width.** `Exact<u2048>` covers every double but lands above the threshold. A width chosen from the value's actual range, with promotion, is the alternative.
- **Single-value speed.** The scalar loses arithmetic speed to FLINT. Whether to accept that (its advantage is storage) or change the scalar is open.
- **The widening rule.** Whole-vector widening is the rule. Phase one found per-element promotion faster when only a few values are wide (up to 2.4× at 0.1% wide); choosing by the share of wide values, which the bit-length scan already measures, is the alternative.
- **Errors in arrays.** Whether division by zero fails a whole array operation or reports the failing elements, as left open in Q v0.3.

## Reopen triggers

- **The threshold:** rerun the threshold test whenever the routines that decide it improve: the small set at 1,024 bits, or GMP's multiplication above it.
- **Storage:** revisit byte granularity if memory becomes the constraint, or if a multiply that handles a partial top limb removes its speed cost.
- **The solve:** test larger systems against Dixon lifting before claiming the solve generally; FLINT's Dixon method scales better with size.
