# Weilert, Math. Comp. 75 (2006) 941–981, read against our work (Nov 4, 2026)

A. Weilert, "Two efficient algorithms for the computation of ideal sums in quadratic orders", Math. Comp. 75, no. 254 (2006), 941–981, electronically published December 8, 2005; received July 20, 2003, revised January 7, 2005. Read in full from a 41-page JSTOR screenshot PDF (PDF page k is journal page 940 + k). Page numbers below are journal pages. Quotations are exact, with mathematical symbols transcribed in plain text where needed. "Our draft" is `papers/weilert-hensel.md`; "our recursion note" is `research/weilert-adic/recursive.md`.

## 1. Summary

The paper is entirely top-down. Algorithm 3.16 (SGCD) is a Schönhage-style controlled Euclidean descent, generalised to "S-euclidean" steps s·x = qy + r with s in a finite set S of positive integers, followed by a correction of the result at the finitely many primes dividing elements of S. Algorithm 4.2 (IDEALSUM) computes a Hermite normal form and then reduces a binary quadratic form with Schönhage's fast reduction. Both run in O(μ(n) log n). The paper contains no binary, (1+i)-adic, Hensel-quotient or valuation-driven division, no residue symbol computation, and no discussion of why bottom-up methods are not used. It cites Stehlé and Zimmermann only as another fast integer gcd. It anticipates none of steps H and S, the mod-16 observation, or the bottom-up recursion with the symbol. The complexity O(μ(n) log n) for a gcd in Z[i] is known, but from Weilert's top-down ANTS-IV paper [39], which this paper restates and generalises.

## 2. Algorithm 3.16 (SGCD) and the "valuation step"

2.1 The framework (pp. 947–948). Definition 2.1: R is S-euclidean with respect to f if, among other conditions, "(S2) There exist q ∈ R and s ∈ S for all x, y ∈ R \ {0} with f(s·x − qy) < f(y)." An S-euclidean step is s·x = qy + r with f(r) < f(y) (Definition 2.3, p. 948). The step matrices are Q_j = (0 1; s_j −q_j) (equation (2.2), p. 948), and the cofactor bounds of Proposition 2.13 (p. 950) are archimedean, in terms of f(x_0/x_j).

2.2 The fast descent (Algorithm 2.19 DESCENT_R, pp. 955–956). The descent is controlled by size: it stops when "min(‖u − v‖, ‖u + v‖) < σ" (Theorem 2.14, p. 951). For large operands it splits into heads and tails: "If the operands have large size, we split them into heads and tails w.r.t. a fixed chosen basis b ∈ R \ (R^× ∪ {0}) using a remainder-division for each of them, and we calculate an S-euclidean descent only with the heads (i.e., the calculated quotient of the remainder-division)" (p. 954). For the imaginary quadratic orders, "Thereby we chose b = 2 (B = 2) as the basis" (p. 964). The heads are the quotients x'' in x = x''·b^T + x', that is, the high digits. The algorithm makes two recursive calls, (D4) and (D6), with fix-up loops "(at most 2 times)" in (D5) and "(at most ℓ_R times)" in (D9) (pp. 955–956). Its lineage is stated on p. 954: "The algorithm is based on Lehmer's ideas that were improved by Knuth [18] and Schönhage [28, 29] for an asymptotically fast GCD calculation in Z. This concept, which does not need special properties of Z, was transferred to Z[i] [39], and is now generalized for S-euclidean domains." Footnote 2 (p. 951) adds: "See also [29, Theorem 2.1] for a controlled euclidean descent in Z, [39] for it in Z[i], and [30] for binary quadratic forms." The cost recurrence is (2.29), t(C'_R·2n, 2n) ≤ 2·t(C'_R·n, n) + O(μ̂_R(2n(1 + C'_R))) (p. 961).

2.3 SGCD itself (Algorithm 3.16, p. 968). Steps: (S1) and (S2) remove the integer content of x and y; (S3) "Calculate an asymptotically fast controlled S_D-euclidean descent on x' and y' (Algorithm 2.19 DESCENT_{O_D}(x', y', 0)) returning g ∈ O_D (cofactor matrix is not required)"; (S4) and (S5) pass to primitive ideals as Z-modules; (S6) for each prime p in V(∏S_D), compute p-valuations with Algorithm 3.14 and adjust g' by composition of binary quadratic forms; (S7) restore the integer content. Algorithm 3.17 (SGCD1, p. 968) is the class number 1 version on ring elements. Theorem 3.18 (p. 969) gives O(μ_{O_D}(n) log n) for both.

2.4 The valuation step. On p. 942: "Apart from the initial calculation of an S-euclidean descent we compute the valuation of ideals at finitely many places, where the places are only depending on the chosen order, and not on the operands. Due to this valuation step our novel Algorithm 3.16 is not very closely related to the concept of a euclidean domain." On p. 951: "The GCD calculation for imaginary quadratic maximal orders consists of the computation of such an asymptotically fast S-euclidean descent and the subsequent valuation at finitely many certain prime places." The reason is that an S-euclidean step multiplies x by s, which can introduce prime factors of s into the remainder sequence. Lemma 2.16 and Proposition 2.18 (pp. 953–954) show the descent gives the right gcd at every prime outside V(∏S), defined in Definition 2.15 (p. 953) as the primes dividing some s ∈ S. The proof of Theorem 3.18 says: "The calculation of the part of the GCD at the places p ∈ V(∏S_D) is done with valuations which removes an S_D part due to the S_D-euclidean descent from the calculated ideal, if necessary" (p. 969). The valuations are computed by Algorithm 3.14 (p. 967) from powers of p built by composition of forms.

2.5 Comparison with steps H and S. The valuation step is a post-processing correction at a fixed finite set of primes. It plays no part in choosing quotients. It is the S-euclidean counterpart of the spurious-factor pass in Sorenson's k-ary reduction, which our draft contrasts with step H after Lemma 1 (step H multiplies only y, so it needs no such pass). It is not like step H or step S: those use the (1+i)-adic valuation of the operands to choose each quotient, and the primes involved are those over 2, not the primes dividing elements of S. For Z[i] the valuation step is empty. Remark 3.2 (p. 961) says O_D "is norm-euclidean for D ∈ {−3, −4, −7, −8, −11} such that one can choose S = {1}", and Definition 3.3 (p. 962) gives S_D = {1} for these D. Then V(∏S_D) is empty and SGCD1 reduces to the descent of [39].

## 3. Algorithm 4.2 (IDEALSUM)

Algorithm 4.2 (p. 973): (I0) code I and J as integer matrices with two rows; (I1) compute the HNF H of I + J, by Hafner and McCurley [13, Theorem 2.1] in O(μ(N) log N); (I2) and (I3) write I + J = C'·(AZ + ((−B + √D)/2)Z) and form the quadratic form g = (A, B, *); (I4) reduce g "using Schönhage's fast algorithm for the reduction of a binary quadratic form [30] and at most four further reduction steps"; (I5) recover the factor s = C'·(δ − γ(b + √D)/(2a)) from the reduction matrix. Theorem 4.3 (p. 973) gives O(μ(N) log N) with N = n + size(ω). Form reduction is archimedean (Definition 1.1 and Theorem 1.2, pp. 946–947), so IDEALSUM is top-down too. It works in any quadratic order, real or imaginary, but for D > 0 the generator of a principal ideal can be exponentially long (Example 4.5, pp. 977–978). Neither algorithm has a bottom-up ingredient.

## 4. Z[i], quotient sequences, matrices, residue symbols

4.1 Z[i]. The paper has no algorithm specific to Z[i]. Z[i] appears as O_{−4} in Table 1 (p. 965), with E(R, |·|) = 1/√2, E_R = 1/√2, E'_R = 0.8536, S_R = 1, C_R = 1, m_R = 14.5105, γ_R = 5, δ_R = 2, ℓ_R = 7. Their δ_R is the splitting margin of (2.21) (p. 954), unrelated to our precision parameter δ; the clash of names should be kept in mind when citing.

4.2 Quotient sequence and matrices. The descent is a matrix recursion. It produces the product M of step matrices Q̂_j (p. 952) and is a Schönhage half-gcd in structure (two recursive calls on heads, then fix-up steps). Algorithms 3.16 and 3.17 discard the matrix: "(cofactor matrix is not required)" (p. 968). The paper says the Z[i] version gives a quotient sequence (p. 943, quoted in 5.2).

4.3 Residue symbols. None is computed or carried. The only symbols used are the classical Legendre/Jacobi symbols (D/p), to classify primes as inert, ramified or split (p. 945, Lemma 3.12 on p. 966, Algorithm 3.14 on p. 967). The quartic symbol appears only as a citation of [41] (p. 943, quoted in 5.2). The paper never mentions the quadratic, cubic or quartic residue symbol as something its algorithms produce.

## 5. Binary, (1+i)-ary, Hensel, 2-adic and Stehlé–Zimmermann remarks

5.1 Every passage that touches these subjects is listed here. There is no remark on why a bottom-up method is or is not used for a fast algorithm.

- p. 942, Stein: "Another GCD algorithm is Stein's binary algorithm [36] (or, [19, Section 4.5.2, Algorithm B]) that uses only addition, subtraction and shifting (division by powers of two). This algorithm has a running time of O(n^2) if the inputs are n-bit integers. There exist many further improvements to these algorithms, but none of these achieve a nearly linear running time."
- p. 943, Stehlé and Zimmermann: "Recently, Stehlé and Zimmermann [35] presented another asymptotically fast GCD algorithm for integers with the same asymptotical running time. It would be nice to also have a comparison of its computation time with Schönhage's implementation [31, Sections 1.3.6, 6.1.3]." Nothing says that [35] is binary or that it could be transferred to Z[i]. This is the only mention of [35]. Read with the sentence from p. 942 above, it is mildly inconsistent: the binary line is said to have no nearly linear algorithm, and [35], which is one, is mentioned only as a fast integer gcd.
- p. 943, the (1+i)-ary algorithm: "We were able to specify an analogue to the binary algorithm for the Gaussian integers [38] that achieves a quadratic running time. Instead of the prime number 2 in case of the binary algorithm, we use powers of the prime element 1 + i in order to reduce the operands. Based on the practical running time tests, Collins [11] presented a faster GCD algorithm for Z[i] with quadratic running time than the so-called (1 + i)-ary algorithm. It calculates euclidean steps, but not necessarily least remainder euclidean steps, and in this sense, his algorithm is called "approximative"."
- p. 943, fast gcd and the symbol: "Furthermore, Schönhage's asymptotically fast GCD algorithm for integers can be transferred to the ring of Gaussian integers [39] such that there exists a GCD algorithm in Z[i] with running time O(μ(n) log n), if the Gaussian numbers have length O(n). This algorithm can also be used to calculate a quotient sequence and cofactors, from which we are able to compute the biquadratic residue symbol of the Gaussian numbers in linear time [41]."
- p. 965, Theorem 3.9 "(Hensel lifting)", with proof by [37, Algorithm 15.10, Theorem 15.11, Theorem 15.12], and footnote 3 (p. 967), which lifts a solution of x^2 − D ≡ 0 mod 4p to accuracy 4p^{k_max} "using Hensel-lifting". This is Hensel lifting of a factorisation, used to write p^k as a Z-module for the valuation step. It is not Hensel (low-digit) division and has nothing to do with quotients.

5.2 The words 2-adic, LSB, Hensel division, k-ary, Sorenson and Jebelean do not occur. The JNT 2002 remark quoted in our draft ("We are not able to use a GCD calculation in Z[i] similar to the binary GCD algorithm ...") is not repeated here, and nothing in this paper retracts or extends it. The p. 943 passage confirms its premise from the other side: the symbol in [41] is computed from the quotient sequence of the top-down algorithm [39].

5.3 "Linear time" on p. 943 is Weilert's own wording for [41]. In context it must mean up to logarithmic factors, since it rests on the O(μ(n) log n) gcd. Our draft's "n^{1+o(1)}" is consistent with it, but if the draft quotes Weilert it should quote this wording and say how it is read.

## 7. Verdict

(a) Step H: not anticipated. No division in the paper uses low digits or an inverse modulo a power of 2 or of 1 + i. The heads-and-tails split with b = 2 (pp. 954, 964) keeps the high part. The only "Hensel" is lifting a square root of D (p. 965, p. 967 footnote 3).

(b) Step S: not anticipated. The only valuations in the paper are p-valuations of ideals at the primes dividing elements of S, computed after the descent to correct the gcd (pp. 942, 951, 967–969). They do not drive quotients, and for Z[i] (S = {1}) there are none. Stehlé–Zimmermann [35] is cited (p. 943) only as a fast integer gcd, with no mention of its binary character or of a Gaussian analogue.

(c) The mod-16 observation (Lemma 3 of the draft): not anticipated. The paper computes no residue symbol. It mentions the biquadratic symbol once, as a consequence of [39] through [41] (p. 943).

(d) The recursive bottom-up gcd with the symbol carried along: not anticipated. The paper's recursion (Algorithm 2.19, pp. 955–956) is top-down, needs fix-up loops of bounded length (D5, D9), and has only an approximate head-versus-full-operand lemma (Lemma 2.22, p. 959, with reduction factor E'_R = (1 + E_R)/2 instead of E_R). Our truncation lemma instead gives identical quotients. The matrix is discarded in SGCD (p. 968), and no symbol is carried.

(e) O(M(n) log n) for a bottom-up method: partly, in the sense that the complexity class is known for Z[i] but only by a top-down method. Page 943 attributes an O(μ(n) log n) gcd in Z[i] with a quotient sequence to [39], and a fast quartic symbol to [41]. Theorem 3.18 (p. 969) proves O(μ_{O_D}(n) log n) for all imaginary quadratic maximal orders, including O_{−4} = Z[i]. Nothing in the paper gives or suggests a bottom-up method with this bound.

## 8. Consequences for our documents (not edited)

8.1 Our recursion note, section 2.3, says of this paper "abstract only ... presumably top-down, but that is not checked". It is now checked: top-down throughout. Section 1.4 credits the fast Gaussian gcd to this paper. The primary credit for Z[i] should go to [39] (ANTS-IV 2000), which this paper itself names (p. 943), with this paper as the generalisation to imaginary quadratic orders and to ideal sums.

8.2 Our draft, section 8.2: item 4 (the thesis) can now be given its exact citation from 6.1. The bibliographic data of [W1], [W2], [W3] in the draft agree with [38], [39], [41] here, and [SZ04] pages 411–425 agree with [35]. Collins [11] should be added to the reading list. Nothing in this paper bears on Wikström's attribution of a Gaussian k-ary reduction to [39]; it describes [39] only as the transfer of Schönhage's algorithm.

8.3 Our novelty claims in draft section 8.1 and recursion note section 9 are not affected by this paper. What is still unread is [38], [39] and [41] themselves, and Collins [11].
